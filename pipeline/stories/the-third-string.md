# The Empty Music Room

Deniz stood outside the music room and listened. Inside, someone was playing the violin, slow and clean.

He pushed the door open. His teacher, Mr. Aydın, was cleaning the strings of an old violin with a small cloth.

"You are early," Mr. Aydın said. "Good. Sit down."

Deniz sat in his usual chair and opened his violin case. The wood inside was dark red, with a small crack near the bottom that Mr. Aydın fixed two years ago.

"The winter concert is in six weeks," Mr. Aydın said. "You are going to play the solo this year."

Deniz almost dropped his bow. "Me? Alone? In front of everyone?"

"Yes. You are ready."

"I am not ready," Deniz said quickly. "Ask Sude. She plays better than me."

"Sude plays faster than you," Mr. Aydın said. "That is not the same as better." He picked up a piece of paper from his desk. "This is the music. It is not easy, but you can learn it."

Deniz looked at the black dots on the paper. They moved up and down like little hills.

"Why me?" he asked.

"Because when you play, people stop and listen," Mr. Aydın said. "Sude plays every note correctly. You make the music feel like something. That is rare."

Deniz did not feel special. He felt afraid. Every year, hundreds of parents and students sat in the big hall for the winter concert. He always played in the group, hidden between other violins. Nobody watched him alone.

That evening, Deniz walked home with his violin case on his back. His father worked at the market and came home tired every night. His mother left three years ago and lived in another city now. Deniz did not see her often.

At home, his father was making dinner.

"How was practice?" his father asked.

"Mr. Aydın wants me to play alone at the winter concert," Deniz said. "A solo."

His father stopped cutting the vegetables. "Alone? That is big news."

"I do not want to do it," Deniz said. "I am going to say no tomorrow."

"Why?"

"Because everyone is going to watch only me. If I make a mistake, everyone sees it."

His father put down the knife and sat at the table. "Your mother played the piano, you know. Did I ever tell you that?"

Deniz shook his head. His father did not talk about his mother often.

"She played in front of many people, when she was young. She told me once that she was always afraid before she played. Every single time."

"Then why did she do it?"

"Because after the fear, there was the music," his father said. "She said the fear never really left. She just learned to play with it standing next to her."

Deniz thought about this for a long time that night, lying in bed with his violin case beside him.

# Six Weeks of Practice

The next morning, Deniz did not say no to Mr. Aydın. He picked up his violin instead and asked him to explain the music again.

"Start slow," Mr. Aydın said. "The first line only. We are not in a hurry."

Deniz played the first line badly, three times in a row. On the fourth try, it sounded a little better.

"Again," Mr. Aydın said.

Every day after school, Deniz practiced for two hours. His fingers hurt, and his arm got tired from holding the bow up. Some days, the music sounded terrible, full of small mistakes. Other days, one line came out clean and beautiful, and Deniz felt something warm inside his chest.

His father listened from the kitchen every evening. He did not say much, but sometimes he stood in the doorway of Deniz's room and watched.

"That part is nice," he said one evening. "Play it again."

Deniz played it again, and his father closed his eyes and listened.

At school, Sude found him in the hallway. "I heard you are playing the solo," she said.

"Yes," Deniz said, waiting for her to be angry.

But she smiled. "Good. You deserve it. Your sound is better than mine, even if your fingers are slower."

"You are not angry?"

"I am not angry. I do not like standing alone in front of people. You can have it." She laughed. "Just do not mess it up."

Three weeks before the concert, Deniz played the whole piece for Mr. Aydın without stopping. He made two small mistakes near the end, but he finished it.

"Better," Mr. Aydın said. "Much better. But your hands are still tight when you play the fast part. Relax your fingers there."

"I get nervous," Deniz admitted. "My hands get cold and stiff."

"Then we practice being nervous too," Mr. Aydın said. "Next lesson, I am going to invite two other teachers to sit and watch you play. Just to get you used to eyes on you."

Deniz did not like this idea, but he agreed.

The next lesson, two teachers sat quietly in the back of the room while Deniz played. His hands shook at the start, and he missed a note in the middle. But he kept playing, all the way to the end.

"You stopped when you missed the note before," Mr. Aydın said. "Today you did not stop. That is the important part."

Two weeks before the concert, Deniz's mother called on the phone. His father spoke to her first, then gave the phone to Deniz.

"I heard about your solo," she said. "Your father told me."

"It is not until next week," Deniz said, surprised she called.

"I know. I am going to come," she said. "If that is fine with you."

Deniz did not know what to say. He had not seen his mother play music in years, not since she left. "You are coming? To watch?"

"Yes. I want to hear you play," she said. "I want to hear what you make of the music."

# The Winter Concert

The big hall was full of people. Rows and rows of parents, grandparents, and students filled every seat. Deniz stood behind the stage curtain with his violin, his hands cold even though the room was warm.

"You look sick," Sude said, standing next to him with her own violin.

"I feel sick," Deniz said.

"That is normal," she said. "I feel sick every year, and I only play in the group."

The group played first, five songs together. Deniz stood in his usual place, hidden among the other violins, and his hands slowly warmed up as he played the familiar notes.

Then the group finished, and the other students walked off the stage. Deniz stayed behind, alone, in the middle of the empty stage. The lights felt very bright on his face.

He looked out at the crowd. In the third row, he saw his father, sitting very straight. Next to him sat a woman Deniz almost did not recognize at first. His mother. She came.

Mr. Aydın stood at the side of the stage and gave Deniz a small nod.

Deniz lifted his violin to his shoulder. His hand shook a little as he placed the bow on the strings. For one second, he thought about running off the stage.

Then he thought about his mother's words on the phone: I want to hear what you make of the music.

He started to play.

The first notes came out soft and a little shaky, but they came out. He remembered Mr. Aydın's voice: slow, we are not in a hurry. He let the first line breathe, just like in practice.

By the second line, his hands stopped shaking so much. He remembered the fast part that gave him trouble for weeks, and he relaxed his fingers the way Mr. Aydın taught him. The notes came faster now, but clean.

He made one small mistake near the middle, a wrong note that lasted only half a second. He did not stop. He kept going, just like the day the two teachers watched him.

The music grew louder and faster, then slower again, moving toward the last part. Deniz closed his eyes for the final line, the part his father called nice so many times in the kitchen.

He finished on a long, quiet note, and then it was silent.

For one second, nobody moved.

Then the whole hall started to clap, loud and long. Deniz opened his eyes. His father was standing, and so was his mother, and Sude, and Mr. Aydın, and people he did not even know.

Deniz walked off the stage with his legs shaking, but not from fear this time.

Backstage, his father hugged him first, holding him tightly. "You played it exactly like you did in the kitchen," he said. "Exactly like that."

His mother stood a little behind, and Deniz was not sure what to do. Then she stepped forward and hugged him too.

"You made something of that music," she said quietly. "Something that was yours."

"I was so afraid," Deniz said.

"I know," she said. "I told you, the fear does not leave. It just stands next to you while you play."

Sude came running backstage, still holding her violin. "You did not mess it up," she said, grinning. "One wrong note. That is nothing."

Deniz laughed, the fear finally leaving his chest for the first time in six weeks.

"Next year," Mr. Aydın said, walking up behind them, "you are going to play something harder."

"Can I have some time first?" Deniz asked.

"You have all year," Mr. Aydın said, smiling. "Use it well."
