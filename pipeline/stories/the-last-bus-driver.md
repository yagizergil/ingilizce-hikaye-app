# The Last Seat on the Left

The rain had stopped by the time Emel turned the bus into Demirkapı, but the road still shone under the depot lights. She pulled in beside the fence, opened the doors out of habit, and looked up into the long mirror above her head. The last seat on the left was not empty. A man sat with his head against the glass and his mouth a little open, and he was asleep again.

"Third time this week," Emel said to nobody.

She switched off the engine and walked down the empty bus. Her shoes made a wet sound on the floor. The man was about sixty, perhaps older, in a grey coat that had been a good coat once. On his knees he held a plastic bag, and inside it she could see two oranges and a folded newspaper. His hands were rough but clean, with short nails.

Emel never touched a sleeping passenger. She knocked twice on the metal rail beside his seat, the way she always did, and the man opened his eyes at once, as if he had only been resting them.

"This is the end of the line," she said. "Demirkapı. There is nothing after this stop."

"I know." He stood up, put the bag under his arm, and pressed his coat flat with one hand. "I am sorry. It will not happen again."

He had said those same words on Monday and on Wednesday. Emel followed him to the door and watched him step down into the dark, and then she watched the dark take him completely. There were no houses at Demirkapı, only the bus depot, a closed tyre factory, and the road that climbed the hill and left the city. The wind came off that hill all night long.

"Which way is your house?" she called after him.

He lifted one hand without turning round, and that was the whole answer.

Inside the office, Orhan was writing numbers on a board with a marker that had almost died. He was a heavy man who had driven the same route for twenty years before his back gave up, and he liked rules the way other people liked football.

"You are four minutes late signing in," he said.

"There was water across the road at Söğütlü."

"There is always water across the road at Söğütlü." He put the cap on the marker. "Emel. Rule twelve. The bus enters this yard empty. Not almost empty."

Emel hung her key on the hook. "He gets off. He always gets off."

"Then wake him earlier, before we reach the gate." Orhan turned round and looked at her properly for the first time. "There is an inspector riding the night routes on Friday. If he finds a passenger asleep in your bus at the last stop, it goes in your file, not in the passenger's. And the night route pays you a fifth more than the day route. You told me yourself that you need that money."

She did need it. Her mother's flat had a damp patch on the bedroom wall that grew a little every winter, and Emel was saving for the deposit on a dry one, two streets away, with a balcony and a working boiler. She had counted the money on the kitchen table three nights ago. She needed eleven more weeks of the night bonus, and Friday was in four days.

"He is not a problem," she said. "He is just tired."

"Everybody is tired," Orhan said. "That is why we have a last stop."

Outside, the staff van was waiting with its engine running. Emel got in and looked back up the black hill while the driver turned the van round. Somewhere up there the man in the grey coat was walking, with his two oranges and his folded newspaper, in a coat that was not thick enough for the wind. She had driven that road a thousand times. She knew exactly what was up there and what was not, and there was no house on it for four kilometres.

# Two Oranges

On Thursday he got on at Gültepe, four stops after the station, and Emel watched him in the mirror while she waited for the lights to change. He put his ticket in the machine, said good evening to her in a low voice, and walked to the last seat on the left as if it belonged to him.

Nine people rode the last bus that night. They came off in ones and twos, and by Söğütlü the bus was hers again, hers and his.

"Excuse me," she said at the empty stop, with the doors still shut. "Do you get off at Demirkapı?"

"If that is where the bus stops," he said.

"There is nothing at Demirkapı."

"There is a depot," he said. "You work there."

She laughed, because it was a fair answer, and drove on. When they arrived she woke him with two knocks on the rail, and he stood up and said sorry, and she made a decision while he was walking down the steps.

She signed the bus in, told the night guard at the yard that she wanted some air, and went out through the side gate on foot. The hill road had no lamps. She kept twenty or thirty metres behind him and stayed on the soft ground beside the tarmac, and she was glad she had worn her old boots. It took a quarter of an hour. Then the road turned, and a wall came out of the dark, and above the wall she saw the lit windows of the district hospital.

The man did not go in. He sat down on a low concrete block beside the gate, under the lamp, and opened his newspaper on his knees. The gate was closed. Behind it, a guard's hut showed a small orange light.

Emel walked up to him. He looked at her over the newspaper without any surprise at all, and she understood that he had known about her boots on the soft ground the whole way.

"You cannot sit here all night," she said. "It is going to be two degrees by four o'clock."

"I have sat in worse places."

"Listen to me." She sat down on the end of the block, because standing over him felt wrong. "The city office on Fener Street keeps beds in the winter. They are clean, and nobody asks you questions. My cousin's husband worked there. If you go tomorrow before six, they will write your name down and you will have a bed until March. There is no shame in it. Half the men there work all day."

He folded the newspaper slowly, in half and then in half again, and put it in the plastic bag with the oranges. When he stood up, he was taller than she had thought.

"I have a house," he said. "It has a door and a key, and my wife's coat is still hanging behind that door. Nine years I paid for that house. Do you understand me?"

"Then why are you here?"

"Good night," he said.

He sat down again on the concrete block and opened the newspaper, and he did not look at her once while she walked back down the hill. Her face was hot all the way to the depot, and the guard asked her if she was ill.

The next morning she could not sleep. She lay on her side and looked at the damp patch on her mother's bedroom wall, which she could see through the open door, and she thought about the two oranges. A man with nowhere to sleep does not carry fruit. A man with nowhere to sleep does not keep his nails short. She had been so pleased with herself, with her Fener Street and her clean beds, and she had got the whole thing wrong from the first night.

# Seven O'Clock

On Monday she brought a thermos.

He got on at Gültepe and said good evening, and by the time the bus reached Demirkapı he was asleep with his head against the glass. Emel woke him at the gate, parked the bus in the yard, and then she carried the thermos and two glasses out to the bench beside the fence, where the drivers sat in summer.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Then walk. It is tea, not charity."

He looked at the glasses for a moment and sat down at the other end of the bench. He drank the first glass in three swallows, like a man who had been cold for hours, and then he held the second one with both hands.

"Mahmut," he said. "That is my name."

"Emel."

"I know. It is written above your door." He turned the glass round in his hands. "My wife's name is Nesrin. In August she was making soup, and she put the spoon down and she could not pick it up again. Her right side stopped working, all of it, in one minute. She is in ward four, up there behind that wall."

Emel put her glass down on the bench.

"Ward four takes visitors from seven until eight in the morning, and from six until seven in the evening," Mahmut said. "That is the rule and they keep it. I clean a school in Yenimahalle from four in the afternoon until ten at night. So the evening hour is finished for me. There is only seven in the morning."

"Then take the morning bus."

"The first bus out here arrives at twenty to nine." He said it without any anger, the way a man reads a timetable he has read four hundred times. "A taxi from my street is two hundred and fifty. Her medicine is nine hundred a month, and the school pays me on the fifteenth. So I finish work, I take your bus, and I wait at the gate until seven. Then I sit with her for one hour, and I go home and sleep until three."

Emel worked it out. Ten at night until seven in the morning, five nights a week, on a concrete block beside a locked gate.

"You sleep on my bus," she said, "because it is the only warm place you get."

"Fifty minutes," Mahmut said. "It is the best sleep of my week. I am sorry that it makes trouble for you."

They walked up the hill together, because she could not think of a single reason not to. At the gate the guard came out of his hut with a torch, and he was old, seventy at least, and he walked with one stiff leg. His name was Hüseyin. He had seen Mahmut every night since September, and he had never asked him a question, because in his opinion a man who sits quietly outside a hospital gate has a reason and it is not the guard's business.

By the time Emel got back to the yard it was twenty-five to two. Orhan was standing beside the office door with the clipboard against his chest.

"Forty minutes," he said. "Where were you?"

She could have said the toilet, or a phone call, or the rain. She was too tired to build anything.

"I walked a passenger up to the hospital gate."

Orhan wrote something on the clipboard, and he wrote it slowly, and that was worse than shouting. "This one is a written warning and a day's pay. If it happens again, you go back on the day route in the spring, and you can explain the difference to your mother."

At home she took the tin box down from the shelf and counted the money again, and then she counted the weeks. Twelve now, instead of eleven. Her mother came in with her cardigan over her nightdress and asked what she was doing at the table at four in the morning.

"Waiting for seven o'clock," Emel said.

# The Gate Is Open

The inspector rode on Friday, a young man with a tablet and clean shoes who sat behind the ticket machine and typed after every stop. Emel woke Mahmut at Söğütlü, five minutes before the depot. He stood at the front doors with his bag under his arm all the way in, and when the bus turned into the yard it was empty, exactly as rule twelve required. The inspector wrote nothing about her, and Orhan said nothing either, which from Orhan was a compliment.

But the gate on the hill was still locked, and it was November.

On the ten past ten run into the city, Emel found Sibel, a nurse from the district hospital who lived two streets from her mother and always sat behind the driver. Emel asked her about ward four, and about Nesrin, and about who was allowed inside the building before six.

"Nobody," Sibel said. "Not because we are hard. There was a man in April who walked into the ward at three in the morning looking for his brother, and he pulled a drip out of a woman's arm. So now the door stays shut. Even I ring the bell." Then she thought about it. "The gate hut is a different thing. That is Hüseyin's hut. It is not the hospital."

Emel sat with that for two days.

On Wednesday she went up the hill with Mahmut again and put it in front of both of them, at the gate, with the wind coming down off the ridge.

"Hüseyin, you have a stove in there and a bad knee and eight hours of nothing. The gate needs sweeping, and those two kettles need filling before the night nurses come out at five. Mahmut, you clean a school for a living. Sit in the hut, sweep the gate, fill the kettles at five, and go in to your wife at seven."

Hüseyin laughed once, like a cough. "The kettles are always empty. The girls complain about it every single morning."

Mahmut did not laugh. He put his bag down on the concrete block and looked at Emel with his chin up, and for a moment she thought he would walk away down the hill.

"I am not a poor man to be put in a corner," he said.

"You would be doing his job for him," Emel said. "He should be ashamed, not you. And if you want, ask him what he gets out of it."

Hüseyin leaned on the gate. "Somebody to talk to at four in the morning," he said. "Do you play backgammon?"

Mahmut swept the gate that night. He did it properly, in lines, the way a man does work he is paid for, and then he sat inside on the second chair with his hands round a glass while Hüseyin explained why the hospital director was a fool.

The winter went on. Emel woke Mahmut at Söğütlü every night, five minutes before the end of the line, and he was always standing at the doors when the bus turned in. In January she moved her mother into the flat with the balcony, one month later than she had planned, and the boiler worked.

Then in the middle of February the last seat on the left was empty at Gültepe, and it was empty on Tuesday, and on Wednesday, and all the next week. Emel asked Sibel, who had been on nights and did not know. She thought about the concrete block and the two degrees, and she did not sleep well.

On the first Monday in March, when she brought the bus into the yard at ten to one, a man was sitting on the drivers' bench with a plastic bag on his knees. He stood up when the doors opened.

"She came home on Tuesday," Mahmut said. "She walks to the door and back, twice a day, holding my arm. On Thursdays we take the twenty to nine bus to the clinic together, so you will not see me at night any more." He held out the bag. There were six oranges in it. "Nesrin says you must come for tea on a Sunday. She says I am to keep asking until you come."

Emel came on the third Sunday, and after that most Sundays. She still looks up into the long mirror when the bus turns into Demirkapı, and the last seat on the left is always empty, and she always looks twice.
