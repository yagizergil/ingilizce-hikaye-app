# A Grey Cat in the Cold

Elif opened the shop early, before the sun.

The street was empty and dark. Cold air came through the door. She turned on the lights and started the oven.

A small sound came from outside. It was quiet, like a question.

Elif looked out the window. A grey cat sat by the step. Its fur was wet and thin. It looked at her and did not move.

"Go home," Elif said through the glass.

The cat did not go home. It had no home. Elif knew that. She saw it every winter, near the market, near the bus stop, always alone.

She opened the door a little. Cold wind came in fast.

"You cannot stay here," she said. "My father does not like animals in the shop."

The cat sat down on the step. It looked very tired.

Elif went back to the oven. She took out warm bread and put it on the shelf. Then she looked at the window again. The cat was still there.

She broke off a small piece of bread. She put it outside, near the step, not too close to the door.

The cat did not move at first. Then it walked to the bread, slowly, and ate it.

Her father came in an hour later. He was a big man with a loud voice.

"Why is the door open?" he asked.

"I am airing the shop," Elif said. It was not true, but it was easy to say.

Her father looked outside and saw the cat. His face changed.

"No animals near my shop," he said. "They bring dirt. They bring bad luck."

"It is not doing anything," Elif said. "It is just sitting."

"Today it sits. Tomorrow it comes inside. Then customers do not like it." He clapped his hands at the cat. "Go! Go away!"

The cat ran across the street and stopped under a parked car.

Elif said nothing. She wanted to argue, but her father was already busy with the bread orders.

All day, Elif thought about the cat. She saw it two more times, once near the bin behind the shop, once under the car again. Each time it looked thinner.

That evening, after her father left, Elif stayed a little longer. She took a plastic box from the back room. She put an old towel inside it. She carried it outside and left it under the car, close to the wall, out of the wind.

The cat watched her from a few metres away. It did not come closer while she was there.

Elif walked home in the dark. She looked back once. The cat was moving towards the box.

"Please stay warm," she said quietly, to no one.

At home, she did not stop thinking about it. Her mother noticed.

"You are quiet tonight," her mother said.

"There is a cat by the shop," Elif said. "My father does not want it there."

Her mother put down her cup. "Winters are hard for street animals," she said. "But your father is not a cruel man. He worries about the business, not about the cat."

"The business is more important than a life?"

Her mother did not answer right away. "Sometimes people need a reason," she said finally. "Find a good reason, and your father can change his mind."

Elif looked at her hands. She did not have a reason yet. But she was going to find one.

# The Box Behind the Shop

The next morning, the box was empty.

Elif's heart dropped. She looked around the car, under the step, near the bin. No cat.

Then she heard the small sound again, from behind the shop, near the wall. She walked around and found the cat there, and next to it, three tiny baby cats.

She stood very still. The baby cats were blind, and they made soft, weak sounds.

"Oh," Elif said. "Oh, no."

Now there were four cats, not one. Her father was never going to allow this.

She ran inside and got a bigger box and an old blanket. She moved everything to the wall, out of the wind. The mother cat watched her hands but did not run.

"I am not going to hurt you," Elif said softly. "I promise."

Her father arrived an hour later. He walked around the back to check the bins and stopped when he saw the box.

"What is this?" he said.

"Kittens," Elif said. "Born last night. They cannot move yet. They cannot survive the cold alone."

Her father rubbed his face with his hand. "Elif, we talked about this."

"I know. But look at them." She pointed at the small shapes under the blanket. "They are not hurting the shop. They are behind the wall, not near the door."

"And in one month? Four cats become five customers who complain about fur, about smell."

"Or four cats become the reason people remember us," Elif said. "The cat lady near the bank has a shop, too. People bring her food. People talk about her cats. It brings people to her street."

Her father was quiet for a moment. He looked at the box again.

"One week," he said. "The kittens stay one week, until they are stronger. Then you find them homes. Not here. Homes."

"One week," Elif agreed.

That week, Elif worked fast. Before school, she cleaned the box and gave the mother cat food and water. After school, she sat by the wall and did her homework there, so the cats got used to a person nearby.

Customers began to notice. A woman with a small daughter asked about the kittens on the third day. An old man who bought bread every morning started bringing fish, wrapped in paper, without saying why.

By the fifth day, three different families asked to see the small cats.

"People like a small, warm story," Elif's mother said, smiling. "Especially in winter."

Elif's father did not smile, but he did not clap his hands at the cat anymore, either.

On the sixth day, it began to snow. Elif worried about the mother cat and her babies outside in the cold. She asked her father again.

"One more week," she said. "Please. It is going to snow all night."

Her father looked at the sky. It was heavy and grey.

"One more week," he said. "But you clean that corner every single day."

"I will," Elif said. "I promise."

# A Home for Every Cat

The snow fell for three days.

Elif's father moved the box closer to the back door, under a small roof, out of the wind. He did not say why. He just did it, early one morning, before Elif woke up.

She saw him doing it through the window and said nothing. She only smiled.

By the second week, the small cats walked well. They fell over their own feet and climbed onto each other. Customers stopped at the back of the shop just to watch them play.

"They are good for business," her father admitted one evening. "People buy more bread when they are happy."

"So the cats can stay?" Elif asked.

"The mother cat can stay," he said. "She catches mice, and she is calm. But the babies must go to good homes. That was the plan."

Elif nodded. She had already made a list.

The woman with the small daughter took the first kitten, a black one with white feet. The little girl cried with happiness and held it all the way home.

The old man with the fish took the second kitten, a small orange one. He said his house was too quiet since his wife died, and now it was not going to be so quiet anymore.

The third kitten was harder to place. It was the smallest, and it was afraid of loud noises. Nobody wanted a nervous cat.

Elif brought it inside the shop one cold afternoon, against the rules, and let it sleep in a box near the warm oven. Her father saw it and did not say anything.

A boy from Elif's school came in for bread and saw the kitten sleeping by the oven.

"Is it for sale?" he asked.

"It is not for sale," Elif said. "It needs a quiet home. Do you have a quiet home?"

"My grandmother lives with us," the boy said. "She sits by the window all day. She says the house is too silent."

Elif looked at the sleeping kitten, then at the boy. "Bring your grandmother tomorrow," she said. "Let her meet the cat first."

The grandmother came the next morning, slowly, with a stick. She sat down near the oven and waited. The small kitten woke up, looked at her, and walked straight into her lap.

"Well," the grandmother said. "That is decided, then."

By the end of winter, the mother cat slept behind the shop every night, warm and fed. Elif's father put a small wooden box there, better than the old plastic one, though he never called it a cat house. He called it "storage."

Elif knew the truth. She did not correct him.

Spring came slowly. The snow melted from the streets, and the mother cat began to sit in the sun by the door, not hiding anymore.

One morning, a delivery man stopped and looked at the cat sleeping in the sunlight.

"Nice shop cat," he said to Elif's father.

Her father looked at Elif. Elif looked back and said nothing, waiting.

"She is a good cat," her father said finally. "She keeps the mice away."

It was not everything, but it was enough. Elif smiled and went back to work.
