# Six Yellow Floats

The yellow floats were new, and Emre could count all six of them from the top of the sea wall.

They sat in a neat line beside the grey rock that his family had always called the Kitchen. His father used to say that this rock fed them every winter, and for eleven years it did. Below the yellow floats, lower in the water, three old red ones were still tied to the same iron ring. His father had painted those red ones himself, and the paint had been thin for years.

Deniz came up the steps behind him with bread under her arm. "You have been sitting here since it was dark," she said. "My mother saw you from the kitchen window and sent me out with breakfast."

"Halil has put his floats on our rock."

"Beside your floats, not on them." Deniz sat down on the wall and let her feet hang over the water. "There is a difference, and you know there is."

"After tomorrow there is no difference at all." Emre took the bread and held it without eating any. "The season opens in the morning. Whatever is tied to the Kitchen at sunrise belongs to the boat that tied it, and nobody argues about it for the rest of the year. That is how this town has worked since before my father was born."

Deniz had a way of thinking with her mouth closed, which Emre found annoying, mostly because she was usually right. "So you want to go out there," she said.

"I want to move our floats to the ring on the north side, tie them with new rope, and finish before breakfast. Halil can keep the south side, and that is fair. But if I do nothing, he takes the whole rock, and my mother buys fish in the market like a tourist."

"With what boat?" Deniz asked, and that question was the end of the road, the way a wall is the end of a street.

Their own boat had been sold in February, engine and all, to a man from the next town along the coast. The money went to the hospital in the city, where Emre's father spent three weeks and came home thinner and much quieter. Since then Emre had worked on other people's boats for other people's money. He could tie any knot in the dark, and he had no boat of his own.

"Your uncle's," he said.

Deniz turned her head very slowly. "Salih is in the city until this evening."

"I know. That is exactly why I said it."

"You are asking me to take my uncle's boat without telling him." She pulled her jacket around her, although the morning air was already warm. "He lets me drive it when he is standing beside me. He has never once let me borrow it."

"Two hours," Emre said. "Out, tie the rope, back before the shops open. He will not even notice that the fuel is lower."

Deniz looked out at the yellow floats for a long time, and then she looked at the red ones. She had known Emre's father too, because he had taught her to clean a fish when she was six years old. "Three conditions," she said at last. "I drive, because you drive like an angry man. We touch nothing on that boat except the wheel and the engine. And we are tied up at the port wall before nine, long before my uncle's bus comes near this town."

The port smelled of fuel and old fish, and nobody was awake except one man mending a net under a lamp. Salih's boat, the Yıldız, sat at the end of the line, blue with a white stripe and a wooden seat worn smooth by thirty years of trousers. Deniz reached into the box under the front seat and took out the key. It hung on a short string with a piece of cork tied to the end, so that it would float if it ever fell into the water. The cork had cracked in half last summer, and only a small grey piece was left.

"My uncle promises every year that he will tie a new one on," Deniz said, and pushed the key into the switch.

Emre looked at his phone while she started the engine. There was one weak bar of signal, and even that would die soon. "Past the point there is nothing," Deniz said, turning the wheel. "No signal, no calls, no help. My uncle says the sea out there is older than telephones."

They went out through the harbour mouth as the sun came up behind the hills, and the water turned the colour of weak tea. For the first ten minutes Emre felt better than he had felt since February.

# A Key on a Wooden Seat

The Kitchen was five kilometres out, past the point and three hundred metres south of the little island that everyone called Bird Island. Deniz brought the boat round in a wide, careful circle, exactly the way her uncle did it, and cut the engine when the rock was an arm's length away. The sea was so flat that Emre could see his own face in it.

Deniz pulled the key out of the switch. Her uncle had a rule about that, and the rule had a story behind it, which involved a neighbour's child and a boat that ended up on the beach. She meant to hang the key on its nail beside the wheel, but Emre was already leaning over the side with the new rope in his teeth.

"Hold this," he said. "Hold it, the water is pulling."

She dropped the key on the wooden seat, took the rope with both hands, and leaned back against the weight of it. Emre worked fast and well. He untied his father's three red floats from the crowded south ring, pulled them along the rock, and made them fast to the north ring with a knot his father would have approved of. The old paint came off on his fingers like dust.

Then the cargo boat passed, far out, a grey shape going north towards the city. Nobody noticed it. Its waves arrived four minutes later, low and long and quiet, and they lifted the Yıldız gently three times.

The key slid down the smooth wood of the seat. It hit the floor, rolled once, and went out through the small round hole at the back where the rain water runs away. Emre saw the little grey piece of cork turn over in the sunlight, and then he saw nothing at all.

For a second neither of them spoke.

"Tell me it is on the floor," Deniz said. "Tell me you can see it under the box."

Emre was already pulling his shirt over his head. The water at the Kitchen is nine metres deep, and on a good day, with a full chest of air, he could reach seven. He went down three times. The first time he found weed. The second time he touched sand and lost his direction and came up too fast, with his ears hurting and his heart banging. The third time he saw only the shadow of the boat above him, a black shape on a green ceiling, and he understood that the key was gone.

He hung on the side, breathing hard, while Deniz looked at the switch as if it might change its mind.

"The radio," Emre said. "Try the radio."

She tried it. The radio, like the lights and the horn and every other useful thing on that boat, drank its electricity through the switch, and the switch needed the key. Deniz sat down on the seat where the key had been and put her hands flat on her knees. "My uncle sold his father's watch to buy this engine," she said. "That is not a story. He really did."

Emre climbed in. His hands were shaking, and not from the cold. "The wind is coming," he said.

It came from the south at about seven o'clock, the way it always did in summer, and it pushed them slowly away from the rock and away from Bird Island. Emre threw the anchor over the side. It was a heavy iron hook on fifteen metres of rope, and it should have held them in the shallow water by the Kitchen. The rope went out, went straight, and stayed straight. The bottom had dropped away to thirty metres, and the hook was swinging in the middle of nothing.

Deniz pulled it back in, hand over hand, and dropped it in the front of the boat.

Emre stood up and looked at the island. It was there, green and brown, with the white stone landing on its north side where the summer boats tied up. It was six hundred metres away, and while he watched it, it became six hundred and twenty.

"There is a board on top of the fish box," Deniz said. "And there is a bucket."

# The Board and the Bucket

The board was a metre long and heavier than it looked, and the bucket was blue plastic with a rope handle. Emre knelt on the left side and Deniz knelt on the right, and they began to paddle towards Bird Island in the flat morning light.

It was slow, stupid work. The Yıldız was built for an engine, not for arms, and she pushed a wide wave in front of her and slid sideways whenever they got out of rhythm. Emre counted a hundred strokes and looked up, and the island had not moved. He counted a hundred more. His shoulders burned, the skin on his hands opened at the base of each finger, and the wind pushed steadily against the side of the boat.

After forty minutes they had gone perhaps eighty metres, and the current had carried them a hundred metres north.

"Stop," Deniz said.

"We cannot stop."

"Emre, stop and think for one minute, or we will still be here at midnight." She dropped the bucket and pushed her hair off her wet face. "We are fighting the wind, and the wind always wins. But it turns. Ask any man on that wall. In summer this wind turns at about nine o'clock, and then it blows in towards the land."

Emre looked at the sun and at the island and at the sea between them. She was right, and being right did not make it easy to hear. "So we do nothing for an hour," he said.

"We do not do nothing. We stop moving away."

He understood then, and the understanding felt like cold water. He took the anchor rope, tied one end to the bucket handle with the knot his father had taught him, and threw the bucket over the back of the boat. It filled and sank a little and dragged. The Yıldız swung round slowly until her nose faced the wind, and she steadied, and the island stopped shrinking. It was not much. It was enough.

They sat in the bottom of the boat out of the wind and shared the bread that Deniz's mother had sent, and neither of them said anything about Salih for a while.

"He will be at the wall at six," Deniz said finally. "He will look for his boat, and he will not find it, and he will think somebody has stolen it."

"We will be back long before six."

"With no key." She looked at her hands. "You know what my father will say. He will say that I am the sensible one, and that if the sensible one does this, then what is the point of anyone being sensible."

The wind turned at twenty past nine. It came round through the west and settled, warm and steady, blowing in towards the land, and the sea changed its colour and its sound. Emre hauled the wet bucket in, and they began to paddle again, and this time the boat went where they pointed it. They came into the shelter of Bird Island at ten o'clock and tied up at the white stone landing, and Emre lay on the warm stone like a fish on a table.

Halil's boat came round the point twenty minutes later.

He was a heavy man of fifty with a burnt neck and a cigarette he never lit, and he had been going out to check his floats before the season for thirty years. He cut his engine, drifted in, and looked at them for a long moment without any expression at all.

"That is Salih's boat," he said.

"Yes," Emre said. There was nothing else to say.

"Salih is in the city." Halil tied his own rope to the stone with one hand. "So either he lent it to two children, which he has never done in his life, or you took it. And you took it to move your father's red floats to the north ring, because you have been staring at my yellow ones from that wall for three days."

Emre stood up. The heat in his face had nothing to do with the sun. "The Kitchen is ours. My father worked it for eleven years."

"Your father worked it for nineteen years," Halil said. "I was there for the first eight of them, in the boat next to his." He climbed onto the landing, and he did not look angry, which was somehow worse. "Sit down, Emre. Your mother came to see me in March."

# What the Paint Costs

Emre did not sit down, but he stopped talking, which was the same thing.

"She came to my house on a Tuesday evening with a plate of something, because she is that kind of woman, and she asked me to take the Kitchen for two seasons," Halil said. "She cannot work it alone, and you were on other men's boats every day, and your father cannot stand for four hours. I pay her a share of everything the rock gives me. The first payment bought your winter fuel. The second one paid the last of the hospital money."

The sea slapped quietly at the stone. Emre thought of the sacks of coal that had appeared in the yard in November, and of his mother saying that his father's cousin had sent them.

"She asked me not to tell you until the season opened," Halil went on. "She said you would take it as charity and do something proud and stupid." He looked at the Yıldız, low in the water with a wet bucket and a board lying in the bottom of it. "She knows you better than you know yourself."

"And the red floats?" Emre said. His voice came out thin.

"I promised her I would leave them tied there. Your father can see them from his chair on the balcony when the light is right." Halil shrugged. "It costs me nothing to leave three old floats on a rock."

Emre sat down on the stone after all.

Halil pulled them back with a rope, slowly, all the way to the harbour mouth, and there he untied it and let them drift the last fifty metres on their own. "I will not bring you in," he said. "You will come in by yourselves, and you will tell Salih, and you will tell him before anybody else does."

Salih was standing on the port wall when they arrived. He had come back on the early bus because the meeting in the city finished sooner than he expected, and he had been standing in that same place for two hours. He watched them come in with a board and a bucket. He watched Emre tie the rope. Then he asked one question.

"Where is my key?"

"Nine metres down, beside the Kitchen," Deniz said. "It went out through the water hole. It was my fault, uncle. I put it on the seat."

Salih did not shout, and Emre almost wished that he would. He came down onto the boat, looked at the empty switch, and pushed the switch in and out with his thumb twice. "You went five kilometres out in a borrowed boat," he said quietly. "No radio, no signal, no second key, and nobody in this town knew where you were. If that wind had come up hard, we would be dragging the bay for you tonight, and I would be telling your mothers that my boat killed you."

"I know," Emre said.

"You do not know. You are seventeen." Salih straightened up. "The whole switch has to be changed now, because the key is not something the shop keeps. Mehmet in the back street does that work, and he will want what you earn in eight days."

"I will pay it," Emre said. "All of it. Not her."

"You will both pay it, in a way that suits me." Salih looked out at the water, and something in his shoulders let go a little. "I need a hand this season. Six mornings a week, four o'clock, and for the first month you will not see a coin, because the first month goes to Mehmet. After that we will talk like men who work together. And before any of that, this boat gets her old paint taken off and a new coat on, and you two will do it with your own hands while everybody on this wall watches you do it."

They started that evening. The sun went down behind the hills, the port turned pink and then grey, and Emre knelt on the stones with a piece of rough paper in his hand, rubbing the old blue paint off the side of the Yıldız. Deniz worked from the other end, and every few minutes she said something rude about his straight lines, and he said something rude about her speed.

Far out, past the point, three red floats sat on the dark water beside six yellow ones, exactly where his father could see them.
