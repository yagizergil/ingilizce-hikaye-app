# Two Keys for One Door

The key turned on the second try, and Selin decided to take that as a good sign after eight hours on the night bus. She pushed the door open with her shoulder, because both of her hands were full of bags. The flat smelled of new paint and old cooking oil, and the light in the hall did not work when she pressed the switch. She left everything by the door and walked into the front room to open a window.

A man was asleep on a thin bed under that window.

Selin did not scream, although she wanted to. She stood still and counted the things in the room that did not belong to her: heavy work shoes, a black bag, a dirty plate, and a phone charging in the wall.

"Hey," she said, and then, much louder, "hey!"

The man opened his eyes, looked at her without any surprise at all, and closed them again.

"You're early," he said. "The paint man told me Thursday."

"I am not the paint man, and I live here."

He sat up so fast that the blanket fell onto the floor, and his hair stood up in every direction at once. "No," he said, slowly and carefully, like a person explaining something simple to a child. "I live here, and I have lived here since Wednesday."

They both reached into their bags at the same moment, and they both pulled out the same folded paper.

The two papers were almost the same: flat five, third floor, Gül Street number eleven, one year, six thousand lira a month. Each one also promised a deposit of twelve thousand lira in cash, which is the money you pay before they give you the keys. At the bottom of both pages stood the same name, E. Toprak, written in the same wide and easy hand.

"This is a joke," the man said. "Somebody is playing a joke on me."

"His name was Erhan," Selin said. "He wore a grey jacket, he smiled all the time, and he counted my money twice."

"He counted mine three times."

His name was Murat, he told her, and he cooked at a restaurant near the station. His old building was going to be pulled down in the spring, so the owner had given every family two weeks to find somewhere else. He had carried his things up three floors on Wednesday alone, because his brother was working that day.

Selin explained that she had arrived that morning on the six o'clock bus from Eskişehir. On Monday she was starting a job at a language school, teaching children in the afternoons and adults in the evenings. She had nine hundred lira in her pocket and no pay until the end of the month.

"Then you should go to a hotel," Murat said.

"With nine hundred lira? And have you got somewhere better to go?"

He did not answer that. He stood up, filled a pot with water and put it on the small cooker, as if the argument was already finished. Selin followed him into the kitchen and stood in the door with her arms crossed.

"Call him," she said.

Murat found the number and pressed it, and they both listened to a flat woman's voice explaining that the phone was switched off. He tried twice more with exactly the same result. Then he laid the phone face down on the table very quietly, which frightened Selin more than shouting.

"Twelve thousand," he said. "I send money to my mother every month, and that was everything else I had."

Outside a bus went past, and the windows shook a little. Selin looked back at the room behind her: bare walls, a chair with a broken leg, her bags on the floor, his shoes by the door. Two people had paid for the same empty box.

"Nobody leaves," she said at last. "Not until we find him."

Murat dropped two eggs into the water. "The small room is yours, because I am already in the big one."

"That is not a gift. That is you keeping the big room."

"Yes," he agreed. "It is."

# Room Four, Above the Phone Shop

The address at the top of both papers was Bahar Street 22, Meydan Property, Room 4, and it took them two buses to get there. Selin read her own paper again all the way, as if the words might change if she was patient enough with them.

Number 22 was a narrow building with a phone repair shop on the ground floor. The stairs at the side smelled of dust, and the door of Room 4 was standing open. Inside there was a plastic table, three chairs, and nothing else except a clean square on the wall where a sign had hung.

The young man in the phone shop was not surprised to see them. He said that two men had used the room for about six weeks, and that they had paid for it in cash. At the end of last month they had carried everything out in a friend's car. He had never learnt their names, and he had never asked for them.

At the police station a tired officer wrote everything down in careful handwriting, and then he asked for a photo of the man. They had none. He asked whether either of them had seen a card with the man's name on it, and Murat said no.

"Nobody ever does," the officer said. "You will hear from us, but these things take months, and sometimes they take much longer than that."

They walked home instead of taking the bus, because they did not want to spend the money. Selin counted out loud as they walked. Twelve and twelve made twenty-four thousand lira, which was more than a year of her rent. All of it was sitting in the pocket of a smiling man in a grey jacket.

On the stairs of number eleven they met Mr Vedat, who cleaned the building and knew every family in it. He listened to the story with his brush in his hand, and his face slowly changed while he listened.

"Flat five?" he said. "Flat five belongs to Mrs Nesrin, and her full name is Nesrin Toprak. She went to live with her daughter in Bursa five years ago, after her husband died. She keeps the flat empty because she says she will come back one day. Her sister's son comes now and then to check the water."

Selin sat down on the step. "What does he look like, this son?"

"Young, and he talks a lot. Very kind, or he seems kind." Mr Vedat lifted one shoulder. "Grey jacket, usually."

For a while nobody spoke, and a door closed somewhere below them while a child laughed on the second floor.

"Then my paper is worth nothing," Murat said at last.

"Mine too," Selin said. "He never owned this flat, so he could not rent it to anybody. We have not been fighting each other at all. We have both been robbed by the same man."

It was a strange feeling, and it almost made her lighter. All afternoon Selin had been building small angry speeches against Murat inside her head, and now every single one of them was useless.

That evening they ate rice and beans out of the same pot. There was only one clean plate, and nobody wanted to be the person who used it. Murat sat on the floor with his back against the wall and searched on his phone while he ate.

"Selin," he said, in a completely different voice. "Come here."

The flat was on the internet, in eleven photos, clean and empty, with the same white walls and the same window. In one photo, near the door, there was a pair of heavy work shoes.

"Those are my shoes," Murat said. "He took these pictures this week."

Selin read the words under the photos three times: free now, serious people only, deposit and first month in cash.

"He is going to do it again," she said. "There is going to be a third one."

# The Morning Bus

Mr Vedat had the Bursa number in an old notebook, and he read it out twice while Selin wrote it down. She used the back of her useless paper. She made the call herself, standing at the window, and she got through the whole story before the woman on the other end stopped her.

"I am coming," Mrs Nesrin said. "Tuesday. Do not touch anything."

She arrived on the morning bus with a small brown case and an umbrella that she clearly did not need. She was shorter than Selin, grey-haired and very straight, and she climbed the three floors without stopping once. At the door she looked at Murat's pots, at Selin's bags, and at the chair with the broken leg.

"Who let you in?" she said.

They gave her the two papers, and she read them at the window, holding them at arm's length. Selin watched her face change from anger into something quieter and much worse.

"I never signed this," Mrs Nesrin said. "But I know this handwriting, because he copied it from my letters. That is my sister's son. That is Erhan."

She sat down on the good chair. Murat could not think of anything else to do, so he made tea and put a bowl of yesterday's soup in front of her. After a minute she had eaten all of it.

"I have been sending that boy money for two years," she said. "His mother is ill. He tells me he works at a garage, and perhaps he really does."

"He has twenty-four thousand lira of ours," Selin said.

"I have no money to give you, and it is not my debt." The old woman put down her spoon. "And I will not go to the police. My sister has one son, and she is in hospital half of every month. Whatever else happens, I will not do that to her."

"Then he keeps it," Murat said. "He keeps it, and he does the same thing again next week."

Selin put her phone on the table with the photos on the screen. She turned it so that Mrs Nesrin could see the shoes by the door.

"Your flat is on the internet, and he is still selling it," she said. "I wrote to him last night from a friend's phone, and I told him my name was Aylin. I said that I needed a flat quickly. He answered in four minutes, and he is showing this room on Saturday at eleven o'clock."

Mrs Nesrin looked at the screen for a long time.

"Saturday," she said.

"He will not come if he sees us," Murat said. "But he will come if the flat is empty and quiet. He will come if a woman called Aylin is waiting at the door with a bag of cash."

"You are asking me to sit here and wait for my sister's son."

"I am asking you to sit in your own home," Selin said. "That is all."

The old woman turned the cup round and round in her hands, and outside the light moved slowly across the wall.

"No police," she said at last. "And any money he gives comes to me first, and then I give it to you. I want that clear."

"Yes," Selin said quickly, before Murat could say anything at all.

"And on Saturday you will clean this kitchen, because I do not want to look at it." Mrs Nesrin stood up and shook out her coat. "One more thing. This flat is mine, and when this is finished I will rent it in the right way. A real paper, with one name on it. One name. You should both understand that now."

Selin and Murat carefully did not look at each other.

# The Third Tenant

By eleven o'clock on Saturday the flat looked like a place where nobody lived. The bags were hidden in the small room, the pots were in a box under the sink, and the floor was still wet in the corners. Mrs Nesrin sat in the good chair by the window with her tea. Selin and Murat stood behind the kitchen door, which they had left open two fingers wide.

The knock came at ten past eleven, and it was three quick, happy knocks.

Selin heard the door open, and then she heard his voice, warm and fast, and she knew it at once. He was not alone: a young man and a young woman came in behind him. The woman held a thick brown envelope with both hands, the way you hold something that is too important to put in a bag.

"New paint, as you can see," Erhan was saying. "Quiet building, good neighbours, and the water is strong on this floor. Now, if you want it, I have to be honest with you, there is another family—"

He stopped in the middle of his sentence, because he had seen the chair by the window.

"Sit down, Erhan," Mrs Nesrin said.

The keys fell out of his hand and hit the floor with a small bright sound. Selin pushed the kitchen door open and came out with Murat close behind her, and the young couple turned round and stared.

"Do not give him anything," Selin said to the woman with the money. "This flat is not his, and we both signed papers for it. Mine was in the spring and his was in the autumn, and both papers were worth nothing at all."

The young man took the envelope out of his wife's hands very quickly. They left without another word, and their steps went down the stairs at a run.

Erhan tried the easy voice first, and then he tried his hands, holding them wide open as if it was a small mistake between friends. He said that he had always meant to pay it back, and that he owed money at the garage. The men there were not patient people, he said. In another month everything would be fine again.

"How much is left?" Mrs Nesrin asked.

"Seven thousand, or maybe a little less."

"Then you will give me the seven thousand today. And you will bring me four thousand on the first day of every month until the twenty-four is finished." She was already writing on a sheet of paper, in the same wide handwriting that Selin had seen twice before. "You will sign this, and then the girl will take a picture of it with her telephone."

"Aunt—"

"If you miss one month, I will go to the police." Mrs Nesrin did not look up from the page. "And before I go to the police, I will sit down on your mother's bed and tell her exactly what you have done."

He signed the page, and Selin took a picture of it, and then a second one, because her hands were shaking a little.

When he had gone, the flat was very quiet. Mrs Nesrin folded the money and put it in the pocket of her coat. Then she counted out three and a half thousand for each of them and held it up in the air.

"This is not yours yet," she said. "This is your deposit. Sit down."

She wrote the new rent paper that afternoon at the kitchen table, in the same careful hand. It said one year, six thousand a month, flat five. At the bottom there were two names instead of one.

"I will come every three months," she said at the door. "If you kill each other, I keep the deposit."

That evening Murat found a roll of tape in the bottom of his black bag. He laid a long silver line down the middle of the front room floor, from the door to the window. Selin watched him with her arms crossed and said nothing at all.

On Monday she left for work at seven, and there was bread and eggs already on the table, with a note beside the plate.

Your half of the kitchen is the left one.
