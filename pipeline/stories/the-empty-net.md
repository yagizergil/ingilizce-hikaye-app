# The Wrong Save

Kaan stood in front of the goal and watched the ball fly past him.

It hit the net and fell to the grass. The other team ran and shouted. Kaan picked up the ball slowly. His hands were cold and wet.

"Bad luck," his coach shouted from the side. "Next time, move left!"

Kaan nodded and kicked the ball back to the field. This was the third goal today. His team, the Blue Lions, was losing four to zero. It was only the first half.

His little sister Aylin sat with their mother on the wooden seats. She held a small blue flag. She waved it every time Kaan touched the ball, even when he lost it.

At half time, the players sat on the grass and drank water. Kaan looked at his shoes. They were old and one had a small hole.

"You are not watching the ball," said Deniz, the team captain. "You watch the other players. Watch the ball, Kaan."

"I try," Kaan said. "But the ball moves so fast."

"Everyone thinks that," Deniz said. "You must learn to guess where it goes next."

Kaan did not answer. He was thinking about his father. His father used to play in this same field, many years ago. People still talked about him. "Your father saved everything," an old man told Kaan once. "Nothing passed him."

Kaan's father did not come to matches anymore. He worked far away, in another city, and he came home only two times a year. Kaan wanted to be a good goalkeeper before his father came home next month. He wanted his father to see one good match.

The second half started. The sky was grey and a little wind moved the flags. The other team attacked again. A tall boy ran with the ball toward Kaan.

Kaan watched his eyes, not his feet. The boy looked to the left, so Kaan moved right.

The ball flew to the left.

It hit the net again. Five to zero.

Kaan sat down on the grass for a second. He felt his face get hot. He heard Aylin's flag stop moving.

"Get up," Deniz called. "The match is not over."

Kaan stood up. His legs felt heavy, but he walked back to his place in front of the goal. He looked at the sky. The rain was starting now, small drops on his arms.

He thought about his father's old stories. His father always said the same thing: "A goalkeeper only needs to save the last one. Not the first, not the middle. The last one."

Kaan did not know that was true. But he decided to believe it, at least for the rest of this match.

# Rain in the Second Half

The rain came harder now. The grass turned dark and slippery. Players slipped and fell, and the ball moved strangely on the wet ground.

The Blue Lions were still losing five to zero, but something changed in Kaan. He stopped thinking about the goals behind him. He started watching only the next ball.

A short player from the other team ran fast down the side. He passed to a friend near the goal. The friend kicked hard.

Kaan jumped to the right and caught the ball with both hands. He fell on the wet grass, but he held on.

"Yes!" Deniz shouted. "That is how you do it!"

Kaan stood up, wet from head to foot. He smiled for the first time that day. He threw the ball far down the field to his own team.

For the next ten minutes, the other team attacked three more times. Kaan saved every ball. One save hurt his arm. One save made him fall hard on his back. But the ball did not go in the net again.

His mother stood up and clapped. Aylin waved her flag so fast it almost broke.

The match ended five to zero. The Blue Lions lost, but Kaan walked off the field with his head up. Deniz put a hand on his shoulder.

"You saved four balls in the rain," Deniz said. "Nobody remembers the first goals. They remember the saves."

"We still lost," Kaan said.

"Yes. We lost badly." Deniz laughed. "But you played like your father today. I saw it."

Kaan looked at him. "You knew my father?"

"Everyone in this town knew your father," Deniz said. "He lost matches too, you know. Many matches. He just never stopped trying."

That night, Kaan called his father on the phone. He told him about the match, about the five goals, about the rain and the saves at the end.

His father was quiet for a moment. "Five goals," he said. "That sounds like a hard day."

"It was," Kaan said. "But I stopped the last four."

"Then you had a good day," his father said. "I am proud of you, Kaan. I want to see your next match."

# The Next Sunday

The next Sunday, Kaan's father came home two weeks early. He said his work finished sooner than he expected. Kaan did not fully believe this, but he did not ask questions. He was too happy.

The whole family walked to the field together. Aylin carried a new flag, bigger than the old one. Kaan's father carried a small bag with old football shoes inside — his own shoes, from many years ago.

"I am not going to play," he told Kaan. "My legs are too old now. But I wanted to bring these. For luck."

Kaan held the old shoes. They were heavy and the leather was hard. "Thank you," he said.

The match started under a clear sky. The other team was strong again, and they scored one goal in the first ten minutes. But this time, Kaan did not sit down. He did not think about the score. He watched every player's eyes.

In the second half, a fast attack came toward him. Two players ran together, and Kaan did not know which one had the ball. He watched their feet instead, just for a second.

The ball went to the player on the left. Kaan threw himself sideways and caught it in the air.

The crowd shouted. His father stood up from his seat, both hands in the air.

The match ended one to zero. The Blue Lions still lost, but only by one goal. Deniz ran over and lifted Kaan's arm like a winner.

"One goal," Deniz said. "One goal in the whole match! That is the best we played all year."

After the match, Kaan's father walked onto the wet grass. He looked at his son for a long moment.

"You are better than I was," he said quietly. "Much better."

"We still did not win," Kaan said.

"No," his father agreed. "But you did not stop. That is the important part. Winning comes later, when you are ready."

Kaan looked down at the old shoes in his bag. He was going to keep them, he decided, even when they did not fit him anymore. He put his arm around his father, and they walked home together in the last light of the evening.
