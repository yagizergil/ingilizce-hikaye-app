# The Night Box

The rain had stopped, but the metal box beside the shop door was still wet when Selin opened it. Inside there were three books, and only one of them was hers. She knew her own books the way a cook knows her own knives. Two of them were cheap novels that somebody had used to fill a shelf. The third was a thin book of poems with a green cover, and it carried her card inside the front.

The lending shelf had been her own idea, and it worked better than she expected. For two lira a week her regular customers could take a book home, and most of them brought it back. She wrote every name in a school notebook, because the street was small and people always came back for more. Nobody had ever put a photograph inside a book before.

She carried the books in, turned the sign on the door to OPEN, and made tea. Then she sat behind the counter and opened the poems to check the pages for water. The photograph fell out and landed face down beside her cup.

She turned it over with one finger, because old paper breaks if you are careless with it. It showed a building of two floors with a wide door and an iron balcony above the door. One bar of the balcony was missing on the left. A sign ran across the front, and the letters were still large enough to read: BREAD. A young woman stood in the doorway with a baby against her shoulder. She wore a light dress and no shoes, and she was laughing at whoever held the camera.

Selin looked at the balcony for a long time, then she stood up and walked out into the street. She stepped back off the edge of the road until a car horn made her jump. Above her own sign, the same iron balcony leaned over the street, and the bar was missing on the left. The wide door was hers, the window on the right was hers, and the crack in the top step ran in the same broken line. Her building was ninety years old, and she had bought it six years ago with the money her uncle left her and a loan she was still paying.

Back inside, she held the photograph under the lamp and studied the writing on the back. Time had eaten most of the pencil, but the month and the year were still clear: April 1989. Under the date there was the first letter of a name, and then nothing at all. Selin was born in April 1989. Her mother had always said that the birth happened in the city, in a hospital near a cousin's flat.

Fikret cut hair in the shop next door, and he had done it for forty-one years. He liked to say that he had cut the hair of three generations on that street. He put on his glasses, held the photograph away from his face, and made a small sound in his throat.

"Bread," he said. "Of course. Rasim's father built that oven himself, and the whole street bought from him."

"Who is the woman in the door?" Selin asked.

"A girl who worked there. There were always girls working there, and none of them stayed very long."

"Somebody put this in my night box," she said. "Did you see anyone at the box last night?"

Fikret cleaned his glasses on his shirt while he thought about it. "A small woman in a grey coat," he said. "She stood in front of your door for about ten minutes in the rain."

"Did she say anything to you?"

"Not a word. I was closing up, and her face was wet, and I did not want to ask a stranger why she was crying."

# The Name in the Notebook

The notebook lived under the counter, between the cash box and a jar of pens. Selin found the green poems on the third page from the back, in her own handwriting. Sevim K., the twelfth of March, and next to the name a short line that meant the book was still out. Six weeks was a long time for one thin book of poems. There was no address beside the name, because she had never asked anybody for an address.

She tried to remember the woman, and the memory came slowly, like a name at the end of a long day. A small customer in a grey coat had stood in front of the poetry shelf for half an hour. She had asked nothing about the books, but she had asked two questions about Selin. She wanted to know where Selin was from, and what her mother's name was. At the time it had felt like ordinary talk, and Selin had answered without thinking.

That evening she put the photograph on her kitchen table next to her tea and built a story out of it. Her mother's family came from this town, and her mother's older sister had died at twenty-two. There was one picture of Bahar at home in a frame, and she also had a wide face and a big laugh. So the girl in the doorway was Bahar, who had worked in the bread shop, and the baby was a cousin Selin had never met. The best part of the story was the chance in it: thirty-five years later, knowing nothing, Selin had bought the same door. She went to bed pleased with the world.

Her mother answered on the fourth ring, with the television loud behind her.

"Do we know a Sevim?" Selin asked. "An old woman, small, grey coat, maybe eighty."

"Half the country is called Sevim," her mother said. "Why?"

"Somebody left a photograph in one of my books. It is my building, but a long time ago, when it was still a bread shop. There is a girl in the doorway with a baby, and I am almost sure it is Aunt Bahar."

The television went quiet, which meant that her mother had picked up the remote control.

"Throw it away," her mother said.

"Why? It is a photograph of my own house."

"It is a photograph of nothing. Old paper out of a dead man's boxes." Her voice had gone flat and careful, like a person carrying full glasses across a room. "Sell that shop, Selin. You could sell books anywhere, in a clean place with a lift."

"Mother, look at the laugh. It is Bahar."

"I have to go. The water is boiling."

It was ten o'clock at night, and her mother never cooked anything after nine. Selin sat with the dead phone in her hand and looked at the photograph again.

For three days she asked her way along the street, and everybody had half an answer for her. The tea man knew a Sevim who had died two winters ago, and the fruit seller knew two more, both under thirty. Then Fikret's oldest customer sat down in the chair and gave her the rest of it.

"Sevim from the bread shop," the old man said. "She worked there with the other girls, and she stayed longer than any of them."

"Do you know where she lives?"

"Up the hill with her son. Blue gate, above the school, next to the old fountain." He waved a hand at the ceiling. "That was true in the spring, anyway."

Selin closed the shop at four o'clock, which she never did, and started up the hill.

# The Blue Gate

The hill was steeper than it looked from the shop, and the school children came down it in a shouting river. The blue gate had lost most of its blue, and the fountain beside it had lost its water years ago. A boy on a bicycle told her to knock hard, because the old woman kept her television loud.

Sevim opened the door with a cloth in her hand and looked at Selin for a long moment. Then she stepped back and held the door wide, and she did not ask who her visitor was.

"You have her hands," she said. "Come in. The tea is old, but it is hot."

Three suitcases stood open in the middle of the room, and a grey coat hung on a hook by the door. The walls were bare except for the pale squares where pictures had hung for many years. Selin sat down and put the photograph on the table between them.

"You left this in my box," she said.

"Yes."

"Why did you not simply hand it to me in the shop?"

"Because I stood in front of your shelves for half an hour in March, and I could not do it." Sevim lowered herself into the chair with one hand on the table. "Then my daughter bought me a bus ticket for Friday, and I decided that the photograph should stay in this town."

"Who is the woman in the doorway?"

Sevim did not look down at the picture, because she knew it well enough already. "Nermin," she said.

The name went through Selin like cold water from a tap. "That is my mother's name."

"That is your mother," Sevim said. "And the baby on her shoulder is you."

Sevim had taken the photograph herself, with the camera that Rasim kept in the shop for holidays. Nermin was laughing because Sevim had never held a camera before and was giving orders like a general. Nermin had come down from a village at seventeen to work in the bread shop, and she slept in the small room above it. Ahmet was the baker's younger son, and everybody on the street knew about the two of them before the summer came. They were going to marry in September, and Rasim's father had already bought the rings.

"In July a wall fell on Ahmet at a building site outside the town," Sevim said. "He died in the back of a truck on the road to the hospital, and he was twenty-three years old."

Nermin was expecting a baby by then, and she stayed in the room above the shop through the autumn and the winter. Her own father would not come to the town, and he would not write. In the spring her aunt arrived on the morning bus, packed two bags, and took her away, and nobody on the street ever saw her again.

"Where was I born?" Selin asked, and her voice came out very quiet.

"Upstairs. In that little room over the front door, in the middle of the night, in April." Sevim smiled at the table. "Fikret's wife boiled the water. Rasim ran down the hill for the nurse and lost a shoe."

Selin thought about the boxes of old newspapers she kept up there, and the floor that complained under her feet, and the window she never cleaned.

"So it was not chance," she said. "I bought that building because it was cheap and the roof was good."

"I know," Sevim said. "Fikret told me the new owner's name two years ago, and I had to sit down on the step of the school. Ahmet chose that name in the summer, before anybody knew whether you were a girl or a boy."

"Then why did you wait two years?"

"I wanted to see whether you knew, and you did not know, and that is a hard thing to break." She pushed the photograph across the table with two fingers. "I am eighty-one, and on Friday I go to another city. Some things should not travel in a suitcase."

"Why did my mother lie to me?"

"Ask her. I only know my part of it," Sevim said. "But your father's name was Ahmet, and you should have that, whatever else she tells you."

# What the Photograph Was For

The bus to her mother's town took two hours, and Selin held her bag on her knees the whole way. She had not called first, because a telephone gives people too much time to prepare.

Her mother opened the door in her house clothes, with flour on her hands, and her face changed when she saw the bag. They drank tea on the small balcony among the plants, and they talked about the price of onions for eleven minutes. Then Selin took the photograph out of her bag and laid it on the table beside the sugar.

Her mother did not touch it. She looked at it for a long time, and then she looked out at the street below, where two boys were kicking a ball against a wall.

"Sevim gave you this," she said at last.

"She left it in my night box in the rain. She is going to her daughter on Friday."

"Sevim always did things in the most difficult way possible."

"You slept in my storeroom," Selin said. "I was born in my own storeroom, and you have known that for six years, and you told me to sell the building."

Her mother turned the photograph over, saw the pencil, and turned it back. Her hands were steady, but she kept them flat on the table. She said that she had been eighteen when she came back to her family with a baby and no husband. Her father would not open the door to her. Her aunt in the city took them both in, on one condition: there had to be a story, and everybody had to tell the same one. So there was a husband who died in a traffic accident, and a wedding in a city nobody visited, and a birth in a hospital near the cousin's flat.

"After that the lie needed another lie every year to hold it up," she said. "By the time you were ten it was a house, and we were living in it, and I could not pull the walls down on your head."

"I asked you about my father a hundred times."

"And every time I gave you a dead man with no face, because a man with no face asks no questions." Her mother's mouth went hard. "I was afraid of your face, Selin. I was afraid you would look at me the way my father looked at me."

Selin remembered the day, six years earlier, when her mother came to see the shop. She had stood in the street for a while without going in. Then she had walked through the two rooms, said that the upstairs was too small for anything useful, and left for the bus station two hours early.

"Say his name," Selin said. "You have never said it in front of me."

Her mother closed her eyes. "Ahmet," she said. "He whistled badly and he could not sit still. He chose your name in the middle of July, and he told the whole street about it before he told me."

They sat on the balcony until the boys below got bored of their ball and went home. Before Selin left she wrote Sevim's daughter's phone number on the corner of a newspaper and pushed it across the table, and her mother folded it and put it in her pocket without a word.

Her mother came to the town three weeks later, on the seven o'clock bus, with a bag of things she had cooked for the journey. They climbed the narrow stairs together and stood in the storeroom while the traffic started up in the street below. Selin had cleared the newspapers out and washed the window. Her mother put one hand flat on the glass, in the light, and stayed like that for a long moment.

The photograph hangs now in a cheap wooden frame behind the counter, next to the school notebook and the jar of pens. Customers ask about it, because the balcony in the picture is the balcony over their heads. Selin tells them that the woman in the doorway is her mother, and that the baby is her, and that the shop used to sell bread. The green book of poems sits on her own shelf at home, and that one is not for lending.
