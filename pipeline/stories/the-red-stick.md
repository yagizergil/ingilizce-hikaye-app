# Three Runners and a Hole in the Ground

The rain stopped at four o'clock, so they ran.

Melek stood at the start line with the red stick in her hand. It was made of wood and somebody painted it a long time ago. The paint came off on her fingers when her hands were hot.

"Ready?" she shouted.

Ada waved from the far side of the field. Sinem waved too. Burcu, at the last corner, did not wave, because Burcu never waved.

They were four girls and one race. Next Saturday, four schools, one field, one cup. Each team runs four times around the field. One girl runs, and then she gives the stick to the next girl, and then that girl runs. The stick must never fall. That is the only real rule.

Melek ran. Her shoes hit the wet ground and the water went up her legs. She gave the stick to Sinem and Sinem gave it to Ada, and Ada was fast, Ada was always fast. She ran like a person with a bus to catch.

Then Ada's foot went into a hole near the tree.

The sound was not loud. It was small and wrong, and Melek heard it from thirty metres away.

Ada sat down on the wet grass and held her foot with both hands. The red stick lay in the mud beside her.

"It is fine," Ada said. "It is fine, it is fine."

"Do not stand up," Melek said.

"I said it is fine."

Ada stood up. Then she sat down again very fast.

The school nurse looked at the foot for ten minutes. She put ice on it and she wrote something on a small card.

"No running," she said.

"Saturday," Ada said. "Only Saturday. After that, no running."

"No running on Saturday," the nurse said. "No running next week. Maybe you can run in three weeks."

Ada looked at the wall. Melek looked at Ada's foot. It was fat and red and it did not look like a foot anymore.

Outside, in the corridor, Sinem was already talking.

"So we do not go," Sinem said. "We tell Mr. Onur. It is not our fault."

"We go," Melek said.

"With three people? The race is for four."

"Then we find a fourth."

Burcu leaned on the wall with her arms crossed. "Who?" she said. "Everybody is in something. The others are slow."

That was the problem, and all three of them knew it.

Their school was small. There were twenty-eight girls in their year, and most of them did not run. Two girls played volleyball on Saturdays. One girl worked in her father's shop. Some girls were fast for ten metres and then they stopped and laughed.

There was one girl who ran. There was only one.

"No," Sinem said.

"I did not say anything," Melek said.

"You are thinking it. Do not think it."

"She is faster than me," Melek said. "She is faster than all of us. Ada is fast, but Zehra is faster."

Burcu made a small noise. "Zehra left the team," she said. "In October. Because of you."

The corridor was very quiet. Somebody far away closed a door.

"I know," Melek said.

In October, Melek said a stupid thing. She said it in front of nine people in the changing room. She said it loudly. She was angry and tired, and their team lost by two seconds. She said Zehra was the reason.

It was not true. Melek knew it was not true on the same evening. But she did not say that part loudly. She did not say it at all.

Zehra put her bag on her shoulder and walked out and she did not come back to the team.

"Talk to Mr. Onur," Sinem said. "He can find somebody."

"There is nobody," Melek said.

She walked home alone. The streets were wet and the shops were putting on their lights. Outside the bread shop on the corner, a girl was carrying a big grey box of bread from a van to the door.

Melek stopped on the other side of the road.

Zehra put the box down. She saw Melek. She picked the box up again and she carried it inside, and the shop door closed behind her.

# The Bread Shop

Melek came back on Tuesday. This time she crossed the road.

The shop was hot and it smelled of bread and old wood. Zehra's grandmother sat behind the desk with a small radio.

"Zehra is in the back," the old woman said. She did not ask who Melek was.

Zehra was putting bread on a shelf. She wore a green apron and there was flour on her arms up to the elbow.

"We need a fourth runner," Melek said.

Zehra did not turn around. "No."

"Ada hurt her foot. It is Saturday. The race is on Saturday."

"I know when the race is." Zehra pushed the last bread onto the shelf. "I am not in the team."

"You can come back."

"I do not want to come back."

Melek stood in the middle of the shop with her hands in her pockets. Somebody came in and bought two loaves and went out again. The bell over the door rang twice.

"I said something bad about you," Melek said.

"Yes."

"It was not true."

"I know it was not true," Zehra said. "I was there. I know how fast I ran that day."

She took the empty box and put it under the table. Then she stood up straight and looked at Melek for the first time.

"You said it in front of everybody," Zehra said. "And then for four months you said nothing. Not one word. In the corridor you look at your phone when I walk past."

Melek opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

"Now Ada falls in a hole," Zehra said, "and here you are, in my grandmother's shop."

"Yes," Melek said. "That is true."

Zehra looked at her. It was a hard look.

"At least you do not lie," she said.

Outside, a van stopped and the driver hit the horn twice. Zehra went to the door and shouted something at him, and he waved and drove away.

"I work until seven every day," Zehra said. "Every day. My grandmother is seventy-nine. My mother is in Ankara until March. I carry bread. That is my afternoon."

"Then we run after seven."

"It is dark after seven."

"There are lights on the small field behind the sports hall," Melek said. "Two of them work."

Zehra said nothing for a long time. She picked up a cloth and cleaned the same clean place on the table three times.

"The stick," she said at last. "You are all terrible with the stick."

"We are not terrible."

"Ada drops it. You give it too late. Sinem looks back over her shoulder and slows down." Zehra put the cloth down. "Three seconds. You lose three seconds every race with that stick, and you shout at each other about speed."

Melek stood there and thought about October. About losing by two seconds. About the stick falling out of Zehra's hand in the corner, because Melek gave it too late, and too high.

"Show us," Melek said.

They ran that night at half past seven, under two working lights and one dead one.

Zehra did not run first. She stood on the grass and she made them pass the stick, again and again, without running fast at all. Sinem was angry after ten minutes. Burcu said nothing, but Burcu never said anything.

"Do not look back," Zehra said. "Put your hand behind you and keep it there. Flat. Like this. Count three steps and go."

"And a bad pass?" Sinem said.

"It is going to be there."

They practised on Tuesday, on Wednesday and on Thursday. On Thursday Ada came and sat on a chair by the line. Her foot was up on a box. She shouted at all of them, and it was almost like before.

On Friday it rained again. Nobody said anything about the hole near the tree, but everybody ran around it.

# Four Seconds

Saturday was cold and bright. The big field belonged to another school and the grass there was short and green and flat.

There were four teams. The girls from Yeşilköy School wore new red shirts and they all had the same white shoes. They practised their passes near the fence, fast and quiet, and Sinem watched them and went very still.

"They are good," Sinem said.

"We are good," Melek said.

"They have shoes."

"Shoes do not run."

Ada sat on the grass with her foot in a bandage and the whole team's bags around her, like a small angry queen.

Mr. Onur came and looked at his paper. "Order?" he said.

"Sinem, Burcu, me, Zehra," Melek said.

Zehra looked up. "I go last?"

"You go last."

The four teams stood at the line. Somebody blew a whistle and the race started.

Sinem ran well. She came around the corner in third place and she put her hand out and she did not look back. Burcu took the stick and ran like a machine. She kept the same speed all the way. On the long side she passed the girl from the third school.

Second place. The red shirts were in front, eight metres in front.

Then it was Melek's turn.

She felt Burcu's hand hit her hand and she went. The cold air cut her throat. The eight metres became seven, then six. She did not close it more. The red shirt in front of her was strong and she ran clean and she did not get tired.

At the last corner Melek's legs were full of sand. She saw Zehra at the line with her back to her, hand out, flat and still, waiting.

Do not look back. Count three steps and go.

Zehra went. Melek put the stick into her hand and it was there, exactly there, and Zehra's fingers closed on it and she was gone.

The red shirt girl in front of her looked back. She looked back for half a second, at the wrong moment, and her own hand moved.

That was the three seconds.

Zehra came out of the corner two metres behind, and then one metre behind. On the long side she was next to the other girl. Their shoes hit the ground together. The two schools shouted from the fence. Ada was standing up on her one good foot and Mr. Onur was holding her arm.

Zehra won by four seconds.

She stopped after the line and put her hands on her knees and she did not fall down. Melek got there first, then Burcu, then Sinem, and all of them shouted at the same time and none of them said anything real.

They gave the cup to Zehra because Zehra ran last. It was a small silver cup and the name of another school was still on the bottom of it.

She held it for about ten seconds. Then she pushed it into Melek's hands.

"Put it in the school office," Zehra said. "I am not in the team."

"Zehra—"

"I am not in the team yet," Zehra said. She picked up her bag. "Ask me again on Monday. In the corridor. In front of everybody."

She walked to the bus with her grandmother's flour still on her sleeve.

On Monday morning Melek waited by the big window in the corridor. The corridor was full and loud and everybody was late for the first lesson.

Zehra came in through the main door with her coat wet from the rain.

Melek walked into the middle of the corridor, in front of all of them, and she said it loudly.
