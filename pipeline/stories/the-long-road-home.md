# Smoke from the Front

The car made a strange sound, and then it stopped.

Emine's father pulled it to the side of the road. Smoke came out from under the front of the car, thin and grey.

"Dad?" Emine said from the back seat.

"It's fine," her father said, but his face did not look fine. He got out and opened the front of the car. More smoke came out.

Emine's little brother Kaan woke up and looked around. "Are we home?"

"Not yet," Emine said.

They were still two hours from home, on a long road between the mountains. There were no houses here, only fields and trees on both sides. The sun was going down, and the sky was turning orange and then grey.

Their father came back and sat in his seat, but he did not start the car. "The engine is too hot," he said. "We must wait for it to cool down."

"How long?" Emine asked.

"Maybe an hour."

Kaan started to cry a little. "I'm hungry."

Their mother turned around from the front seat. "We have some bread and cheese in the bag. Let's eat now, while we wait."

They sat by the side of the road and ate bread and cheese from their bag. Cars passed sometimes, fast, their lights bright in the growing dark. Nobody stopped.

After an hour, their father tried the car again. It started, but after five minutes, the same smoke came back, thicker this time.

He stopped the car again, harder this time. "This is not good," he said. "The car needs real help. A person who knows engines."

"There's nothing here," their mother said. "No shops, no houses."

Emine looked down the road. Far away, she saw one small light. "There! Is that a house?"

Her father looked where she pointed. "Maybe. It's far, but maybe."

"I can walk there," Emine said. "I'm the oldest. I can ask for help."

Her mother did not like this idea at first. "It's dark, and you are twelve."

"I have a light on my phone," Emine said. "And you must stay with Kaan. He's too tired to walk."

Her father thought for a moment. "I'll go with her. You stay here with Kaan and lock the doors."

Emine and her father started to walk towards the small light in the distance.

# The House with the Light

The walk was longer than it looked.

Emine held her father's hand, even though she usually thought she was too old for that. The road was dark, and small stones moved under her shoes. Her phone light showed only a small circle in front of them.

"What if nobody is home?" Emine asked.

"Then we walk to the next light," her father said. "We keep walking until someone can help us."

After twenty minutes, they reached the house. It was small, made of stone, with one window lit up. A dog started to bark before they even knocked on the door.

An older man opened the door. He had grey hair and strong hands, like someone who worked outside all his life.

"Yes?" he said, looking at them with surprise.

"I'm sorry to bother you," Emine's father said. "Our car broke down on the road. There's smoke coming from the engine. My wife and son are waiting there, in the dark."

The man looked past them, down the road. "How far?"

"Maybe two kilometers."

The man thought for a second, then nodded. "I have a truck. And I know a little about engines — not much, but enough to look." He turned and called into the house. "Fatma! Get some blankets and the big flashlight!"

A woman appeared behind him, already moving fast. She grabbed two thick blankets from a chair and a large flashlight from a shelf.

"Get in," the man said, pointing at an old truck outside. "We'll bring your family back here first. It's warmer, and my wife makes good tea."

They drove down the dark road to the car. Emine's mother almost cried with relief when she saw the truck's lights. Kaan was asleep against the car window.

The man looked at the engine with his flashlight. "Water," he said after a minute. "The engine needs water. This happens on long roads in the summer heat. It's not serious, but you can't drive it now."

"Can you fix it?" Emine's father asked.

"Not tonight, in the dark. But I have water at home, and the engine needs to cool down completely first. Tomorrow morning, we can try again."

They all went back to the man's house. Fatma made tea and gave Kaan warm milk. She put a blanket around Emine's shoulders. The house smelled like bread and wood smoke, and it felt safe after the long, dark road.

That night, Emine and her family slept in a small room with two beds, all together, listening to the wind outside.

In the morning, the man — his name was Hasan — helped Emine's father add water to the engine and check every part carefully. The car started without any smoke this time.

"Drive slowly," Hasan said. "Stop if you see smoke again. And check the water every few weeks — this is an old car, and it needs care."

Emine's father tried to give him money, but Hasan shook his head and refused it. "You needed help. I had a truck and some water. That's all this was."

They said goodbye and got in the car. As they drove away, Emine looked back at the small stone house, growing smaller behind them.

"We were lucky," her mother said quietly.

"We were," her father agreed. "Very lucky."

Emine thought about the small light in the dark, the one she saw from far away. It did not look like much from the road. But it turned out to be exactly what they needed.

They drove the rest of the way home in the morning light, the mountains behind them, the smoke only a memory now.
