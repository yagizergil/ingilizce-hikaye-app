# Tomatoes on the Wrong Side

Mrs. Aydan looked out of her kitchen window and saw a red tomato on her side of the garden.

It grew from a plant that belonged to her neighbor, Mr. Faruk. His plants pushed under the low fence between their two gardens, every summer, a little more.

She walked outside in her slippers and picked up the tomato. It was big and perfect, warm from the sun.

"This is mine now," she said to herself. "It grew on my side."

Mr. Faruk came out at that moment, with a watering can in his hand. He saw her holding the tomato.

"That is my tomato," he called across the fence. "It came from my plant."

"It grew on my side of the fence," Mrs. Aydan said. "So it is my tomato."

"Your side? The plant is mine. I planted it. I watered it every day."

"Then keep your plant on your side," she said. "It walks into my garden every year."

Mr. Faruk put down his watering can. "Plants do not walk," he said. "They grow. And this garden was one garden before you built that fence."

"I did not build the fence. My husband built it, twenty years ago, because your dog kept digging holes in my flowers."

"My dog died fifteen years ago."

"The fence stayed," Mrs. Aydan said. "For good reason."

They stood on each side of the low fence, both angry, both holding garden tools. Neither of them moved.

"Fine," Mr. Faruk said. "Keep the tomato. I am going to build my own fence. A real one. Taller. Then your side and my side will be truly separate."

"Good," Mrs. Aydan said. "Build it."

He walked away without another word. Mrs. Aydan looked down at the tomato in her hand. It suddenly did not look so good anymore.

That afternoon, she heard hammering from his garden. She looked over the low fence. Wooden posts stood in a line, right where the old fence used to be, but taller, much taller.

By evening, a new wooden wall stood between the two gardens. It was as tall as a person. Mrs. Aydan did not see Mr. Faruk's tomato plants anymore. She did not see his roses either, or his old apple tree.

She sat on her garden chair and looked at the new wall. For twenty years, she looked at that garden every morning with her coffee. Now she saw only wood.

Her granddaughter Ela came to visit the next day and found her sitting there, quiet.

"What happened to Mr. Faruk's garden?" Ela asked. "I cannot see it anymore."

"We had a fight," Mrs. Aydan said. "About a tomato."

"A tomato?" Ela laughed, then stopped when she saw her grandmother's face. "Are you sad about it?"

Mrs. Aydan did not answer for a moment. "I do not know," she finally said.

# The Tall Wooden Wall

The new wall stayed up all summer.

Mrs. Aydan's flowers still grew, but something felt different. She missed the sound of Mr. Faruk's radio in the mornings. She missed seeing his cat walk along the top of the old, low fence.

One hot day in August, Ela came to visit again. She brought a book and sat under the apple tree in her grandmother's garden.

"Grandma," she said, "why do you and Mr. Faruk not talk anymore?"

"We do not need to talk," Mrs. Aydan said. "We have a wall now."

"But you look at that wall every day. You look sad when you look at it."

Mrs. Aydan opened her mouth to argue, then closed it again.

The next morning, a strange smell came over the wall. Something was burning. Mrs. Aydan walked to the wall and listened. She heard a small, worried voice.

"Help! Somebody help!"

She recognized the voice. It was Mr. Faruk.

"What is wrong?" she called over the wall.

"My kitchen! There is smoke! I cannot find my phone!"

Mrs. Aydan did not think twice. She ran to her own kitchen and called the fire department. Then she ran back outside.

"They are coming!" she shouted over the wall. "Are you outside? Are you safe?"

"I am outside," Mr. Faruk shouted back. "But my old cat is still inside. She is hiding under the bed. She is scared of the smoke."

Mrs. Aydan looked at the tall wooden wall. There was no door in it, no way through, only around, through both front gates and down the street.

"I am coming," she said.

She ran around the block as fast as her legs let her. By the time she reached his front gate, small gray smoke was coming from his kitchen window.

"Where is the bedroom?" she asked.

"Last door on the left," Mr. Faruk said, his voice shaking. "Please, be careful."

Mrs. Aydan went inside, low to the ground, away from the smoke near the ceiling. She found the bedroom and looked under the bed. Two green eyes looked back at her.

"Come here, little one," she said softly. "Come to me."

The cat did not move. Mrs. Aydan reached under the bed slowly and pulled the frightened animal out. She held it close and walked back outside fast.

Mr. Faruk took the cat from her arms, tears in his eyes.

"Thank you," he said. "Thank you, Mrs. Aydan."

The fire truck arrived two minutes later. The firemen put out the small kitchen fire quickly — a pan of oil, left too long on the stove.

Mrs. Aydan and Mr. Faruk sat on his front steps together, the cat between them, both breathing hard.

"I forgot the oil," Mr. Faruk said. "I heard your voice, and I panicked, and I forgot everything else."

"It happens to everyone," Mrs. Aydan said. "I am just glad you are safe."

They sat quietly for a while, watching the firemen pack up their equipment.

"Mrs. Aydan," Mr. Faruk said finally, "about the wall—"

"Let us not talk about the wall today," she said. "Let us just sit here."

# One Door in the Wall

A week later, Ela visited again and found something new in the garden.

There was a door in the tall wooden wall. It was small, painted green, with a simple metal handle.

"Grandma! There is a door now!"

Mrs. Aydan smiled and put down her watering can. "Mr. Faruk built it," she said. "Last Tuesday."

"Why?"

"He said a wall with no door is not a fence. It is a prison. For both of us."

Ela pushed the door open carefully. On the other side stood Mr. Faruk, cutting roses with a small pair of scissors.

"Hello, Ela," he said. "Do you want to see my garden?"

Ela looked at her grandmother, who nodded. The three of them walked through the small door together.

Mr. Faruk's garden looked different up close — bigger, with more color than Mrs. Aydan remembered. His old apple tree still stood in the corner, full of small green apples. His cat slept in a patch of sun near the tomato plants.

"The tomatoes are doing well this year," Mr. Faruk said. "Maybe too well. They are growing toward your side again."

Mrs. Aydan laughed, really laughed, for the first time in weeks. "Then we share them," she said. "Half for you, half for me."

"Half for you, half for me," Mr. Faruk agreed. "And half for Ela, when she visits."

"That is three halves," Ela said, smiling. "That is not how math works."

"With good tomatoes," Mr. Faruk said, "the math is different."

That evening, Mrs. Aydan invited Mr. Faruk to have dinner in her garden, under the apple tree that used to be shared by both families. She made a salad with fresh tomatoes from both sides of the small green door.

"This tomato," she said, holding one up, "I really do not know which side it grew on."

"Does it matter?" Mr. Faruk asked.

"No," she said. "Not anymore."

They ate together as the sun went down, the small green door standing open between their two gardens. Mr. Faruk's cat walked between the chairs, looking for pieces of food. Somewhere behind them, in the fading light, tomato plants grew freely from one side to the other, not caring about fences at all.
