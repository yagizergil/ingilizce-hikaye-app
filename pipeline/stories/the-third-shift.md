# The Warm Press

The press was still warm when Derya reached machine seven, and nobody along line four had spoken for a quarter of an hour. Someone had already washed the floor, so that the smell of soap sat on top of the older smell of oil and hot metal. Twenty men in grey work clothes stood against the wall with their gloves in their hands, watching her walk the length of the silent line.

"They took him away twenty minutes ago," said Kemal, the line foreman, a heavy man whose voice usually carried over the noise of the whole shop and who was now almost whispering. "Two fingers of the right hand, and the doctor at the gate thought he might save one of them, although he refused to promise anything."

"Bora Demir, nineteen years old, and he started here in February," Derya said, because she had signed his training card herself in the spring. She set her bag down well away from the machine, took out her camera and her notebook, and turned to face the foreman. "Nobody touches anything until I say so, and that means the machine, the parts box and the floor."

Kemal spread his heavy hands, as though the matter had already been decided by somebody else. "The floor is washed, because I could not leave it in that state with the men walking past it every minute of the morning."

"Then tell me exactly what he was doing, in the order in which it happened, and do not decide for me which parts matter."

The story came out in pieces, the way such stories always did. A steel part had gone into the press crooked and had stuck in the lower half of the tool, and Bora had opened the safety gate and reached inside with his hand instead of using the metal hook that hangs on the side of every press in the building. The machine had come down while his hand was still in there, and the boy had not made a sound that anybody could remember afterwards.

"He knew about the hook, because everybody on this line knows about the hook," Kemal said, and there was something defensive lying underneath the words.

Derya wrote down the time, the date, the machine number and the exact words the foreman had used, in that order, because that was how Halit had taught her to build a page. Photographs first, then the words of the people who had seen it, and only then the machine itself. If you look at the machine first, he had told her, your eyes will find the answer you already expect, and after that you will stop looking.

She took eleven photographs before she touched anything at all. Then she stood in front of press seven and studied the safety gate, a steel frame with a wire window set into it, hanging across the front of the machine like a small door.

The rule of that gate was simple, and it was the same in every factory in the country: while the gate stands open the press cannot move, and when the gate closes the press is allowed to work. A small switch inside the frame tells the machine which of those two worlds it is living in at any given moment.

Derya opened the gate, and the green lamp on the control panel stayed on, which meant that the machine still believed itself free to work. She closed it and opened it again, and this time the lamp went out, exactly as it should have done. Working slowly, counting under her breath, she opened and shut that gate eleven times, and on three of those attempts the lamp did not go out at all.

"How long has it been doing that?" she asked, without turning round.

Kemal did not answer immediately, and behind her she could feel twenty men deciding at the same moment how much they were willing to say in front of a woman with a notebook.

"A while," he said at last. "Since the winter, perhaps. You have to lift the gate a little as you open it, and then it works properly, and every man on this line has known that trick for months. Every old machine in this building has a trick of some kind."

"It is not a trick," Derya said, closing the gate for the last time and reaching for her red tags. "It is a broken switch, and it is the reason a boy is in an ambulance instead of at his bench."

The far door of the shop opened, and the men along the wall straightened up before she had even turned around. Director Tunç came down the line with his coat still on and his telephone in his hand, a careful, quiet man of about fifty who had run the plant for six years and who had never once, in Derya's memory, raised his voice at anybody.

"How bad is it?" he asked Kemal, and when the foreman told him, he closed his eyes for a second, as though he were doing a sum he did not want to finish. Then he looked at the machine, and afterwards at Derya, and something in his face changed shape and became practical. "You are the safety inspector on shift, so this is yours. Aksoy, isn't it? Halit's girl."

"Derya Aksoy," she said, "and I am putting a stop tag on this press now, and it stays there until my report is finished."

"Of course it does, and you may take whatever you need from anybody in this building." He looked down the line at the four other presses, all of them standing silent under their lights. "How long will that be?"

"I cannot answer that yet, and I would rather not invent a date in front of twenty witnesses."

"Then let me tell you what I can tell you instead," he said gently, without any threat in it, as though he were describing the weather. "Line four makes the frame parts for the pump order, which is the largest contract this plant has ever taken, and the last date on it is the twentieth of next month. If line four is not running by Monday, we begin losing money that we do not have, and in the autumn I shall be standing in front of these same men explaining why some of them are going home."

"Then somebody should have replaced the switch in the spring," Derya said, and the shop went very quiet.

Kemal studied the floor as though it needed washing again. The director looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable, and she understood, too late, that she had said the true thing rather than the careful thing, and that in this building those two were not always the same.

"Write your report, all of it," Tunç said. "I would far rather have it from you than from the government inspector who arrives next week."

He walked back down the line and out through the far door. Derya tied the red tag to the control panel, signed it and wrote the date, and then stood there with one hand flat on the warm side of the machine, remembering the first thing Halit Erdem had ever said to her, four years earlier, on her first afternoon in this building. Any doubt, stop the machine, and argue about it afterwards.

# Report 0314

The record room lay in the basement of the office building, and it smelled of paper and cold concrete even in July. Sevim, who had kept those records for nineteen years and guarded them like a mother, looked up from her desk and read Derya's face before either of them had spoken.

"Line four," she said, and it was not a question. "Tell me first whether the boy is alive, and then tell me what you want from my shelves."

"He is alive, and I need the fault book for line four from January onwards."

"You need tea before you need anything, and you will get both, because I have never once let an inspector work down here on an empty stomach."

The fault book was a thick, ugly green folder in which every machine problem in the plant was written by hand on a numbered form by whoever had found it. The top copies travelled upstairs into the offices, while the bottom copy stayed here in the basement under Sevim's eye, because Sevim did not believe in throwing anything away.

Derya worked backwards from that morning, finding April thin and March a good deal thicker. She turned the pages slowly, reading every line instead of hunting for the one she expected, and near the middle of March she stopped.

Fault report number 0314, machine press seven, line four, written by K. Aydın, night shift operator, in careful capital letters by a man who had clearly not enjoyed writing any of it. The safety gate does not always stop the machine when it is opened, and it works about four times in five. I found this on Tuesday night and I have shown it to the shift foreman. The machine should not be used until it has been repaired.

Underneath, in the box marked action taken, somebody else had written two lines in a small, fast, sloping hand that Derya had been reading for four years. Fault confirmed, part not held in store, so a new switch has been requested and the machine is to be watched until it arrives.

Below that, in the same ink and the same hand but dated eleven days later, stood a single sentence that closed the whole page. No further action required at this time, and the machine will be checked at the yearly service in October.

The signature at the bottom was one letter H, one letter E, and a single line drawn beneath both.

Derya sat very still for a while. Somewhere above her a door shut and a man laughed in the corridor, and the sound came down through the ceiling as though it belonged to another building entirely.

"Who signs off the action box at the bottom of these forms?" she asked, although she already knew the answer and simply wanted to hear it said by somebody else.

"The safety manager signs everything on this side of the plant, and Halit has been signing that book for eleven years," Sevim said, without looking up. "Which page are you holding?"

Derya put her hand flat on the paper, as if it might blow away in a room where there was no wind at all. She had come to this factory at twenty-three with a certificate and no experience, and the men on the floor had looked straight through her for a month. Halit Erdem had walked her along every line, machine by machine, for six weeks, and had made her write out the guard rules until she could repeat them in her sleep. A safety inspector who wants to be liked, he had told her more than once, is of no use to anybody in a building full of moving steel.

He had also stood in the director's office two years ago and said that if the plant let her go at the end of her first contract, it would be losing the only person in the building who read the fault book properly.

"Was there a second report about this machine, after March?" she asked. "Anything at all, from anybody?"

"If there was, then it is in the book, because everything is in the book."

There was nothing. Derya went through April, May, June and the first half of July twice, page by page, and press seven appeared only once more, in May, and only because a lamp on the control panel had stopped working.

She photographed report 0314 from four angles and then asked for the purchase records, and Sevim's face changed as she said it.

"Purchases are not mine, because they live upstairs in the buying office, and I can ask for them but I cannot promise them." Sevim folded her arms and waited until Derya finally looked up at her. "Whose signature is on that page, Derya?"

"You knew before I opened the folder, and I think you have known since March."

"Then think carefully about what you are doing before you go upstairs asking for paper with people's names on it." Her voice was steady and unhappy at the same time. "Halit has eleven months left before he retires, and a wife who has been ill for two years, and I do not need to explain to you what happens to a man of fifty-eight in this town if he leaves a job like this in the wrong way."

"A boy lost two fingers this morning on a machine which a piece of paper says should have been stopped in March."

"I know perfectly well what happened this morning, and I am not telling you to hide a single word of it." Sevim closed the green folder gently, as if it were sleeping. "I am telling you that you cannot put that page into a report and then be surprised by what it does afterwards, because once it is written it stops belonging to you."

Derya climbed the stairs with the photographs in her pocket. Through the window on the landing she could see line four standing silent under its lights, with the red tag on press seven showing small and bright at that distance. Halit's office was the third door on the second floor, and his name sat on a plastic plate that had been there so long that the letters had gone grey. She stood outside it for perhaps thirty seconds, and then she knocked.

# The Rule He Taught Her

He was standing at the window with his back to the door, and he did not turn around when she came in.

"They have taken him to the state hospital, and I telephoned twice this morning," Halit said. "They think one finger can be saved, and the boy is left-handed, which is the only good news that anybody has given me today. I taught him the guard rules myself in February, two hours in the training room with the model, and he could repeat every one of them back to me."

When he finally turned he looked older than he had looked on Friday, and he had not shaved. Derya laid the photograph of report 0314 on his desk, face up, and pushed it across the wood towards him.

He looked at it for a long time without picking it up, and then sat down slowly, the way men sit down when their knees have settled the matter before their minds have.

"It is my signature, and you knew that before you climbed the stairs," he said. "So ask me the question you came up here to ask."

"Then explain the third line to me, because the first two lines are the work of a good safety manager and the third one closed the report."

"Do you want me to explain it or to defend it, because those are different jobs and I can only manage one of them today?" He rubbed his face with both hands. "The switch failed in March, Aydın wrote it up properly, and he was right, and I checked the machine myself the following morning. I ordered the part that same week, and it costs roughly as much as a decent pair of shoes, and it comes from one supplier who told us six weeks."

"And after that?"

"After that the buying office refused the order, along with every other order in the plant that was not for the pump contract." He looked up at her without any attempt to arrange his face. "There was a freeze on spending from the middle of March, and you never heard about it because you work on the floor, and the floor is not told about money. I went to Tunç and told him what the fault was and what it could do to somebody's hand, and he answered that the freeze would last six weeks, that I should watch the machine, and that the part would go through in May."

"Did you write any part of that conversation down anywhere at all?"

"I wrote the first two lines that you are holding in your hand, and nothing else."

"Then let us talk about the third line, because you did not write it in March." Her voice came out harder than she had planned, and she let it. "You wrote it eleven days later, on the same page and with the same pen, and it closed the report. After that, nobody in this building had any reason to look at press seven again until October."

Halit did not answer for a while, and the hum of the plant came up faintly through the floor between them.

"Because an open fault report on a machine means a stopped machine, and that is the rule I taught you and the rule I still believe in," he said at last. "For as long as that report sat open in the book, either the press stopped or I was a man who had left an open report on a running machine, and every week that this remained true it became harder to live with. So I closed the page and promised myself that I would check the gate every single day."

"And did you?"

"For about three weeks, and then the contract arrived, with four new men to train and a fire drill to organise, and the gate worked perfectly every time I stood in front of it." He said it quietly, without softening any part of it. "It works four times out of five, Derya, and that is exactly what makes a switch like that so dangerous."

She had imagined this conversation on the stairs, and in her imagination he had lied to her, and she had been ready for a liar. She was not ready for this.

"You are going to write all of it, aren't you," he said, and it was not really a question either. "Then write it properly, and not in the usual way, where the machine breaks, the story is sad and everybody learns a lesson. Write the freeze, write the date on which I asked for the part, and write the name of the man who told me to wait for it."

"I am going to write your name as well, in the first section, next to that third line."

"I know you are," Halit said, "because I put it there myself."

Her telephone buzzed against her belt as she reached the corridor, and the director wanted to see her.

Tunç's office was warm and quiet, with the noise of the plant reduced by the glass to a low hum. He asked her to sit down, and asked after her mother, whom he had met once at a company dinner, and only then did he come to the reason.

"Your contract ends in September, and the paper making you permanent has been sitting on my desk since June," he said. "Halit wrote the recommendation himself in April, and I want to sign the thing this week."

"Thank you," Derya said, as carefully as she could manage.

"I also want to tell you how these reports normally go, because you have never written one for a real accident before." He leaned back in his chair. "The government inspector comes on Thursday, and he will read your report first, since you were the first qualified person to reach the machine, so what you write shapes everything that follows it. If your report says that the switch failed and the operator put his hand where his hand should never have gone, that is a true report and nobody alive could argue with a word of it. The machine gets a new switch, the boy gets his money, and four hundred people keep their work."

"And if the report says more than that, what happens then?"

"Then it becomes a story about people rather than about a machine, and once it is about people it stops being about safety at all." He said it kindly, almost sadly. "I have watched that happen twice in my life, and on both occasions everybody spent a year defending himself and nothing whatever was repaired."

"There was a spending freeze in March," Derya said, and something moved behind the director's eyes and then went still again.

"There was, and there is a freeze in every factory in this country every March, which is neither a crime nor the reason why a boy of nineteen reached into a press without the hook."

"It is the reason the switch was still broken in July, and that sentence belongs in the report whether it is a crime or not."

Tunç stood up, and she understood that the meeting had ended. "Write what you saw, because I have never asked anybody in this building to write anything else," he said. "But be certain of every line, because you cannot take any of it back, and you will be the youngest person in that room on Thursday morning."

# The April Sheets

That evening she went back down to the record room with a torch, because the basement lights switched off at seven and Sevim had handed her the key without asking a single question, which was its own sort of answer.

The buying office had sent down a thin folder marked with the month of March, and near the back of it lay a request form for one safety gate switch for press seven, line four, written in Halit's small sloping hand and dated the sixteenth. Across the bottom, in blue pen, somebody had written five words: not urgent, hold until pump contract complete. The letters beneath them were a T and a small mark shaped like the wing of a bird.

Derya photographed the form twice and then sat down on the cold floor with her back against the shelves.

She should have stopped at that point, since she now held the fault, the report, the closing line, the refused order and the director's own handwriting, and any inspector in the country would have called that a complete file. Halit, however, had taught her that a file is only finished when the dull paper has been read as carefully as the interesting paper, so she pulled down the monthly check sheets for line four and worked through them.

Those sheets were the most boring documents in the building. Once a month a certified inspector walks every machine on a line, tests every guard, and signs a single page with twelve boxes on it.

April, line four, all twelve boxes marked as tested and correct, press seven among them. The signature at the bottom of the page was her own.

She stared at it for a long moment before the memory arrived. It had been the end of April, a Friday afternoon, in the corridor outside the training room, and she had been carrying the folder for the guard course when Halit came out of his office with three check sheets and a pen. He had walked lines three and four himself that morning, he said, because he had wanted to look at the new men, and she was the certified inspector for the shop that month, so the sheets needed her name at the bottom. She had signed all three against the wall, standing up, in about eleven seconds.

May, line four, her signature again. June, line four, her signature again.

She sat on the basement floor with the torch shaking very slightly in her hand. Three pages stated that a qualified person had opened the safety gate on press seven and confirmed that the machine stopped, and behind those three pages there was nothing at all, because on none of those days had she stood in front of that machine and touched that gate. Halit's signature had closed the fault report, and hers had quietly kept it closed for eleven weeks.

She found him the next morning in the yard by the gate, where he parked, and he saw at once that something in her face had changed overnight.

"The April sheets," she said, and he looked away across the car park while a whole argument happened inside him and lost.

"I walked line four that morning, and I opened that gate three times, and it stopped three times, and I remember being pleased about it," he said.

"You gave me a page to sign for a check that I had never carried out, and then you did it again in May and again in June."

"Yes, because I trusted my own eyes more than the paper, and because you were standing in front of me with a pen in your hand." He said it flatly, without any excuse in it. "And because after a while a man stops noticing what he has stopped doing, and there is nothing clever underneath that at all."

A truck came in through the gate behind them, and neither of them spoke until the noise had passed.

"So now you know what your report does," Halit said. "It ends me, which is a small thing at my age and which I have certainly earned. It takes a piece out of Tunç, which he has also earned, although men like Tunç always survive it. And it puts your name on three false check sheets in front of a government inspector, in the fourth year of a career that has not properly begun."

"I know exactly what it does, and I have known since eight o'clock last night."

"Then leave the sheets out of it, and understand that I am not asking you to protect me." He turned to face her fully. "Put my signature on report 0314 in your first paragraph, in capital letters if it pleases you. Those three pages tell nobody anything that the file does not already prove, and they will cost you your certificate for the sake of it. I gave you those pages, and it was my hand and not yours."

"It was my name, and a name on a check sheet is the whole reason the sheet exists."

"You were twenty-five and you trusted the man who had trained you, and if that is a crime then this factory should have closed thirty years ago."

Derya thought about Bora Demir, nineteen years old and left-handed, sitting in a state hospital with his right hand wrapped, waiting to learn how many fingers he would have for the next sixty years. She thought about the third line on report 0314 and about the reason it existed at all, which was that an open report made Halit a man who left open reports, and closing the page had been cheaper than fighting the freeze.

"That is precisely what you did in March," she said. "You took the true thing out because leaving it in was going to cost you too much."

Halit opened his mouth, and then said nothing whatsoever.

"I am sorry," she said, and discovered that she meant it, and that meaning it changed nothing at all. "I am writing every part of it."

She worked until two in the morning at her kitchen table, and when she had finished the report ran to nine pages. She read it through three times: once for the facts, once for the language, and once at the very end to be sure that she had not made herself look better in any single sentence than the evidence allowed.

# Rule One

The meeting room on the second floor held six people on Thursday morning. The government inspector was a thin, grey man named Pekcan who wrote continuously and hardly ever looked up. Tunç sat at the far end with the plant lawyer beside him, while Sevim had been called as the keeper of the records and sat with her hands folded on the green folder. Halit sat alone against the wall near the door, and nobody had asked him to be there.

Derya read her report aloud, because Pekcan asked her to and because the first paragraph made it clear that she intended to. She read the description of the machine and of the fault, and then Aydın's report of the fourteenth of March, word for word, including the sentence stating that the machine should not be used until it had been repaired. She read the two lines that confirmed the fault and ordered the part, and afterwards the refusal on the purchase form, together with the words hold until pump contract complete and the date beside them. She read the third line on report 0314, the line that had closed it, and then she read out the signature.

Then she turned the page and read the final section without altering her voice.

"Between April and June the monthly guard checks for line four record press seven as tested and working, and those three sheets are signed by me. I did not carry out those tests. I signed the sheets in a corridor without reading them, because they were handed to me by the safety manager, who told me that he had walked the line himself. I had no reason to doubt him and I did not ask a single question. Had those checks been done as the sheets claim, the fault would have been found again in April, and press seven would have been stopped fourteen weeks before Bora Demir put his hand into it."

The room had gone completely silent, and Pekcan had stopped writing.

"The failure of the switch is the cause of the injury, and the refusal of the part is the reason the switch was never replaced," Derya said. "The closing of report 0314 is the reason nobody chased that part, and my signatures are the reason nobody found the fault a second time. All four of those things had to happen together, and all four of them did."

She put the pages down and sat back, and was faintly surprised to find her hands perfectly steady.

The lawyer began to talk about operating procedures and the correct use of the hook, and Pekcan allowed him roughly a minute before he raised one finger and the lawyer stopped.

"Mr Tunç, is the handwriting on that purchase form yours?" the inspector asked, and Tunç looked down the table at Derya for a moment with something in his face that was closer to tiredness than to anger.

"It is mine, and I held that order along with eleven others in the same month, believing that I was holding them for six weeks."

"You held it for seventeen," Pekcan said, and wrote something down.

Then Halit stood up at the back of the room without permission, and everybody turned towards him. "The third line on report 0314 is mine and it should never have been written," he said. "Inspector Aksoy did not fail to carry out those checks, because I handed her three pages in a corridor and asked her to sign them, and I have done the same thing with junior inspectors more than once in eleven years, as the record room can confirm. That habit belongs to this factory, and I am the man who built it."

He sat down again, and Sevim, without saying one word, opened the green folder and slid it along the table towards the inspector.

The committee took eleven days. Press seven was rebuilt and every guard switch in the plant was replaced within a month, at a cost which Derya later worked out to be slightly less than one day of the pump contract. Tunç kept his position, received a formal warning from the board, and paid a state fine out of his own money. Halit Erdem left the factory at the end of August, ten months earlier than he had planned, without a farewell dinner or a speech from anybody.

Derya was suspended for six weeks and given a written warning that would sit in her file for three years. In his own report, Pekcan wrote one line about her that she cut out and kept: the inspector's account was complete, and it included evidence against herself which no other person present would have produced.

She went to see Bora Demir in September, on the day he came back to the plant to work in the store room. He had three fingers on his right hand and a direct, unfriendly way of looking at people which he had not possessed in February.

"They say that you wrote your own name into the report," he said, "and my father says that was a stupid thing for anybody to do."

"It belonged there, and a report which leaves out the inconvenient names is not a report at all."

"My mother says that is why the switches were changed, and I honestly do not know which of the two of them is right."

"Neither do I," Derya said. "Come and find me if any machine in that store room does anything twice that it should not do once."

In October a package arrived for her at the gate with no letter inside it, only a small worn notebook of the kind Halit had carried in his top pocket every day for eleven years, filled with dates and machine numbers and short remarks in that fast sloping hand. On the inside of the cover he had written four words and nothing else: any doubt, stop it.

She did not know whether he would ever speak to her again, since she had written to him twice and received no answer, and she had stopped expecting one. She carried that notebook out onto the floor every day afterwards, and when new inspectors came through their six weeks of training she made them write out the guard rules until they could repeat them in their sleep, and she never once handed anybody a page to sign in a corridor.
