# Eight Lines of Music

The lift had been broken for a week, so Deniz climbed to the fifth floor with the smell of the hotel still in her coat. It was ten past seven in the morning, and the light in the stairway was grey and thin. She let herself in, dropped her keys into the bowl by the door, and pulled the curtains shut. Sleep was the only thing she wanted.

Her work at the hotel desk finished at seven, and her classes did not begin until four, so the hours in between belonged to her bed. She had learned to sleep through traffic, through the man downstairs who shouted at the football on television, and through the water pipes that knocked like a visitor. In three weeks she would take the most important exam of her year. If she failed it, she would lose the money that paid half of her rent.

She was asleep by half past seven. At two o'clock the piano began.

The sound came through the wall beside her head, close enough to feel in the pillow. It was always the same eight lines of music: one hand first, then two hands together, slow and careful. The player reached the same place every time, a run of notes that climbed and then fell. Every time, the run broke apart like a person missing a step on the stairs. Then there was silence for a few seconds. Then the eight lines started again from the beginning.

Deniz lay on her back and counted. She counted eleven attempts before she understood that sleep was finished for the day.

She got up, drank a glass of water at the sink, and stood with her hand flat against the wall. The sound was warm and wooden, an old piano and not a machine. On the twelfth attempt the run broke in exactly the same place, and something inside Deniz broke with it. She hit the wall three times with the side of her fist.

The music stopped. For half a minute the whole building was quiet. Then it began again, from the beginning, at the same patient speed.

She had lived here for fourteen months and had never met the person in flat 5B. There was only a brown door with a small round window of glass, and letters that sat in the box downstairs for days. One day she had seen a pair of men's shoes outside that door, cleaned and placed neatly side by side. The person inside, she had decided, was tidy and probably old. Old people, she thought, were supposed to sleep in the afternoon like everybody else.

Her mother called at three, while Deniz was eating bread and cheese over the sink.

"You sound tired," her mother said.

"The lift is broken," Deniz answered, because that was easier than the truth.

Through the wall the run climbed, fell, and broke. Her mother did not hear it, and Deniz did not explain.

That night she fell asleep on the bus and woke three stops past the hotel, which meant that she began work eight minutes late. Mr Servet, the night manager, looked at the clock and said nothing, and his silence was worse than shouting. At four in the morning she wrote the same guest's name into the book twice. When she walked home at seven, the sky was the colour of dirty water. Somewhere above her, on the fifth floor, somebody was already awake.

On the fourth day she wrote the note.

# The Rule on the Door

The note was short, because she was too tired to be polite for long. "I work at night and I sleep between eight and two. Please play in the evening. Your neighbour in 5A." She pushed it under the brown door and went to bed feeling almost hopeful.

At two o'clock the piano began, and the run broke in the usual place.

The building manager lived on the ground floor with a cat and a television that was never off. Aygül Hanım listened to the whole story with her arms folded, and then she shook her head slowly.

"That is Halit Bey in 5B," she said. "He has lived here for twenty-two years and he has never given anyone a single problem."

"He is giving me one now," Deniz said. "I have an exam. I am going to lose my job."

Aygül Hanım looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she found a pen and a sheet of paper, and she wrote out the building rule about noise in large letters. The paper went up beside the lift that afternoon: no music, no drilling and no loud work between eight in the morning and four in the afternoon.

The next day the wall stayed silent. Deniz slept until half past one and woke up feeling like a different person. She studied for four hours before her classes, and for the first time in a month she understood the chapter she was reading. On the second silent day she even had time to wash her clothes.

On the third day she met him on the stairs.

He was coming up with two shopping bags, a tall thin man of about seventy, resting at each floor with his back against the wall. Deniz took one of the bags without asking, and he was too out of breath to argue. At the fifth floor he put his key in the brown door and turned around.

"You are the girl from 5A," he said. "I want to say sorry about the piano. Aygül Hanım explained to me that you work at night."

"It is all right," Deniz said. "It is quiet now."

"The rule is a good rule." He was holding the bag handles with both hands, and she could see that his hands were shaking. They moved like the surface of water in a moving car. He noticed her looking and smiled without any embarrassment. "They have been like this for three years. Please do not worry about it."

He thanked her again and closed the door, and Deniz went into her own flat and slept until two.

The poster was in the bread shop window on the corner, between the price list and a photograph of a wedding cake. She only stopped because she was waiting for bread. It was a plain white sheet with a photograph of a woman at a piano. The woman was young in the picture, with her hair tied back and her eyes on her hands.

"An evening for Nurten Aksu," the poster said. "Forty years of teaching in this neighbourhood. The culture hall, Saturday the twentieth, at seven o'clock. Her students will play."

Deniz read it twice. Then she thought about the letter box downstairs, the one for flat 5B, with a strip of old paper on it that said AKSU in careful blue letters.

She stood in the shop with the warm bread in her hands and counted the days on the calendar behind the bread shelves. There were twelve.

# Twelve Days

She knocked on the brown door at half past four, before her classes, when she was sure he would be awake.

The flat smelled of coffee and old paper. In the main room there was a brown piano against the shared wall, and above it, on a shelf, a small wooden machine that ticked when he turned it on. There were photographs everywhere: children at the piano, teenagers at the piano, a woman with grey hair standing behind a boy whose feet did not reach the floor.

"My wife taught in this room for forty years," Halit said. "The neighbours were very patient. Some of them learned here as children, so they could not really complain later."

"And you?" Deniz asked. "Did she teach you?"

"Never." He laughed and sat down on the piano seat with his back to the keys. "I sold shoes for thirty-five years. I listened to her every day, but I never touched the piano while she was alive, because I did not want to be one more student. She used to say that I had good hands. Now look at them."

The music on the stand was thin and yellow, and the notes were large, the kind that are printed for beginners. Deniz did not need to be told which eight lines they were.

"She died in the spring," he said. "Her students want to make an evening for her, and they have asked me to stand up and speak. I do not want to speak. I want to play the piece she played on Sunday mornings. I have been learning it since June, and I can play eight lines of it. Then my hand comes to that place and it stops."

"So play it," Deniz said.

"The rule is on the door by the lift."

"I will speak to Aygül Hanım. Play in the afternoon, and I will manage."

He looked at her carefully, the way people look at a promise that is too generous. Then he thanked her. That afternoon at two o'clock the piano began again, and Deniz lay in the dark with her eyes open, listening to the run climb and fall and break.

She managed for three days. On the fourth night she fell asleep at the desk at four in the morning with her cheek on the guest book. Mr Servet woke her by putting a cup of tea down beside her head, very quietly, and that was somehow the worst thing he could have done.

"You are a good worker," he said. "But this desk cannot be empty at night. If I find you asleep here again, do not come back the next evening."

Two days later her practice exam paper came back with 40 out of 100 written at the top in red.

That afternoon she lay in bed and listened to the eight lines and felt something turn over inside her chest. She was not angry with the old man any more, and that was the problem. Anger would have been simple. Now there were two things she wanted, and the days were disappearing, and only one of the two could win.

There were eight days left.

# The Hall in the Morning

The culture hall was a low grey building behind the market, and it smelled of cold stone and soap. Deniz went there on Monday morning instead of going home to bed.

Inside, a man was pushing a long brush across the wooden floor between the rows of seats. On the stage stood a black piano, closed and silent.

"It is open from six in the evening," the man said. "Concerts, meetings, children's shows. In the mornings I clean."

"Does anyone use the piano in the morning?"

"Nobody has used it since June." He rested on his brush. "Why?"

His name was Rıza, and he listened to the whole story while the water dried on the floor around him. He said the hall was not officially open before six, and that he could not give a key to a stranger. But he was here from nine until eleven every morning anyway. If the old man came while he was cleaning, then nobody would be in the building who should not be there. That was how he put it, slowly, as if he were explaining it to himself as well.

"He can come," Rıza said finally. "But somebody has to come with him. I am not looking after anybody's grandfather on my own."

Halit refused at first. He stood at his open door and said that a hall was for people who could play, and that Rıza would hear every mistake.

"Rıza hears everything in that building and says nothing to anybody," Deniz said. "And he has a brush. He will be at the back."

The next morning she met him at the corner at nine o'clock. He carried the yellow music under his arm like a document, and he walked more slowly than she wanted to walk. Inside, he sat down at the black piano and did not touch it for almost a minute. Then he played the eight lines, and the run broke, and the sound of the break went up into the high roof and came back down on all of them.

Deniz sat in the third row with her books open on her knees. She slept from half past eleven until half past three instead of from eight until two. That was two hours less, but the hall was quiet and warm, and she got more studying done there than she had ever done in her flat.

On the fourth morning she recorded the run on her phone.

Sevgi sat next to her in the Tuesday class and studied music teaching, and she listened to the recording twice with her head bent.

"He is putting his hand in the wrong place," she said. "He waits and then jumps. He should move his thumb under on the third note, before he needs it, and then his hand is already where it has to be. And he must play it at half speed for a week, even when he thinks he can go faster."

"He has six days."

"Then he must play it at half speed for six days," Sevgi said, and she lifted her shoulders.

Halit was not grateful. He said that he was seventy-two years old and that his fingers would do what they wanted. Then he tried it slowly, four times. On the fifth attempt the run climbed and fell and did not break, and he sat and looked at his own hands as if they belonged to somebody else.

"She used to tell her students something," he said. "The wrong note is not the problem. Stopping is the problem."

# The Twentieth

The exam was on Thursday morning, in a cold room with numbered desks. Deniz had slept five hours, which was not enough. But she had studied in the third row of the culture hall for nine days, and the questions were not strangers. She wrote until the last minute and walked out into the sunlight with ink on her hand.

On Saturday she finished work at seven in the morning, slept until three, and ironed the only white shirt she owned that did not belong to the hotel.

The hall was almost full by ten past seven. There were old people, and parents with children, and grown men of forty who kept saying to each other, "She taught me too." Rıza had put out extra chairs at the back and was standing by the door in a jacket. On the stage the black piano was open, and the photograph from the shop window had been made large and set on a stand beside it.

Nine students played first. A girl of about ten played a short piece almost perfectly. A woman in her fifties played something long and difficult and cried a little at the end, and the hall was loud with hands for a long time.

Then Halit walked out from the side of the stage in a grey suit, and the room went very quiet, because most of them knew him and none of them had ever heard him play.

He sat down. He put the thin yellow music on the stand, although Deniz knew that he no longer needed it. He waited, and his hands shook above the keys, and somebody behind Deniz breathed in quickly.

The first eight lines came out slowly and clearly, the way they had come through her bedroom wall a hundred times. Then the run began to climb. His thumb moved under on the third note, exactly as Sevgi had said, and the notes went up. Near the top of the run, his hand stopped.

The silence lasted perhaps three seconds. It felt much longer to Deniz, who was sitting in the fourth row with her hands closed tightly together.

Then Halit put his fingers back at the start of that line and played it again. This time the run climbed and fell and went on, and the piece continued into a part that Deniz had never heard in her life. It was quiet and simple and it lasted about two minutes more. When it finished, the hall stood up.

He found her afterwards near the door, by the tea table, and his hands were shaking worse than ever.

"I stopped," he said. "In front of all of them, I stopped."

"You started again," Deniz said.

Her result came out the following Wednesday: 63 out of 100. It was not a beautiful number, but it was a passing one, and the money would come again in October. Mr Servet said nothing about the exam, because she had never told him about it, but he began giving her Sunday nights off.

On Thursday she came home at seven in the morning, pulled the curtains shut, and slept. At two o'clock the piano started, and it was not the same eight lines. It was something new, slow and uncertain, with wrong notes falling into the quiet like dropped coins.

Deniz turned over, pulled the blanket up to her ear, and was asleep before the second line.
