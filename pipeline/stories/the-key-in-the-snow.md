# The Small Silver Key

Emre pushed the shop door with his shoulder. The wood was old and it always stuck in cold weather. Inside, the air smelled of bread and coffee. His grandmother stood behind the counter with her coat already on.

"You are late," she said.

"The bus was slow," Emre said. "Snow everywhere."

She did not answer. She looked at the window instead. The street outside was white and quiet. Only one car moved, very slowly, with yellow lights.

"I must go to your aunt tonight," his grandmother said. "She is sick. I am going to stay two days."

Emre put his bag on the floor. "Two days?"

"You can open the shop. You are seventeen. You know the work." She took a small silver key from her pocket and held it out. "This one opens the back door. The delivery man comes at six in the morning. He puts the boxes in the back. Nobody else has this key."

Emre looked at the key. It was smaller than he expected. There was a red string through the hole at the top.

"Only one key?" he asked.

"Only one." His grandmother closed his fingers around it. Her hands were dry and warm. "Your grandfather made this door himself. He never wanted two keys. He said one key means one person is responsible."

Emre wanted to laugh, but her face was serious. He put the key in his coat pocket.

"Six o'clock," she said again. "The man does not wait. If the door is closed, he goes away, and we get no bread for the whole week."

"I can do it."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up her bag and walked to the door. At the door she stopped.

"Emre. The pocket."

"What?"

"That pocket has a hole. I saw it last month." She pointed at his coat. "Use the other one."

"It is fine," Emre said.

His grandmother made a small sound. She went out into the snow. Through the window Emre watched her walk to the corner. She was short and she moved slowly, but she did not slip. She never slipped.

He locked the front door and turned off the lights. The shop was dark and warm and completely his. He liked that feeling. He liked it very much.

Then he pushed his hand into his pocket to touch the key again.

His fingers went straight through the cloth.

# Under the Streetlight

Emre ran outside without his hat.

The street was empty. The snow was not deep. But it was soft and new, and it covered everything — the road, the cars, the front steps of the shop. His grandmother's footprints were already thin lines.

He walked back the way he came. He looked down. Snow, snow, snow. A cigarette box. A bottle top. No key.

He stopped under the streetlight in front of the bank. He remembered standing here. He remembered pushing his hands into his pockets because the wind was cold. The key must be here, or between here and the shop.

He got down on his knees and pushed the snow with his hands. His fingers went red, then white. He found a coin. He found a small piece of glass. He did not find the key.

A voice came from behind him.

"Are you looking for money?"

Emre turned. A girl stood by the bank door with a big brush in her hands. She wore a grey coat and she was cleaning the steps.

"A key," Emre said. "A small one. Silver, with a red string."

"When?"

"Twenty minutes ago. Maybe less."

The girl looked at the street. Then she looked at the sky. It was still snowing, in small dry pieces.

"That is bad," she said. "In twenty minutes the snow can cover a key. And people walk. Feet push things down."

"I know," Emre said. He heard his own voice and it sounded strange, high and fast.

"Why is it important?"

"The delivery comes at six. I must open the back door. There is only one key."

The girl put the brush against the wall. "Then we have a problem," she said, "and we are going to need a plan."

Emre looked at her. "We?"

"I finish at eleven," she said. "It is ten now. And I am cold and bored." She smiled a little. "My name is Nesli."

# What the Snow Keeps

Nesli was better at this than Emre.

She did not push the snow with her hands. She got a piece of card from the bank bin and she moved the snow in long, slow lines, like a farmer. She started at the shop door and she worked towards the streetlight, one metre at a time.

"You must think about where the hole is," she said. "In your pocket. Left side or right side?"

"Right."

"Then the key fell on your right. Not in the middle of the road." She moved to the side of the road, close to the wall. "Things fall and then they move to the low place. The road here is not flat. Look."

She was right. The ground went down a little towards the road.

They worked. The church clock hit eleven, then twelve. Emre's feet stopped feeling like feet. Twice a car came and they stood back against the wall and waited.

At one o'clock Nesli sat down on the shop step.

"I am going to say something and you are not going to like it," she said.

"Say it."

"Maybe the key is not on the street. Maybe it is in the shop. Maybe it fell inside, near the door, when you put it in your pocket."

"I looked."

"You looked for two seconds and then you ran outside."

Emre opened his mouth to argue. Then he closed it. He took his own key for the front door and he opened the shop.

They turned on all the lights. They moved the boxes by the door. They looked under the table. They found dust and an old pen. They found a photograph too. It showed his grandfather at the same door, forty years ago, with a hammer in his hand.

No key.

"Right," Nesli said. "Now we know. It is outside."

It was half past two in the morning. The snow stopped. The sky went clear and hard and full of stars. The cold became a different kind of cold, the kind that hurts.

"I can call her," Emre said. "I can tell her now, and she can come back tomorrow, and—"

"And what? She is with your sick aunt." Nesli stood up and picked up her piece of card. "Three hours. We have three hours."

They went back to the streetlight. They started again from the other end. This time they moved the snow all the way down to the stone.

At twenty past five Emre's card hit something hard.

He thought it was a stone. He almost pushed it away. Then he saw a thin red line in the white.

He tried to pick it up. His fingers did not work anymore. Nesli took it out of the snow. She put it in his hand and closed his fingers around it, exactly like his grandmother.

"Go," she said.

He ran. He fell once, on the corner, and he did not feel it. He opened the back door at twelve minutes to six. He stood there and he breathed. His breath went out white into the dark yard.

The delivery man came at six exactly. He looked at Emre — no hat, wet coat, red hands — and he looked at the open door.

"Long night?" he said.

"Yes," Emre said.

The man carried in the boxes. Bread, and flour, and two big bags of sugar. When he went away, Emre locked the back door and put the key in his left pocket. Then he took his coat off. He found a needle and thread in his grandmother's drawer. He sat down on the shop step in the grey morning light, and he sewed the hole shut.

He did it badly. The line was not straight. But it held.

At eight o'clock Nesli walked past the window with her brush. She did not come in. She just looked at the boxes on the counter and lifted her hand, and Emre lifted his hand too.

His grandmother came home two days later. She looked at the shop. She looked at the empty flour bags. She did not say anything about the delivery.

At the door, she stopped, the same way she stopped before.

"Emre. Your coat."

"What about it?"

"The pocket," she said. "Somebody sewed it. Very badly."

"Yes."

She agreed slowly. She put her bag on the shelf. She went behind the counter and she started to make the coffee.

"Good," she said.
