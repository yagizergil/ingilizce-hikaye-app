# Twenty-One Out of Twenty-Two

The chairs were still upside down on the tables when Derya laid her papers out on the long table by the window. Aylin stood at the sink with her sleeves pushed up, washing a pot that nobody had touched for six weeks. They had not spoken much since the funeral, and the kitchen felt larger than it used to feel.

"I made a list," Derya said. "Twenty-two dishes. If we agree on it today, the print shop can have the file by Friday."

Aylin dried her hands on a towel and came over, and for a moment the two sisters stood side by side without looking at each other. The paper smelled a little of the office in the city where Derya worked, and the writing on it was small and very straight.

"Kemal Bey wants the new rent paper signed before the end of the month," Derya said. "He says he has a man waiting, somebody who wants to open a phone shop here."

"He has been saying that for eleven years."

"This time he showed me the man's number."

They went down the list together, and it was easier than Aylin had expected. The bean dish stayed, because their mother had made it every Monday for thirty years, and half the street came in for it. The rice with meat stayed, the three salads stayed, and the bread still came from Osman's oven at the corner. Derya crossed out two sweet dishes that nobody ever ordered, and Aylin did not argue, because she had never liked making them.

"Number twenty-two," Derya said, and she did not need to read it out.

"The fish soup," Aylin said.

"The fish soup." Derya put her pen down on the paper. "It has to go."

Aylin laughed once, the way people laugh when something is not funny at all. "It is the first thing on the wall, and it has been the first thing on that wall since I was nine years old."

"I know, and I also know what it costs." Derya turned a page and found a long line of numbers. "Four hours on the stove, two kinds of fish, and one of them is only in the market in winter. Onions, lemons, milk, a whole box of fresh green leaves. Mother sold a bowl for sixty, and it cost her more than eighty to make."

"That cannot be right."

"I checked it three times last night."

Outside, a car went past with its window open and music playing, and then the street was quiet again. The old menu still hung on the wall behind Aylin, behind dusty glass, with the fish soup on the first line of the first column.

"People come here for that soup," she said.

"Which people? Give me their names."

"They come from other towns for it."

"Then they can come for the bean dish," Derya said. "Aylin, we owe two months of rent, and if Kemal Bey raises it — and he will raise it — every dish on that list has to pay for itself. A dish that loses twenty on every bowl is not a dish. It is a hole in the floor."

Aylin picked up the pen and put it down again. She had washed dishes in this kitchen at fourteen, and she had stood at that stove alone on the nights when their mother's back was bad. Derya had been in the city for nine years, sending money at the New Year and calling on Sundays.

"You have been here for eleven days," Aylin said quietly.

"That is not fair."

"It is not fair to cross out her soup with a pen from your office, either."

Derya's face did not change much, but she gathered her papers into a careful pile, which was what she always did when she wanted to leave a room without shouting. "The health office needs a printed menu before we open, Kemal Bey needs a signed paper, and the print shop needs the file on Friday. Somebody has to decide."

"Then decide with me, not instead of me."

"Fine." Derya pushed the list across the table. "Show me. Cook it, and I will sit here with a clock and write down every cost, and then we will see what one bowl really costs. If I am wrong, it goes on the menu and I will never say another word about it."

Aylin looked at the twenty-one lines they had agreed on, and then at the twenty-second line with a thin mark through it. "Thursday," she said. "The fish comes in on Thursday."

# Four Hours and Nine Bowls

The market was still dark when they got there on Thursday morning, and the men were washing the stones in front of the shops with buckets of water. Aylin walked past four fish men without stopping and then stopped at the fifth, where a young man in a blue coat was laying out fish in rows on ice.

"Your father is not here?" she asked.

"My father is at home, because his chest is bad this winter." The young man dried his hands on his coat. "You are Nurhan Hanım's daughter."

"Both of us are," Derya said.

He looked at them for a second longer than he needed to, and then he began to choose the fish himself, without being asked. He put the small ones to one side and the bigger ones on the paper, and he did not weigh them the way he weighed things for other customers. When Derya took out her money, he named a price so low that she said the number back to him as a question.

"That is the price," he said, and he turned away to serve somebody else.

In the kitchen Aylin tied her hair back and began. Derya sat on a chair by the door with her phone and her notebook, and she wrote everything down. She noted the time the water went on, the weight of the onions, the number of lemons, and the moment when the first white cloud came off the pot. She had not watched anybody cook properly since she was a girl, and she was surprised by how much of the work was simply waiting.

Her sister worked without looking at any paper. Aylin's hands knew the order of things, and she moved the way their mother had moved, taking two steps and turning at the corner of the table. It was as if the kitchen were a room she could cross with her eyes closed.

"Four hours," Aylin said at last. "Taste it."

Derya tasted it, then sat with the spoon in her hand and said nothing for a long moment. In the end she looked down at her notebook so that her sister could not see her face.

Aylin stood over her with the towel still in her hand. "Say it."

"It is the best thing I have eaten in nine years." Derya put the spoon down on the table. "And the pot holds nine bowls, and one bowl cost us eighty-four. That is with the fish at a price the boy gave us as a gift because of Mother."

"Then we raise the price."

"To a hundred, in this town?" Derya shook her head slowly. "Aylin, I want it to work, and I have wanted it to work since I got on the bus. But wanting is not a number."

The bell above the door rang and Kemal Bey came in without waiting to be asked. He was a heavy man who always looked slightly sorry about everything he said, which somehow made it worse. He walked around the tables, touched the glass over the old menu, and told them that the rent would go up by a quarter. He had held the shop for them out of respect for their mother, he said, but he needed the paper signed by Monday.

"And I need to see the menu," he added at the door. "The bank asks for these things."

When he had gone, the kitchen smelled of fish soup and nothing else.

"Monday," Derya said.

Aylin turned off the last ring on the stove. "She would never have taken it off that wall. Not for Kemal Bey, not for the bank, and not for you."

"You keep telling me what she would have done." Derya stood up and closed her notebook. "So show me. Show me one place where Mother wrote that this soup had to stay on that wall, and I will sign anything you want."

"There is nothing to show, because I was here and I know."

"Then look," Derya said, "because I am going to look."

# Her Own Handwriting

The office behind the kitchen was a very small room, with a desk, a chair and eleven years of paper. They started at eight in the evening and they were still there at midnight, sitting on the floor with piles around them, because their mother had never thrown away a single sheet.

Derya was looking for the old rent paper. She found bills from before she was born and a card from a customer who had moved to Germany. Then she found a photograph of the four of them outside the restaurant on the day it opened, with their father holding a paint tin and looking into the sun. She put it on the desk without a word, and Aylin held it for a long time.

It was Aylin who found the box of old menus.

They were folded flat in piles, and each pile was tied with string and marked with a year. Aylin opened the pile marked 2013, and there it was: fish soup, first line, first column, sixty a bowl. She opened 2016, and it was there too. Then she opened the last pile, the one from five years ago, which matched the menus still lying by the door, and she read it twice from top to bottom.

There was no fish soup on it.

"That is not possible," she said.

Derya took the paper from her and checked both sides. "She took it off herself."

"She could not have. I would have seen it, because I was standing right there when the boy brought them from the print shop." Aylin's voice went up and then stopped. "I never read it. Why would I read it, when I knew every line?"

They sat on the floor of the little room with the menu between them, and for a while nobody said anything.

"Then what has she been cooking on Thursdays?" Derya said slowly.

The account notebooks were on the bottom shelf, one for each year, all in the same small round handwriting. Derya pulled out the last one and turned the pages, and after a minute her finger stopped. Every Thursday, near the bottom of the page, their mother had written the same three words more than two hundred times.

Fish soup — two bowls — 0.

"Zero," Derya said. "For five years, every Thursday, two bowls and no money."

Aylin took the notebook. She thought about all the Thursday mornings when her mother had sent her out for onions and cheese and bread. The lists were always too long for one bag, and the jobs could easily have waited until the afternoon.

"Hüseyin," she said.

"The fish man's father?"

"His name is Hüseyin Amca, and his wife was Mine Hanım." Aylin closed the notebook and held it against her chest. "The winter after Father died we had nothing at all. You were four. Mother kept the door open because she was too proud to close it, and there were days when there was no fish in this kitchen."

"And he brought it?"

"He came every morning for a whole season and left a box on the step, and he would not take money. When Mother tried to pay him in the spring, he shouted at her in the street."

"I do not remember any of that."

"You were four," Aylin said again, "and she never told it as a story. She just cooked."

Derya looked at the row of notebooks on the shelf, at all those years of small round numbers. "So she took it off the menu because it was losing money, and then she went on making it anyway, once a week, for two people, out of her own pocket."

"And I have spent nine days fighting you for a line that has not existed since 2019."

"And I have spent nine days trying to cut something I did not understand." Derya pressed her hands against her eyes. "Mine Hanım died last spring. I saw it in the paper in the city, and I did not come."

Aylin stood up, and her knees hurt from the floor. "Get your coat."

"It is after midnight."

"He is old and he does not sleep," Aylin said. "Tomorrow is Friday, the print shop wants the file, and I do not want to decide this in that room again with a pen."

# The Line at the Bottom

Hüseyin Amca's house was two streets from the sea, and the light was still on in the front room. He came to the door in a thick brown coat, holding the door with one hand, and when he saw the two of them standing there together he understood something before anybody spoke.

"Both of you," he said. "Come in, come in. There is no fire in the kitchen, but this room is warm."

They sat on his old chairs while he made tea slowly and would not let them help. He asked about the funeral, about Derya's work in the city and about the restaurant, and his hands shook a little when he poured. On top of the television there was a photograph of his wife in a blue scarf, and next to it a smaller one of Nurhan and Mine laughing in front of the restaurant door.

"You did not come on Thursday," he said at last. "And for five weeks I have not come either. I did not want to knock on that door and ask."

Aylin put her cup down. "You should have knocked."

"A man does not knock on a house where there has been a funeral and ask for soup."

"It was never soup," Derya said. "It was two hundred Thursdays."

The old man looked at the window for a while. "She was not paying me back, and you should understand that," he said. "I told her forty years ago that there was no debt. She said fine, there is no debt, come on Thursday. That is how your mother won arguments."

Derya laughed, and then she was crying a little, and then she took her notebook out of her bag and put it on her knees, because that was what she did when her hands needed something to do.

"Nine bowls," she said. "The pot holds nine."

Aylin turned to her. "What?"

"Thursday only. Nine bowls, and when they are gone they are gone. We charge a hundred and ten, we say clearly why, and we say that it is only on Thursday." Derya was writing quickly now. "People always want the thing they cannot have on Wednesday. Nine bowls at a hundred and ten covers the fish and the hours and leaves a little over. Thursday becomes the busiest day of the week, and the busiest day carries the quiet ones. Seven bowls for the customers, and two bowls for this house, for as long as this house wants them."

"And the price on the menu?" Aylin asked.

"There is no price on the menu, because it is not on the menu, and it has not been for five years." Derya looked up. "We put one line at the bottom, under everything else. Thursday soup — ask us."

Hüseyin Amca said nothing, but he got up and turned on the second lamp, as if the room had suddenly needed more light.

They signed Kemal Bey's paper on Monday morning, both names, one under the other. The menus came from the print shop on Wednesday, four hundred of them in a heavy box, and Aylin carried it in and cut the string with a kitchen knife. Twenty-one dishes stood in two clean columns, exactly as Derya's list had said, and at the bottom, in slightly larger letters, there was one line by itself.

The restaurant opened on Thursday. Aylin was at the market before it was light, and the young man in the blue coat had already put her fish aside, but this time she made him take the proper price. The pot went on at seven. By one o'clock the room was full, the bean dish was almost finished, and seven bowls of soup had gone out to seven tables. Two people had already asked whether there would be any left next week.

At half past one the door opened and Hüseyin Amca came in slowly, holding his son's arm. Derya took his coat and put him at the small table by the window, where Mine Hanım had always sat.

Aylin came out of the kitchen with the eighth bowl in her hands and set it down in front of him herself.
