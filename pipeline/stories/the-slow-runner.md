# Fifty-Five Years Old

Mehmet put on his running shoes on the kitchen chair. His wife watched him from the table.

"You are fifty-five years old," she said. "Why do you want to run in a race with young people?"

"Because I want to," Mehmet said. "I ran when I was young. I stopped for thirty years. Now I want to start again."

"The city race is ten kilometers," she said. "That is a long way."

"I know how long it is." He stood up and moved his leg up and down. "I am going to finish it. Maybe not fast. But I am going to finish."

His wife shook her head, but she smiled a little too.

Mehmet worked at a small shop that sold shoes and bags. Every morning, before the shop opened, he put on his running clothes and went out to the park near his house. At first, he ran for only five minutes. Then he had to stop and walk.

"This is harder than I remember," he said to himself. He was under a tree, breathing hard.

A young man ran past him, fast and easy, like it was nothing.

Mehmet watched him go. He remembered being like that, many years ago, when he was in school. He used to win small races in his town. Then he grew up, got a job, and stopped running. Life became busy, and running became a memory.

His son, Tolga, came to visit one Sunday. Tolga was twenty-five and played in a small football team on weekends.

"You are training for a race?" Tolga asked, looking at his father's running shoes by the door.

"Yes. The city race, next month."

Tolga laughed, but not in a mean way. "That is great, Dad. Can I train with you on some days?"

"You run faster than me," Mehmet said. "You will not like it."

"I will run slow," Tolga said. "I want to help."

The next morning, Tolga came to the park at six o'clock. He ran next to his father, at the same slow speed.

"Breathe with your steps," Tolga told him. "In for two steps, out for two steps."

Mehmet tried it. It felt strange at first, but after a few minutes, his breathing became easier.

"How do you know this?" Mehmet asked.

"My coach taught us," Tolga said. "It works for everyone, not just football players."

They ran together three times a week after that. Slowly, Mehmet's five minutes became ten minutes, then twenty. His legs hurt every night, but he did not stop.

One evening, his wife found him sitting on the floor. He was holding his knee with his hand.

"Does it hurt too much?" she asked, worried.

"It hurts," Mehmet said. "But I am not going to stop just because it hurts. I stopped running once already. I am not doing that again."

# Rain Before the Race

Two weeks before the race, it rained almost every day. Mehmet still went to the park, wearing an old jacket. Tolga came with him when he was free, but he had his own job now. He did not always have the time.

One morning, alone in the wet park, Mehmet fell. His foot hit a wet stone, and he went down hard on his hands and knees.

He sat on the ground for a moment, checking his hands. They were scraped and dirty, but nothing was broken.

"Old man," he said to himself, half laughing, half angry. "Watch where you are running."

He stood up slowly and finished his run anyway, walking the last part.

That night, his wife cleaned the small cuts on his hands. "Maybe this race is not a good idea," she said quietly.

"It is a good idea," Mehmet said. "I fell one time. That does not mean I stop."

Three days before the race, Mehmet ran his longest distance yet: eight kilometers. It took him a long time, much longer than a young runner needs. But he finished, and he did not walk once.

Tolga met him at the end, holding two bottles of water.

"Eight kilometers," Tolga said. "You are ready, Dad."

"The race is ten kilometers," Mehmet said. "I only ran eight."

"You have two more days," Tolga said. "Rest now. Do not run again before the race. Your legs must rest."

Mehmet listened to his son's advice, even though it was hard to sit still for two days. He watched other people jog past his window and felt his legs move a little under the table, wanting to go.

On the morning of the race, the sky was clear and cold. Hundreds of runners gathered near the old stadium. They moved their arms and legs and talked to each other. Mehmet felt small among all the young bodies around him.

His wife and Tolga stood near the starting line with a small sign that said his name.

"You do not have to win," his wife said. "Just finish. That is enough for us."

"I know," Mehmet said. His hands were shaking a little, but not from cold.

A loud horn sounded, and the runners started to move forward. They were slow at first. Then they went faster, and the big crowd moved out along the road.

Mehmet found his speed quickly, the same slow, steady speed he practiced with Tolga. He did not try to run with the fast group at the front. He ran in his own way.

# Ten Kilometers

By the fifth kilometer, Mehmet's legs already felt heavy. The morning sun was warmer now, and sweat ran down his face. Many runners passed him, but he did not care about them. He only cared about the next step, and the one after that.

At the sixth kilometer, he saw a water table beside the road. He drank quickly and kept moving. His breathing was loud, but he remembered Tolga's words: in for two steps, out for two steps. He used them like a small song inside his head.

Near the seventh kilometer, his right knee started to hurt, the same knee he hit on the wet stone weeks before. He slowed down a little, but he did not stop.

"Almost there," he told himself. "Almost there."

A woman running beside him, maybe forty years old, looked over and smiled. "First race?" she asked, a little out of breath.

"First race in thirty years," Mehmet said.

"That is a good story," she said, and then she ran ahead of him, disappearing into the crowd.

At the eighth kilometer, Mehmet's legs wanted to stop completely. He thought about walking, just for a minute. But he remembered falling in the park, and getting up anyway. He remembered his wife's worried face, and his son's slow, patient running beside him for weeks.

He kept his legs moving, even when they moved slowly.

The ninth kilometer felt like the longest part of his whole life. His knee hurt with every step now, and it did not stop. But the road ahead started to look familiar. This was near the old stadium, near the finish.

He heard noise ahead, people shouting and hitting their hands together. The last few hundred meters appeared in front of him, with a big banner over the road.

Mehmet found something inside himself, some small extra strength he did not know he had. He moved his legs a little faster, and he did not think about his knee.

He saw his wife and Tolga near the finish line, both jumping and waving.

"Go, Dad! Go!" Tolga shouted.

Mehmet crossed the finish line with his arms up in the air. He was not fast and not first, but he was on his feet, breathing, and happy.

A worker put a medal around his neck. It was heavier than he expected. He looked at it for a long moment, then looked up at his family.

"I finished," he said, his voice shaking.

"You finished," his wife said, hugging him even though he was covered in sweat.

Tolga picked up his father's arm and put it around his own shoulder, helping him walk. "Ten kilometers, Dad. You did it."

"My knee hurts," Mehmet said, laughing and wincing at the same time.

"Then we rest tomorrow," Tolga said. "And next month, we start training again. For the next race."

Mehmet looked at his son, then at the medal in his hand. Thirty years without running, and now this. He was fifty-five years old, and he was going to run again.

"Next month," he agreed. "But this time, you run at my speed from the start."

Tolga laughed. "Deal, Dad. Deal."
