# The Metal Door

The key turned halfway and stopped, exactly as it had always done. Derya lifted the metal door with her shoulder, pushed hard against the frame, and the lock finally gave way.

Inside, the shop smelled of oil and cold dust. She found the light switch by memory, and two of the three bulbs came on.

Nothing had moved in eleven years. Her father's tools still hung above the long table in their old order, from the smallest to the largest, each one in its own painted circle on the wall. An old radio sat in the middle of the table with its back removed, and its wires waited for a hand that was not coming.

Derya put her bag down and checked her phone. It was Monday, the buyer wanted to sign the papers on Friday, and the bank in the city wanted its money before the end of the month. She had counted the days twice on the bus. The numbers were not kind, but they worked. If the shop sold on Friday, her little print business would survive the summer.

"So you came back," a voice said behind her.

Nesrin stood in the doorway with a cloth bag in her hand. She was smaller than Derya remembered, and her hair was completely white now, but her eyes had not changed. She had been selling bread across the street since before Derya was born.

"Hello, Nesrin Abla."

"Six weeks. You buried your father and you left after two days." There was no anger in it; she said it the way other people report the weather. She put the bag on the table, and the smell of warm bread filled the little room. "Eat something. You look like a coat hanging on a chair."

"I had work in the city. I couldn't stay longer."

"Everybody has work in the city." Nesrin looked slowly around the shop, the way people look at the face of someone in hospital. "And now you are selling it."

"I have to sell it. The shop has been closed since March and it brings in nothing at all." Derya heard how sharp her own voice sounded, and she tried again more gently. "My business is in trouble. This is the only thing I have."

"Vural is buying?"

"He made an offer, and it's a fair price."

Nesrin's mouth moved, but no words came out. She took a piece of bread from the bag, pressed it into Derya's hand, and closed her fingers around it like a mother closing a child's fist around a coin. Then she crossed the street again without saying goodbye.

Derya ate the bread standing up, because there was still only one chair in the shop and it had been her father's.

She spent the afternoon making lists on the back of an old paper bag. The tools could go to a dealer in the next town, the table was too heavy to move, and the papers in the desk would have to be sorted before Friday. Work helped. While her hands were busy, she did not have to think about the man who had sat in that chair for thirty-five years.

At five o'clock she opened the door of the back room, which she remembered as an empty space with a broken window.

It was full. Machines stood on the floor and on shelves in three lines: televisions, fans, two water heaters, a sewing machine, a child's electric car, radios, clocks, an iron with a burnt mark on its side. Every single item had a small paper tag tied to it with white string. Each tag carried a name, a date, and a short note in her father's careful handwriting.

Derya counted them twice. Forty-one machines. Some of the tags were three years old.

"He never threw anything away."

A young man was standing in the shop with a key in his hand. He was about twenty-two, with paint on his shoes and an honest, worried face.

"I'm Emre. Nesrin Abla is my aunt. I worked here with your father for two years, and I come twice a week to open the windows." He said it quickly, as if he expected to be sent away. "I have a key. He gave it to me."

"Who are all these people?" Derya asked, pointing at the tags.

"Customers. They bring things, and he fixes them."

"And do they pay?"

Emre looked at the floor. "Some of them," he said.

She turned back to the desk and pulled the wide drawer under the top. It was locked, and the key was not on her father's ring.

"The small key is inside the radio," Emre said. "He always kept it there."

# The Blue Notebook

The notebook had a blue cover, soft at the corners, and a rubber band around it to hold the loose pages in.

Derya sat in her father's chair and opened it under the lamp while Emre made tea on the little cooker in the corner. Each page had a name at the top, and under the name there was a column of dates and a column of numbers. The handwriting was small and neat, the way it always was when he was being serious.

Nesrin. Hakan. Fatma. Selim. İbrahim. Nineteen names, and every one of them lived or worked on this street.

At first she thought it was a list of customers who owed him money, and something in her chest lifted. Then she saw the words at the top of the first page, written in her father's hand: *Money taken. To be returned.*

She read it four times before she understood.

Her father had not been lending. He had been borrowing. He had borrowed from the woman who sold bread, from the man who cut hair on the corner, from the taxi driver, from the family who sold cloth two doors down. Some sums were small, like two hundred lira in a cold week in February. Some were not small at all.

Derya took the paper bag and added the numbers three times, because she did not trust her own hands. The total came to about twenty-six thousand.

"Emre," she said quietly. "Did you know about this?"

He put the tea glass down in front of her and did not answer immediately. "I knew he was careful with money," he said at last. "He always said the shop was slow, but it would get better in the spring."

"Twenty-six thousand." She turned the notebook so he could see it. "Vural is paying forty-five for the shop. The bank takes what I owe. If I pay these people back, I keep almost nothing."

"Nobody is asking you to pay."

"That isn't how debts work." She heard her father's voice in her own mouth and hated it. "You take money, you return it. He taught me that himself, and then he went and did this to half the street."

She did not sleep well in the flat above the shop. The bed still had his blanket on it, and the ceiling still had the same brown mark near the window. At six in the morning she gave up, washed her face, put the notebook in her bag, and crossed the road to the bread shop.

Nesrin was pulling trays out of the oven. Flour hung in the air like smoke.

"Sit," she said, without looking up.

Derya stayed standing and put the open notebook on the counter, at the page with Nesrin's name. "Four thousand three hundred lira," she said. "Over eleven years. Is this true?"

Nesrin looked at the page for a long moment. She wiped her hands slowly on her apron.

"It's true that he wrote it down," she said.

"Then I'll pay you on Friday, when the sale is finished. It may take a few weeks for the bank to move the money, but you will get all of it."

"No."

"Nesrin Abla—"

"I said no, and I am too old to say it twice." The older woman finally raised her eyes, and they were hard and wet at the same time. "That money was never a loan. It was bread money going back where bread money comes from. I will not take it from his daughter, and I will not take it from his grave."

"He wrote it in a book," Derya said. "He wrote every lira. He thought it was a loan."

"Your father thought many things." Nesrin pushed a hot loaf across the counter and turned back to her oven, and her shoulders said clearly that the conversation was finished.

Derya walked back across the street with the bread burning her hands. She was still standing outside the shop when a grey car stopped at the corner and Vural got out.

He was a heavy, comfortable man of sixty, in a clean shirt, with the easy smile of someone who is never in a hurry. He owned the hardware shop, two flats, and a piece of land by the road.

"Derya Hanım. Everything ready for Friday?"

"Everything is ready."

"Good, good." He looked past her into the dark shop and nodded slowly, as if he were agreeing with something she had not said. "Forty-five thousand is a good price, considering everything. We'll talk about the details on Friday."

"What details?"

Vural was already opening his car door. "Friday," he said pleasantly.

# What the Dates Meant

By Wednesday evening she had visited eleven of the nineteen names, and eleven people had told her no.

Hakan, who cut hair on the corner, laughed at her and offered her a coffee instead. Fatma from the cloth shop got angry and said Derya was insulting her family. Selim the taxi driver drove her to the cemetery for free, waited an hour with the engine off, and refused to discuss the six hundred lira on his page at all.

"Why?" Derya asked him on the way back. "None of you are rich. Explain it to me, because I don't understand."

Selim watched the road. "Ask him," he said, and it was a moment before she realised he meant the shop and not her father.

She went back and asked the shop.

The repair book was in the second drawer, thick and grey, one line for every job. She carried it to the table, opened the blue notebook beside it, and began to compare the dates.

February the ninth, three years ago: two hundred lira taken from Nesrin. In the repair book, the same week, a water heater repaired for a family with a new baby, and in the money column one word — *later*. April, five years ago: a thousand lira taken from Hakan. That month her father had repaired eight machines and been paid for two.

Derya worked until midnight, and the pattern rose out of the pages like a photograph coming clear in water.

He had not been careless. He had not been drinking his money away or lending it to bad men, which was the story she had told herself for eleven years, because it was easier than the truth. Her father had simply refused to say no. When a fan came in from a house with no money, he fixed the fan. When the month ended and the electricity had to be paid, he crossed the street to the bread shop and borrowed enough to keep the door open, and he wrote the amount in a blue notebook so that nobody could ever say he had forgotten.

The whole street had been feeding a man who was quietly working for them for nothing. They had all known. She had been the only person who did not know.

She was closing the notebook when she saw the last page.

It was a page with no name at the top, only a date nineteen years old and three words in her father's writing: *For Derya. School.*

One thousand two hundred lira, taken from Nesrin, in the September when Derya went to the city to study.

She remembered that money perfectly. She remembered him counting it out on this table and saying that he had sold an old machine to a dealer, and that she should not think about it again. She remembered believing him, because at eighteen you believe what is comfortable.

Derya put her head down on the table, next to the radio with its back off, and stayed there for a long time.

On Friday morning Vural came at ten with a lawyer, a folder, and his easy smile. He sat in her father's chair without asking, spread the sale papers on the table, and then took out a second, older sheet of paper.

"Before we sign, we should settle one small thing," he said. "Your father borrowed twelve thousand from me six years ago, when he changed the front of the shop. This is the paper. That is his signature — you know it better than I do."

She knew it. It was his signature.

"With the interest we agreed, the amount today is nineteen thousand," Vural went on. "I'm not an unreasonable man. We take it off the price, I pay you twenty-six, and we all go home happy."

Derya looked at the notebook, which was still lying open beside the sale papers. Vural's page was there, with his name at the top in the same careful hand.

"His book says twelve thousand," she said.

"His book doesn't include interest."

"His book includes everything." She kept her voice flat, because if it moved at all it would break. "Nineteen thousand is not what he wrote."

Vural spread his hands, patient and warm and completely immovable. "Then bring me something that says different," he said. "The bank wants its money at the end of the month, doesn't it? I can wait. I'm not the one who is in a hurry."

# Forty-One Machines

She searched the shop all night, and Emre searched with her.

They emptied the desk, the shelves, the two metal boxes under the table, and a cardboard box of old calendars. They found receipts for electricity going back fourteen years, three photographs of Derya as a child, a hundred small screws in a jar, and nothing else.

At four in the morning Emre stopped in front of the radio.

"He put the drawer key in here," he said slowly. "He always said the safest place in a repair shop is inside something broken, because nobody steals a machine that doesn't work."

Derya lifted the radio down and turned it over.

Inside, folded into a tight square behind the speaker, there was a brown envelope. It held forty-eight small slips of paper, each one for two hundred and fifty lira, each one dated on the first day of a month, and each one signed at the bottom by Vural himself.

Forty-eight months. Twelve thousand lira. Paid back in full, one small payment at a time, over four years, while her father borrowed bread money across the street to do it.

Vural arrived at ten o'clock, exactly on time.

Derya let him sit down. She let him open his folder and put the old signed paper on the table again, and only then did she place the envelope in front of him and pour the slips out in a small paper river.

He looked at them for perhaps ten seconds. His face did not change very much, but his hand came off the folder.

"These prove nothing," he said. "People pay in parts. There could be other debts."

"Then we can ask the lawyer to write to the tax office and check the whole thing properly," Derya said. "It takes a few weeks, and I am willing to wait now. Or you can pay me forty-five thousand today, as you offered, and we will both stop talking about my father."

The lawyer coughed and studied the ceiling with great interest.

Vural signed. He signed quickly, the way men sign when they want to be somewhere else, and he shook her hand at the door because he was a man who always shook hands. The money would be in her account on Tuesday, which meant the bank in the city would be paid, which meant she still had a business in July.

She should have felt like a winner. She felt like someone who had won an argument with a dead man.

On Saturday she and Emre opened the metal door wide and carried all forty-one machines out onto the pavement, one by one, each with its paper tag still tied on with white string.

The street came for them slowly. Fatma took her sewing machine and would not look at Derya's face. Hakan carried away a fan under each arm. A woman Derya did not recognise took the child's electric car and cried, and then apologised for crying. Twelve machines were still broken, and Emre wrote those names in the grey book and said he would finish them himself in his uncle's garage.

Nesrin came last, for a clock that had been waiting for nineteen months.

"Four thousand three hundred," Derya said. "And one thousand two hundred more, from September, nineteen years ago. I saw the page."

"I know what page you saw."

"Let me pay you."

"No." Nesrin held the clock against her chest like a cat. "Listen to me, girl. Your father carried this street for thirty years and never once asked to be thanked for it. We carried him for eleven, and we never asked either. That is not a debt. That is just what people are." She put one dry hand on Derya's cheek. "You want to pay me? Come at New Year. Sit down. Eat something."

Derya took the bus back to the city on Sunday morning with the tools in two bags and the blue notebook in her coat pocket.

Somewhere after the second town she took it out and turned to the last empty page. She wrote the date, and under the date she wrote nineteen names, in the order they appeared in her father's careful hand, and she did not write any numbers beside them at all.

Then she wrote her own name at the bottom, and under it one word: *everything*.
