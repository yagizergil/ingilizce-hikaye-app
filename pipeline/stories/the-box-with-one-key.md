# Nine Hundred and Forty

Deniz put the metal box on the table and opened it in front of the whole class.

"Count it with me," she said. "Everybody watch."

She said this every Monday, and every Monday nobody watched. Poyraz was drawing a car on his hand. Two girls in the back row were laughing about a video. Only Mr Kaya, at the front, looked up from his papers.

"Nine hundred and forty," Deniz said. She wrote the number in her small green notebook. "Six people still have to pay."

"On Friday," somebody said.

"Friday is the last day. The bus man wants all the money on Friday morning."

She closed the box and turned the key. It was a small flat key on a piece of string, and she wore it around her neck under her shirt. Mr Kaya gave it to her in September. The class chose her because she was good with numbers and because she never lost anything.

She put the box in the tall cupboard at the back of the room, on the middle shelf, behind the old maps. Mr Kaya locked the cupboard. Then he locked the classroom door, and everybody went home.

That was Friday afternoon, two weeks before the trip.

On Monday morning Deniz opened the box in front of the class again.

The box was empty.

For a moment nobody said anything. Deniz turned the box in her hands, as if the money can hide in the metal.

"Is this a joke?" she said. "Who did this?"

Poyraz stood up. He was tall and loud and he always spoke first.

"You have the key," he said.

"Yes. I have the key."

"Only you."

The room became very quiet. Deniz felt her face go hot. She looked at Mr Kaya, but Mr Kaya was looking at the box, and his face was grey.

"Sit down, Poyraz," he said.

"But sir, it is a simple question." Poyraz did not sit down. "One box. One key. One person."

"I said sit down."

Poyraz sat. He did not look at Deniz.

Mr Kaya took the box and looked inside it, then under it. He shook it. Nothing.

"Deniz," he said quietly. "Where was the key on Saturday and Sunday?"

"Around my neck. At home."

"Did you take it off?"

"In the shower." She heard her own voice and it sounded small. "And when I slept. It was in my school bag, in my room."

"Who was in your house?"

"My mother. My brother. He is four years old."

Somebody at the back laughed, one short ugly laugh, and stopped.

Mr Kaya put the box on his desk. "Nobody is going to say anything about this outside this room," he said. "Not one word. Do you understand?"

Twenty-four heads went up and down. Deniz knew that meant nothing. By lunch time the whole school is going to know.

She was right. At lunch, a boy from another class came up to her in the yard.

"Is it true?" he said. "Nine hundred lira?"

"Nine hundred and forty," Deniz said. Then she wanted to hit herself. It sounded like the answer of a person who counted the money in her own hand.

She sat alone on the wall and ate nothing. Her sandwich stayed in the paper.

The worst part was simple, and she knew it, and she thought about it all afternoon. Three weeks ago she told Mr Kaya she cannot pay for the trip. Her mother works in a shop and there is no father in the house and the trip costs four hundred lira. Mr Kaya said, we are going to find a way, do not worry. He said it kindly, at his desk, in a low voice.

But Poyraz sat two metres away, and Poyraz has good ears.

At four o'clock Deniz walked home the long way, past the market, past the mosque, past the shop where her mother works. She did not go in.

The trip is on Friday, she thought. Five days.

She stopped at the corner and stood there for a long time. Then she turned round and walked back to school, fast.

# The Smell of Paint

The school door was still open. A woman was washing the floor of the corridor and she looked up.

"We are closing," she said.

"I need to see my classroom. Please. It is very important."

"Which class?"

"Two years, ground floor. Mr Kaya."

The woman put her cloth in the bucket. "Not today," she said. "That corridor is closed. They painted the walls at the weekend and the floor is still wet at the far end."

Deniz stopped.

"They painted?"

"On Saturday. The men came in the morning." The woman shook her head. "Paint, paint, and now I have paint on my floor. Nobody thinks about the floor."

"Which rooms?"

"All of that corridor. Your room too."

Deniz's mouth was dry. "Did the men go inside my classroom?"

"Of course they went inside. They painted the wall behind the cupboards. They moved everything out from the wall." The woman picked up her bucket. "Go home, girl. Come tomorrow."

Deniz went home, but she did not do her homework. She sat on her bed with her green notebook and wrote three lines.

Saturday: men in the room. Cupboard moved.
Sunday: nothing.
Monday: money gone.

Then she wrote: the cupboard was locked. And under that she wrote: WAS IT?

In the morning she got to school at seven. The gate was open and the corridor smelled strongly of new paint. The classroom door was closed but not locked, and inside, Mr Kaya was at his desk with his coat still on.

He looked up. "You are early."

"Sir. On Saturday men painted this room."

"I know."

"They moved the cupboard."

Mr Kaya put down his pen. "Deniz—"

"Sir, please. Did you give them the key of the cupboard?"

He was quiet for two seconds. "Yes," he said. "I left it in the office on Friday, for the school. They must open the cupboards and move them. That is normal."

"So on Saturday the cupboard was open, and it moved, and there were men in the room."

"And the box was locked," Mr Kaya said. "That is the problem, Deniz. The box. Not the cupboard. The box was locked and it is still locked and it is empty."

She looked at the metal box on his desk. He was right. That was the wall in front of her, and she cannot climb it.

Behind her, the door opened. Poyraz came in with his bag on one shoulder. He saw Deniz and he stopped.

"Sorry," he said to Mr Kaya. "I forgot my book."

He did not look at Deniz. He went to his desk and started to move his things around very slowly, and Deniz understood that he was listening.

"Fine," she said loudly, to nobody. "Then I am going to look at the box."

She took it. Mr Kaya opened his mouth and closed it again.

Deniz sat on the floor by the window with the box in the light. It was an old thing, older than her. Grey metal, with a lid and a small lock on the front.

She turned it over. On the bottom, at one corner, the metal was open — a thin dark line, about as long as her finger.

She pushed it with her finger and the line opened a little more. The bottom of the box was not one piece. It was two pieces, and at that corner the metal was old and tired and it bent.

Deniz's heart started to go fast.

"Sir," she said. "Do you have paper? Any paper. Old paper."

Mr Kaya gave her a page from his notebook. She made it small, like a note, and put it in the box. She closed the lid. She turned the key.

Then she picked the box up and shook it. Not hard. She shook it the way a man carries a box across a room.

The paper fell out of the corner onto the floor.

Nobody in the room said anything.

Then Poyraz said, very quietly, "Do it again."

# Behind the Cupboard

They did it again. The paper fell out again. Mr Kaya did it himself, twice. Both times the small white square came out of the box and landed on the floor.

"So the money is in the cupboard," Poyraz said.

"We looked in the cupboard," Mr Kaya said.

"You looked in the cupboard. Did you look behind it?"

The cupboard was tall and heavy and it stood against the wall at the back. The new paint behind it was a slightly different white.

The three of them pulled it out from the wall, centimetre by centimetre. It made a terrible sound on the floor. Deniz got down and looked into the dark space at the back.

Nothing. Dust and an old pencil and a dead insect.

"The bottom," Poyraz said. "Under it."

The cupboard stands on four short legs. Under it there is a space, as high as a hand.

Deniz put her arm under the cupboard, into the dark, up to her shoulder. Her cheek was on the cold floor. She moved her fingers.

Paper.

She pulled her arm out. In her hand there were banknotes, dusty and soft, a lot of them, and one blue one fell and went back under the cupboard again.

Mr Kaya sat down on a chair.

They counted it on the desk, all three together. Then they counted it a second time, because the first number was wrong.

Nine hundred and forty.

"Every lira," Mr Kaya said. He put both hands on his face for a moment. Then he stood up. "The men moved the cupboard on Saturday. They took the box out, and the money came out of the corner, and the box went back on the shelf. Nobody heard a thing."

Deniz did not answer. She was looking at her hands, black with dust.

The bell went and the class came in, loud. Then the class became quiet. There was money on the teacher's desk. The cupboard stood in the middle of the room.

Poyraz did not wait for Mr Kaya. He climbed onto a chair.

"Listen to me," he said. "The box is broken. The money fell out on Saturday when the painters moved the cupboard. It was under the cupboard all the time." He looked around the room. "And I said a stupid thing yesterday. In front of everybody. So now I am saying this in front of everybody too. Deniz did not take anything."

He got down off the chair. His ears were very red.

Mr Kaya let the silence stay for a moment. Then he said, "Sit down, Poyraz." But he said it in a different voice.

At the end of the lesson Deniz gave the key back.

"No," Mr Kaya said. "Keep it. But we are going to buy a new box. You are going to choose it."

"With money from the trip?"

"With money from my pocket," he said. "Go to your lesson."

On Friday morning the bus man came and Mr Kaya paid him, and the class went to the sea for two days. Deniz sat by the window at the front. Poyraz sat behind her and talked about football for one hour and forty minutes and she let him.

Her mother paid one hundred lira for the trip. The rest came from somewhere in the school. Mr Kaya said it was normal, and Deniz decided to believe him. Some questions are good questions and some are not.

But she kept the old box. It is at home now, on the shelf in her room, with the broken corner, and she keeps her bus tickets in it.
