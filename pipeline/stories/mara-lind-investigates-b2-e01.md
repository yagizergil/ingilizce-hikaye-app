# A Message at Midnight

The message arrived at eleven minutes past midnight, which was precisely the kind of hour that Mara Lind had learned, over fifteen years of journalism, to treat with suspicion. Nobody who had something simple to say chose to say it at that time of night.

_Come to my house at six tomorrow morning, alone, and I will tell you everything about Harbour Lights. – Piet Haas_

She read it three times in the cold blue light of her phone, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her flat. The sofa had disappeared months ago under a landscape of printed contracts, planning documents and maps of the old fishing quarter covered in her own untidy handwriting. Through the window, the harbour of Port Lumen lay almost completely dark, except for the construction cranes of the Harbour Lights project. They rose above the black water like enormous metal birds whose red warning lights blinked slowly and patiently, as if they had all the time in the world and knew it.

Piet Haas was seventy-four years old, and for the past eight months he had been the most famous resident of the old quarter. He had never wanted to be famous for anything except catching more cod than his brothers. He was the last of fifty-three owners who had refused to sell their homes to the developers. His small blue house, with its tired wooden balcony and its window boxes full of stubborn red geraniums, now stood completely alone in the middle of the building site. It was surrounded by fences, mud and yellow machines that worked around it with an almost insulting politeness.

The local television channel adored him, because an old fisherman defending his birthplace against a group of glass-and-steel companies was the kind of story that wrote itself. The developers, who operated under the cheerful and carefully tested name of Harbour Lights, clearly did not adore him, although they had never said a single unkind word about him in public, which Mara found more worrying than if they had.

She had interviewed him twice, and on both occasions he had made her coffee strong enough to dissolve a teaspoon, told her long and wonderful stories about the winter storms of the seventies, and said absolutely nothing about the people who were trying to buy him out. "I am a fisherman, not a politician," he had said the second time, smiling at her notebook as though it were a slightly foolish pet. "All I want is to die in the house where I was born, and I do not think that is a story for your newspaper."

So why, after eight months of polite and immovable silence, had he suddenly decided at midnight that he wanted to tell her everything?

She typed _I will be there_, deleted it, typed _Why now?_, looked at the two words for a long time, and deleted those as well. Finally she sent the first reply, because she knew from experience that a source who was asked why too early often decided that he did not know the answer. Then she set three alarms, lay down among the documents with her coat over her shoulders, and spent the rest of the night not sleeping in a very determined way.

# The Contract on the Kitchen Table

At ten minutes to six, the old quarter was grey, wet and silent. A thin rain was hanging in the air rather than falling through it, the kind of rain that seems to wait patiently for you to walk into it so that it can take the credit for making you cold. Mara parked at the edge of the construction site and walked along the fence until she found the gap that the local children used as a shortcut to the sea. It was also the gap she had used for her last interview, when a security guard with a very small umbrella had pretended not to see her.

The blue house looked exactly as it always did, except that there was a light on in the kitchen, which was unusual for a man who liked to boast that he had not paid an electricity bill above the minimum since the year his wife died.

She knocked, waited, and knocked again more loudly, and the door, which had not been properly closed, swung slowly inwards with a long complaint from its rusty hinges, as though the house itself was reluctant to let her in.

"Piet?"

He was sitting at the kitchen table in his heavy fishing coat, as if he had been sitting there all night and had forgotten to take it off. In front of him there were two empty cups and, between them, a thick document with a blue plastic cover. Mara did not need to read the title, because she had seen the same blue cover at least a dozen times during the previous year, lying on the kitchen tables of his former neighbours. She even knew by heart the long and friendly sentence on its first page about "a brighter future for the waterfront community."

It was a Harbour Lights purchase agreement, and on the last page, in shaky blue ink that had pressed so hard into the paper that it had almost torn it, was Piet's signature.

"You signed," she said, and she was surprised by how much it hurt to say it.

"Sit down, Mara, and drink some coffee, because you look as though you need it more than I do." His voice was tired but completely steady, the voice of a man who had already finished arguing with himself and had no energy left to argue with anyone else.

"Six hours ago you sent me a message saying that you were going to tell me everything."

"I know exactly what I wrote," he said, and he looked at the document as though it belonged to a stranger who had left it on his table by mistake. "And now I am telling you, as politely as I can, that I am not able to."

Mara sat down, because years of difficult interviews had taught her that silence was frequently a sharper instrument than any question she could think of. People who had decided to say nothing often said a great deal if they were given enough quiet to say it into. She waited while the rain tapped on the window and somewhere outside a large machine started up with a deep, unhappy cough.

"They came last night, not long after I sent you that message," Piet said at last. "Two men and a woman, all very polite, all very well dressed, all very sorry to disturb me so late. They offered me a price."

"How much?"

He named the figure, and although Mara did not allow a single muscle in her face to move, she felt the number land in her stomach like a stone dropped into deep water. It was more than twice what any of his neighbours had received for houses of exactly the same size. It was the kind of number that made a journalist ask why before she had even finished hearing it.

"And you said yes."

"I said no, and I kept saying no for almost an hour, until I was tired of the sound of my own voice." He rubbed his eyes with the back of his large, scarred hand. "And then the woman took a photograph out of her bag and put it on the table, just there, next to the sugar."

"A photograph of what?"

Piet looked at her for a long time, and Mara could see him weighing something carefully behind his eyes, measuring exactly how much of the truth he could afford to give away and how much it would cost the people he loved.

"Of something that is none of your business," he said finally, without any anger. "Something private, and before you ask, nothing illegal, nothing that any judge would care about. Just something that would hurt people I love very much if it appeared one morning in a newspaper, even in a newspaper as careful as yours."

"So they blackmailed you."

"They did not threaten me, Mara, and you must not write that they did, because it is not true. They did not need to threaten anything. They simply showed me, very gently, that they knew." He pushed the blue document away from him across the table. "I am an old man, and I do not have the strength to fight people who know everything about me before I have even opened my mouth. I am sorry I called you, and I am sorry that you came out in the rain for nothing."

# What the Neighbours Did Not Say

Mara walked out of the blue house and into the rain with a feeling that she recognised immediately and liked even less than usual. It was the sense of having arrived exactly one step too late in a race that somebody else had been running for much longer than she had realised.

She drove straight to the Herald, where the newsroom was still almost empty at that hour, and found her editor, Elias Brandt, standing in front of the coffee machine with the expression of a man who hoped it might give him advice about his life.

"You look as though you have just seen a ghost," he said without turning around.

"I have seen something worse, which is a signed contract," she replied, and she told him everything, keeping her voice low even though the only other person in the room was a cleaner listening to the radio through her headphones.

Elias listened with the patient and slightly anxious expression he had worn in her presence for three years, ever since the story about the hospital director. That story had been wrong, not completely and not deliberately, but wrong in precisely the details that mattered most, and the Herald had been forced to publish a long apology on its front page. Elias had defended her in every meeting that followed, and it had very nearly cost him his job. Neither of them ever mentioned it, and neither of them had ever forgotten it for a single day.

"So what exactly do we have?" he asked when she had finished. "We have an old man who sold his house for an excellent price and who would prefer not to discuss his private life with the newspaper, which, if you think about it for a moment, is not a scandal, Mara, but a perfectly happy retirement."

"He was paid more than twice the market price, and nobody pays double for a small wooden house in the middle of a building site unless they need it very badly or very quickly."

"Or unless they want a good headline for the weekend," said Elias, and he drew the words in the air with one finger: "'Developer Treats Last Fisherman With Generosity.' I can already see the press release, and I can already see the photograph of a smiling director shaking Piet's hand."

"Then explain the photograph on the kitchen table."

Elias sighed in the way that only editors and parents of teenagers know how to sigh. "We have nothing except his word about that photograph, and he has already told you that he will not repeat it, so it does not exist. Find me something on paper, Mara, something solid that I can show to a lawyer without both of us losing our jobs this time."

On paper. Mara went back to her desk and opened the spreadsheet that she had been patiently building for eleven months. It contained every house in the old quarter, every owner, every sale, every price and every date: fifty-three rows of other people's decisions arranged in neat and silent columns. Fifty-two of those houses had been sold to Harbour Lights over a period of two years, and Piet's had just become the fifty-third.

She called every former neighbour whose number she still had, and most of them did not answer. Those who did were friendly, careful and almost identical in what they said. Yes, they had sold; yes, the price had been fair; no, nobody had pressured them in any way. They were all very happy now in the new apartments on the hill, which were warm in winter, had lifts, and were popular with their grandchildren.

Only one of them, a woman called Greta who had run the little fish café on the corner of the old quarter for thirty years, said something that did not sound as though it had been practised in front of a mirror.

"They were very well informed, those people," Greta said after such a long pause that Mara thought the line had gone dead. "That is all I am going to say. They knew about my debts before I had told anybody, even my own son. They knew which of my children needed money and how much. It was like sitting across the table from someone who had read my diary, although I have never written a diary in my life."

"Did they show you a photograph, Greta?"

There was another pause, longer and heavier than the first. "Goodbye, Mara," said Greta quietly, and this time the line really did go dead.

# The Intern and the Address Above the Laundry

At four o'clock in the afternoon, Sofi, the youngest intern in the newsroom, appeared beside Mara's desk holding her laptop against her chest as if it were a shield that might protect her from being noticed.

Sofi was twenty-two years old and had been at the Herald for only three months. In that time she had spoken very quickly and apologised constantly for things that did not need an apology. She had also proved, in Mara's private and rather competitive opinion, to be considerably better with data than anybody else in the building, including Mara herself.

"I am so sorry to interrupt you," said Sofi. "But last month you asked me to look at the companies behind Harbour Lights, and I think I have found something strange, although of course it might be nothing, so I apologise in advance if it is."

"Sofi, if you apologise one more time today, I am going to put you on the weather page for a month. Show me what you have."

Sofi opened the laptop, and on the screen there was a diagram of boxes connected by arrows that looked like a family tree drawn by somebody who was extremely angry with their family.

"Harbour Lights is not really one company at all," Sofi explained, and as she began to talk about something she understood, the apologies disappeared from her voice. "It is a group of six companies working together. Most of them are completely normal, a construction company, an architecture office and a bank, all with offices and websites and annual reports. But three of them are very small, and they were all registered in the same month, two years ago, only a few weeks before the first houses in the old quarter were sold."

She pointed at three boxes on the edge of the diagram. "Their names are different, and their directors are different people, but their registered address is exactly the same."

"Where?"

"Number fourteen, Corder Street, on the first floor, above a laundry."

Mara knew Corder Street, a narrow and slightly tired street of small shops on the edge of the Old Market. It was the kind of street that people walked through on their way to somewhere else, and exactly the kind of street where nobody would ever look for anything important.

"And what do these three tiny companies actually do?"

"On paper, they provide consulting, community research and data services," said Sofi, and she hesitated before continuing. "But one of them, Coastline Insight, received a payment from Harbour Lights every single month for two years, always described as 'neighbourhood engagement analysis,' and the payments started in the same month that the first owner sold, and they stopped two weeks ago."

Mara looked at the diagram for a very long time while Greta's voice repeated itself in her memory: _They knew about my debts before I had told anybody. It was like sitting across the table from someone who had read my diary._

"Neighbourhood engagement analysis," she said slowly. "If we translate that into normal human language, somebody was paid every month to find out everything about the people who lived in the old quarter. That way the buyers always knew exactly which button to press."

"That is what I thought too," said Sofi, and then, catching Mara's expression, she swallowed the apology that was already halfway out of her mouth and simply nodded.

# A Smashed Phone in a Rubbish Bin

Mara left the Herald a little after seven, when the rain had finally stopped and the wet streets of Port Lumen were shining under the yellow street lights as though somebody had polished them for a special occasion.

She did not go home, although she told herself several times during the drive that she was going to, and twenty minutes later she found herself parking at the end of Corder Street.

Number fourteen looked exactly as Sofi had described it, with a laundry on the ground floor whose windows were completely white with steam, and above it two windows with their blinds tightly closed. Next to the laundry there was a narrow door with three small metal company signs, so new that the screws holding them still shone. Nobody answered the bell when she pressed it, even though she pressed it twice and then, out of pure stubbornness, a third time.

She stood in a shop doorway across the street for twenty minutes, pretending to read messages on her phone. During all that time nobody went in or came out, and the only movement was the steady breathing of the laundry's steam against the glass.

She had almost decided to leave when the narrow door opened and a woman stepped out onto the pavement. She was in her forties, with a neat grey coat, short dark hair and an expensive leather bag. She walked quickly and confidently towards a dark car parked at the corner, like somebody who had never once in her life been late for anything. As she passed beneath a street light, she took her phone out of her pocket, looked at it for a moment, and then, without slowing down, dropped it into a public rubbish bin.

Then she got into the car and drove away without looking back.

Mara crossed the street and looked into the bin, where the phone was lying on top of a pile of wet newspapers. Its screen had not simply cracked, as happens when a phone falls on the pavement. It had been completely destroyed, beaten again and again by something hard and heavy until the glass looked like frozen spider webs.

She knew perfectly well that she should leave it exactly where it was, and she knew exactly what Elias would say if she told him, and exactly what a lawyer would say immediately afterwards, in a much more expensive voice.

But she also knew, with a certainty that she felt in her stomach rather than her head, that the woman in the neat grey coat was one of the three visitors who had sat at Piet Haas's kitchen table the night before. She matched his description perfectly: neat, polite, very calm and very well dressed. She was the kind of person who would apologise for disturbing you while placing a photograph next to your sugar.

Mara took a plastic bag out of her coat pocket, picked up the phone through it as carefully as if it were evidence in a television crime series, and put it inside her coat.

# The Repair Shop

The following afternoon, she walked into a repair shop in the narrow streets of the Old Market, a shop so small that she had to turn sideways to close the door behind her. A quiet young man in round glasses was bent over a desk under a bright lamp, holding a screwdriver so tiny that it looked like a toy.

"I am Mara Lind, from the Herald," she said, and she put the smashed phone on his counter, still inside its plastic bag. "I need to know what is on this phone, and I need you not to tell anyone, under any circumstances, that I was the person who brought it here."

The young man looked at the phone for a long moment, then at her, and then at the phone again, with the expression of somebody who was not judging her but was certainly thinking about it.

"Is it yours?" he asked.

Mara did not answer. In the silence that followed, her own phone vibrated in her coat pocket with a new message from a number she had never seen before, a message that contained only one word, written in capital letters.

_STOP._
