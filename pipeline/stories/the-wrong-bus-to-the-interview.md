# The 42 and the 42A

The night train left Adem on a wet platform at ten past seven, in a city he had never seen before. He had slept sitting up, with his good jacket folded across his knees so that it would stay clean. Now he shook it out and put it on. The interview was at eleven, and he had planned every hour between the platform and that room.

In the station washroom he shaved with cold water and combed his hair twice. He drank tea standing up, because the tables were wet, and he counted his money on the shelf beside the cup. There was enough for the ticket home and one meal, and nothing else. That was fine. If the day went the way he had planned it, he would not need anything else.

Outside, the air smelled of salt and hot bread. Buses stood in a long line along the station wall with their engines running, and the numbers in their windows meant nothing to him. Adem took out the letter, which he had read so often that the folds had gone soft. It invited him to an interview at eleven o'clock for the job of planning assistant at Meridian Logistics.

He had wanted this job for three weeks, since the morning his sister sent him the advertisement. It paid enough for a room in the city and a little more. If he got it, his mother would not have to work winters at the packing tables any more.

A man in a grey uniform was sweeping the edge of the platform. Adem asked him which bus went to Meridian Logistics, and said the name slowly, in case his accent hid it.

"Meridian? The big glass building on the seafront," the man said. He pointed with the handle of his brush. "Forty-two. At the end of the line, the blue one."

Adem thanked him and walked to the end of the line. He had seen that glass building on the company website many times, and he could describe the front doors from memory. It felt good to be sent somewhere he already knew, in a city where he knew nothing.

The blue bus was almost empty. He sat by the window, put his bag between his feet, and let himself breathe. It was twenty-five minutes to nine. He had more than two hours, and the seafront was only twenty minutes away.

The bus filled and emptied, filled and emptied. Adem watched the streets and tried to learn them: a market with grey awnings, a school with a yellow wall, a bridge over a river that was the colour of tea. After a while the buildings became lower and further apart. Cranes appeared above the roofs, and then the smell changed, and it was diesel and fish instead of bread.

He should have seen the sea on his left. He did not see the sea at all.

Adem took out his phone. The battery was at eight per cent, because the socket at his seat on the train had not worked, and he had spent the night unable to charge it. He opened the map and watched the small blue dot crawl across the screen. It was moving south, out along a narrow finger of land, towards a port. The seafront and the glass building were far behind him, in the other direction.

He stood up too quickly and knocked his bag over. An old woman in the next seat picked it up for him.

"Excuse me," Adem said. "Does this bus go back to the centre?"

"This one goes to the container port," she said. "Then it turns and comes back. About fifty minutes."

"There is another forty-two?"

"There is a forty-two and a forty-two A," she said, as if everybody knew this, and everybody did. "The A goes north. This one goes south."

The bus stopped, and Adem got off into a wide grey street with a fence on one side. The phone said six per cent. It said twenty-five minutes past nine.

# Six Per Cent

There was a bench, but he did not sit on it. He put his bag on the bench instead and spread the letter open on top of the bag, because his hands were not steady enough to hold paper.

He had read the letter for the first time in his mother's kitchen, and he had read it since then for pleasure, the way people read a good message twice. He had never once read the address line properly.

Meridian Logistics, North Yard, Gate 2, Sanayi Street.

Not the seafront. Not the glass building with the doors he could describe from memory. The interview was at a yard somewhere north of the city, and he had spent fifty minutes travelling south of it.

Adem looked at the fence and the gulls and the grey road, and for a moment he did not think anything at all. Then he picked up the phone and used the battery he had left, because there was no other way to know.

The map took a long time to load. Sanayi Street sat at the very top of the screen, past the ring road, past a river he had crossed once already that morning. Twenty-four kilometres. The line the map drew was long and thin, and beside it the phone printed a small unhappy number: one hour and ten minutes by car.

The screen went black. He pressed the button, and nothing came back except a picture of an empty battery.

He had ninety minutes, no phone, no map, and a city he did not know at all.

There was a taxi stand fifty metres up the road, with two cars waiting in the shade of the fence. Adem walked to the first one and leaned down to the window.

"North Yard, on Sanayi Street," he said. "How much?"

The driver looked at him, and then at his jacket, and named a price that was more than the ticket home and the meal together. Adem's mouth went dry, but he was already reaching for his money, because a job was worth more than a ticket. Then the driver held up a hand.

"I will take you, but I am telling you the truth first," he said. "The coast road is closed until noon. They are laying pipes at the bridge. Everything is going through the old road, and the old road is one lane." He turned his hand over. "An hour and forty minutes. Maybe two."

"And the buses?"

"Same road, my friend. Same road for everybody."

Adem stood up straight. Behind the fence, a line of trucks was waiting to enter the port, and their engines made the ground shake a little through his shoes. He thought about calling the company, and remembered that his phone was dead, and felt the whole morning press down on his shoulders.

Then he thought about the trucks.

"The trucks," he said. "Where do they go, when they leave here?"

The driver shrugged. "Everywhere. North, south. The port feeds the whole city."

Across the road there was a low white building with steam on its windows and a sign for bread. Adem crossed without waiting for the light. Inside, the heat hit his face, and a woman with flour on her forearms was pushing trays into an oven with a long wooden paddle.

"I am sorry," he said. "I need to get to Sanayi Street before eleven. Is there anything that goes north from here?"

She did not stop working. "Nothing that carries people," she said. "Things, yes. Half this street sends things north in the morning." She slid another tray in and looked up. "Why?"

"Because there is a man in an office there who is expecting me," Adem said, "and I got on the wrong bus."

The woman put the paddle down. She went to the back door, opened it, and shouted a name into the yard behind the bakery. Then she turned around and pointed at Adem with a floury hand.

"Fikret is loading now," she said. "He goes to the industrial zone every morning. His boy is sick today, so he is carrying everything alone, and he is in a very bad mood."

# Sacks of Flour

The yard behind the bakery smelled of dust and warm metal. A small truck stood with its back open, and a heavy man in his fifties was dragging a sack of flour along the ground because he could not lift it any higher.

"I can carry that," Adem said.

Fikret straightened up and looked at him: the jacket, the clean shoes, the bag over his shoulder. "You will ruin your clothes."

"Then I will ruin them." Adem put his bag down, took off the jacket, and folded it over the side of the truck. "How many are there?"

"Fifty-two."

They loaded fifty-two. Adem had worked three summers in his uncle's cold store, and his back remembered how to hold the weight low and turn with his feet instead of his shoulders. The flour got into his hair and into the creases of his shirt, and by the twentieth sack his arms were shaking. Fikret said nothing for a long time. Then, near the end, he began counting out loud with him, and it became easier.

At two minutes past ten they closed the doors, and Adem put his jacket back on over a shirt that was no longer white.

"Sanayi Street," Fikret said, starting the engine. "You are lucky. I stop at the plastics factory, which is three streets from your yard."

"How long?"

"If the old road behaves, fifty minutes. If it does not behave, I do not want to say."

The truck moved slowly through the port streets and joined a line of traffic that crawled along a road with houses on both sides. Adem watched the clock on the dashboard and made himself stop watching it. Beside him, Fikret ate half a loaf of bread and offered him the other half, and Adem took it, because he had eaten nothing since the train.

"May I use your phone?" Adem asked. "I will pay you for the call."

"You will not pay me anything."

The number for the company was printed at the top of the letter. A woman answered, and Adem told her his name and the time of his appointment. Then he said the part he had been dreading, which was the truth.

"I took the wrong bus this morning and went to the port," he said. "I am on the old road now. I will be about fifteen minutes late, and I am very sorry."

There was a pause, and a sound of typing. "Mrs. Demir has interviews all morning," the woman said. "I will tell her. I cannot promise anything."

"I understand. Thank you."

He gave the phone back. Fikret was smiling slightly at the road.

"You told her the wrong bus," he said. "Most people say traffic."

"It was the wrong bus."

They passed the bridge, where three men in orange were standing in a hole, and after the bridge the traffic opened up a little. Fikret drove with one hand and talked about the zone they were driving towards, because he had delivered there for eleven years.

"Your yard, the Meridian one, that is the big one with the four gates," he said. "I go past it every day. Everybody waits there. Gate two has a queue like a wedding, and gate four is empty from morning to night."

"Why?"

"Because the sign at gate four says staff only. They changed the barrier in the spring and put the wrong sign back, and nobody has taken it down since." He laughed without much humour. "I lose forty minutes there some days. Forty minutes, for a sign."

Adem said nothing, but he turned the words over the way you turn a coin in your pocket.

The truck stopped at the plastics factory at eight minutes past eleven. Adem shook Fikret's hand hard, ran three streets with his bag banging against his hip, and saw a long fence, and behind the fence a yard full of trucks.

# The Wrong Gate

The woman at the desk told him that Mrs. Demir was now in a meeting until twenty minutes to twelve, and that he could wait if he wished. Adem said that he would wait. He went to the window at the end of the corridor, put his bag against the wall, and looked down at the yard.

He was too tired to be nervous, which was almost restful. He watched, because there was nothing else to do, and because Fikret's forty minutes were still turning in his head.

There were four gates. At gate two, eleven trucks stood nose to tail along the fence, and the line reached back into the road outside. At gate three, two more waited. Gate one was for cars. Gate four stood open and completely empty, with a white sign on the barrier and a man in a hut reading a newspaper.

Adem took out a pen and wrote on the back of his letter, on the empty side. In twenty-six minutes, four trucks went in through gate two. Nothing at all went in through gate four. The road outside the fence was blocked twice by trucks that could not turn.

At twenty to twelve exactly, a door opened, and a woman in her forties looked out at him. She had reading glasses pushed up into her hair and the face of a person who has already had a long day.

"Adem Kaya," she said. "You are fifty minutes late for a thirty minute interview."

"Yes."

"Come in."

The office was small and hot. Mrs. Demir sat down, opened a thin file with his name on it, and did not offer him tea.

"Tell me about the bus," she said.

He told her. He did not decorate it and he did not apologise more than once, because a second apology would only have taken up her time. He told her about the forty-two and the forty-two A, about the address line he had never read, about the flour and the truck and the old road.

Mrs. Demir listened without writing anything down. "Half the people I interview would have gone home," she said at last. "Why did you not go home?"

"Because I could still get here," Adem said. "It was ten o'clock and the door was still open. If the door is open, you keep walking towards it."

She turned a page. "You have applied for planning assistant. This yard moves four hundred trucks a day. If I put you in that chair tomorrow morning, what is the first thing you look at?"

Adem put the letter on the desk, address side down, and turned it so that she could read his handwriting.

"Gate four," he said.

Mrs. Demir stopped moving.

"There are eleven trucks at gate two right now, and there have been eleven since I arrived. Four went in during twenty-six minutes. Gate four is open and empty, and the barrier still has the staff only sign from when it was changed in the spring." He kept his voice flat, because the numbers were better than any voice he could use. "The drivers I have spoken to say they lose forty minutes here. They do not report it, because they do not think anybody is listening."

She got up and went to the window. She stood there for a while with her arms folded, and Adem could hear a clock somewhere and his own heart.

"How many drivers have you spoken to?" she asked.

"One," he said. "He was carrying flour."

Mrs. Demir laughed once, shortly, and came back to the desk.

"I am not giving you the planning job," she said. "You have no experience of a yard this size, and I have two people who do. But we are three men short on the loading floor, and the floor is where everybody here learns the traffic." She wrote something on a card and pushed it across the desk. "Three months, then we talk again. You start on Monday at six in the morning. Do not take the forty-two."

Adem picked up the card. His hand was not steady, and he did not mind.

"There is a night train home at eight," he said. "I have the ticket."

"Then you have time to eat something," Mrs. Demir said. "There is a canteen at the end of the yard, and it is free for staff." She was already opening the next file. "Go out through gate four. Somebody should."

He walked across the yard in the middle of the afternoon, past the long line of waiting trucks, and the sun came off the metal roofs and made him squint. At gate four the man in the hut lifted the barrier for him without looking up from his newspaper.

The flour on Adem's sleeve had dried into a pale line above the elbow. He did not brush it off.
