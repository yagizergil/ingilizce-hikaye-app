# Thirty-One Papers

Mr. Vural stuck the list on the wall before the bell had finished ringing.

Derya reached it first, because she had been standing by the door since the middle of the last lesson. She ran her finger down the column of numbers and stopped at the top, where the highest mark on the whole list was forty-one.

"That has to be wrong," Kaan said behind her.

"It's yours," Derya said, "and mine is thirty-eight."

The hall filled up in less than a minute, and then it went strangely quiet. Thirty-one students had taken the science exam on Friday, and every one of them had failed. Somebody laughed near the window, the way people laugh when they are frightened.

Mr. Vural stood with his arms folded and waited for the noise to come back. He was a tall, tired man who had taught in the school for twenty-two years, and he did not enjoy being surprised in front of a crowd.

"I marked every paper twice," he said, "so before anybody asks me, no, I did not make a mistake."

"Sir, all of us?" Kaan said. "Thirty-one people can't forget everything on the same morning."

"That is exactly what thirty-one people can do," Mr. Vural said. "Perhaps now some of you will open the book at home."

He walked off towards the staff room, and the crowd broke apart slowly, but Derya stayed in front of the list until the numbers stopped meaning anything at all.

She needed an average of fifty. Not sixty, not fifty-five, just fifty, on the sheet that the school office would close on the fifteenth of May. Her form for the college in Bursa had to be posted before the twentieth, and her mother had already written the address on the envelope in her slow, careful handwriting. The envelope had been standing against the sugar jar for three weeks. If this grade stayed where it was, it would never leave the kitchen.

Derya caught Mr. Vural at the staff room door before it closed. "Sir, can I see my paper?"

"The papers stay in the office until the end of term."

"Then can I see the questions again?"

He looked at her with something that was almost patience. "You saw the questions on Friday, for ninety minutes. Study, Derya. That is the answer to every question you are going to ask me this week."

At home her mother had left the light on over the table and gone to bed early, because the bakery wanted her at five. Derya sat down with a glass of tea and did not drink it. She kept going back to Friday morning, and the more she looked at it, the stranger it became.

The first five questions had been fine, and she had answered them quickly and felt almost proud. Then she had turned the page, and question six had used a sign she had never seen in class. Question seven had asked about gases under pressure, which was Chapter Twelve, and they had stopped at Chapter Nine in March. Question nine had been three lines long, and it had contained two words she did not know.

At the time she had decided that the fault was hers. The room had been hot, her hands had been shaking, and she had told herself that she was simply forgetting things under pressure. She had written something in every space, and she had walked out of the room feeling ill.

Now she sat in the kitchen and thought about Kaan, who never forgot anything, and who had got forty-one.

There was one more thing, and it had been sitting at the back of her head for three days. At the bottom of the second page, in very small print, there had been a letter. She had noticed it while she was waiting for the clock to move, and then she had noticed nothing else for an hour.

The letter had been a B.

# The Line in the Register

The plan of topics for the year was pinned to the board outside the science room, where it had hung since September, going yellow at the corners.

Derya read it twice on Tuesday morning before the first bell: Chapters One to Nine in the second term, Chapters Ten to Fourteen next year. She took a photograph of it with her old phone, and the picture came out grey and dark, but you could still read the words.

Then she went to look for the class register, because every teacher wrote the topic of the lesson in it and signed the line. Miss Nalan kept the registers in a grey cupboard in the small office beside the library.

Nalan had been at the school for eight months, and she taught the evening group and covered lessons when other teachers were ill. She was also the person who had sat with Derya for an hour in March and helped her fill in the college form, because Derya's mother could not read small print without her glasses.

"You look like somebody who wants something," Nalan said.

"The register for 11-B. Just the science pages, two minutes."

Nalan put the book on the desk and turned it around so that they could both see it. Derya went down the column of dates with her finger, through February, March and April. Chapter Eight, Chapter Eight, test, Chapter Nine, Chapter Nine again. Then, on the last line before the exam, in Mr. Vural's small handwriting: Going over Chapters 1 to 9.

"Nothing after nine," Derya said. "Not one lesson."

"That's normal, because you finish where you finish."

"Miss, half of the exam came from Chapter Twelve."

Nalan's hand stopped on the page, and it was a very small thing, which Derya only saw because she was already looking. "You're sure?" Nalan asked.

"Question seven was gases under pressure, and we have never done gases under pressure."

"Then you should talk to Mr. Vural, not to me." Nalan closed the register, and her voice had changed, though her face had not. "He wrote the paper, and I only teach the evening group."

Mr. Vural was in the science room at lunchtime, marking something else and eating nothing. When Derya explained, he listened with his pen still in his hand, which she decided was better than being sent away.

"You are telling me that I set questions from a chapter I never taught you," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Come here, then."

He opened his file, took out a paper and put it flat on the desk in front of her, and across the top it said: Science, 11-B, Second Term, Form A. Derya read the questions one by one, and her face went hot.

Question six was about metals, which they had done in February, and question seven was a simple sum from Chapter Five. Question nine was one line long, and she could have answered it in her sleep.

"Well?" he said.

"This isn't the paper we had."

"Derya."

"Sir, I promise you, I have never seen these questions in my life."

Mr. Vural put the paper back in the file and closed it, and the patience went out of his face like water out of a sink. "Thirty-one people failed, and now they need a story. Your friends will love this one. Go to your lesson."

She stood in the hall afterwards with her ears burning, and she knew that he had not been lying, because nobody lies while handing you the proof. The paper in his file was fair, and the paper on her desk on Friday had not been. So the two papers were not the same paper, and somebody had put one of them into thirty-one pairs of hands.

Form A was in the file, and she had seen a B at the bottom of page two.

Derya walked past the library and the small office, and stopped in front of the copy room door.

# The Paper Bin

The copy room was locked at lunchtime, but the bin was not in the copy room.

It stood in the yard behind the building, next to the kitchen door, and it was a tall plastic box for waste paper. The council truck emptied it on the first Monday of the month, and Derya checked the date on her phone. The truck had come six days before the exam, and it would not come again until next Monday.

She waited until the yard was empty, and then she climbed up on a wooden box and looked in.

It was almost full. She pulled out old letters, torn posters and grey copies of a map of Europe, and she piled them on the ground beside her. Her hands got black. Somebody's lunch had been thrown in on top of everything, and the smell followed her home for two days.

Near the bottom, under a heavy pile of forms, she found the exam.

There were about fifteen copies, crushed together, the kind of copies a machine throws out when it goes wrong. The cover page said Form A, and she turned it over with her thumb. Page two was Chapter Twelve, and so was page three. At the bottom of each page, in tiny letters, it said: Evening Group, Form B.

Derya sat down on the box in the middle of the mess, and she understood the whole thing in about four seconds.

Two exams had been copied on the same morning, and the machine had stuck, as it always stuck. Somebody in a hurry had pulled the paper out, put the two piles back in the wrong order and pressed the green button. Every student in 11-B had then received a cover page from Form A and inside pages from Form B. Mr. Vural had marked thirty-one papers at home with the Form A answer key, quickly, twice, without ever reading a single answer properly.

Nobody had noticed, because the students believed they were stupid and the teacher believed he was right.

She put four clean copies inside her folder and left the rest.

Miss Nalan was in the small office at four o'clock, with the registers piled in front of her and her coat already on. Derya shut the door, which she had never done before, and laid the papers on the desk.

"I found these behind the kitchen," she said. "Page one is ours, and pages two and three belong to the evening group."

Nalan did not pick them up. She looked at them for a long time, and then she sat down again very slowly, and Derya watched a person become smaller in a chair.

"I opened the copy room that morning," Nalan said. "It was ten past eight, the machine stuck twice, and I had a lesson at half past. Both piles were on the shelf."

"You put them back in the wrong order."

"Yes."

The room was quiet enough to hear the clock outside in the hall. Derya had been ready for an argument, for a locked door, for a teacher shouting at her, but she had not been ready for this.

"Then we tell Mrs. Aydın tomorrow," she said. "She cancels the exam, and we sit it again."

"Derya, listen to me." Nalan's hands were flat on the desk. "My job here finishes in June, and they decide about next year at the meeting on Friday. If this goes to the head teacher with my name on it, there is no next year."

"Thirty-one people failed."

"I know, so let me fix it quietly. I will go to Mr. Vural tonight and ask him to add fifteen points to everybody. Fifteen points, and all of you pass, and nobody's name goes anywhere." She looked up. "Including yours. Do you want a report on your file three weeks before your college form goes in?"

Derya picked up the crushed papers and held them against her chest, and she did not answer, because the honest answer was no.

# Two Doors

Mr. Vural said no.

Derya heard about it from Kaan, who had heard about it from his sister, and his sister cleaned the staff room in the evenings. The two teachers had argued for half an hour behind a closed door, and the young one had come out first.

Derya found Nalan the next morning by the gate, standing in the light rain without an umbrella.

"He asked me why he should add fifteen points, and I told him that the class had been unlucky," Nalan said. "He said that if he changes numbers without a reason, then his numbers mean nothing, and he is right about that." She laughed, and it was not a good laugh. "He is a difficult man, but he is not a dishonest one."

"Then there is only one door left."

"There is another one, because we can wait until July. The grade goes on the sheet, the year ends, and next term I can quietly help each of you with extra lessons."

"My sheet closes on the fifteenth," Derya said, "and that is Friday."

They stood in the rain, and for a while they both stayed still. Derya thought about the envelope against the sugar jar and about her mother's handwriting. She thought about the way Nalan had read the college form out loud in March, line by line, until Derya understood every word of it.

"You helped me, and I know what I am asking you to lose," Derya said.

"Then don't ask."

"I have to, because Kaan wants to be an engineer and he thinks he is stupid now. Melis has stopped coming to school, and there are thirty-one of us, Miss."

Nalan pushed the rain off her face with the back of her hand and said nothing at all.

"I am going to Mrs. Aydın at eight tomorrow morning," Derya said, "and I will take the papers, the register and the photograph of the year plan. I would rather stand next to you than in front of you, but I am going."

She turned and walked into the building before her voice could break.

That night she did not sleep much. At half past seven she was sitting on the bench outside the head teacher's office, with the folder on her knees. The hall was so empty that she could hear the man who cleaned the school moving chairs upstairs.

At five to eight, Nalan came around the corner in her grey coat.

She sat down next to Derya without speaking, and when the door opened she stood up first and went in first. She told the whole thing from the beginning: the paper that stuck, the two piles, the green button and the six days of silence. Derya put the crushed papers on the desk when Mrs. Aydın asked for them.

The head teacher read page two with her glasses pushed up on her forehead, and then she called the science room.

Mr. Vural arrived four minutes later. He looked at the paper, and Derya watched something break quietly in his face.

"I marked them with the key," he said. "I never read the questions on the copies, because why would I? I wrote them."

"You will read them next time," Mrs. Aydın said.

The exam was cancelled that afternoon, and the class sat a new one on Thursday, in the big hall, with the papers checked page by page at the door. Nalan received a written warning and lost the evening group, but her job was renewed in June. At the meeting, in front of everybody, Mrs. Aydın said that a teacher who reports her own mistake is worth more than one who hides it.

Kaan got eighty-eight, and Derya got sixty-four.

Mr. Vural handed her the paper himself, on the fifteenth, an hour before the office closed the sheets. He stopped at her desk on his way back to the board.

"Question nine," he said. "Your method was better than mine."

Then he went on with the lesson, because he was not a man who said two nice things in one morning.

Derya posted the envelope on the way home, and her mother had drawn a small line under the address, so that nobody could possibly get it wrong.
