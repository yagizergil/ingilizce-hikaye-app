# The Blue Box

Nesrin cut the blue box open with her house key, because the scissors were already packed. The truck was coming on Saturday morning, and this box was the last thing left in the bedroom. Inside she found old school papers, a broken watch, a bag of buttons, and three photographs of a class she had taught in a village school. She lifted the papers out one thin pile at a time and put them on the floor beside her knee.

Then she saw the envelope.

It was yellow along the edges, and her own young handwriting sat in the middle of it. Halit Aydın, 14 Fig Street, Beyşehir. There was no stamp in the corner, and the envelope had never been opened. Nesrin sat back on the floor and held it in both hands, as if it might be hot.

She had been thirty-two in the spring when she wrote that name, and she was sixty-two now. Halit had taught mathematics in the room next to hers for two years, and he used to fix the school clock for free. When his father became ill, he had to go home to Beyşehir and take over the family workshop. He asked her to come with him. She told him she needed a few weeks, and he went alone.

For thirty years she had told herself the same story about this envelope. In that story she wrote three brave lines: wait for me until the spring, I only need one winter, I will come. In that story she put the letter in her coat pocket and walked towards the post office in the rain. She always stopped the story at the corner, because that was the evening her mother fell in the kitchen. After that there were doctors, and then two years of care, and the coat went into a cupboard with the letter still inside it. She had never posted it, and she had never opened it again.

The front door opened, and her daughter came in with bread under her arm and a ball of string in her hand.

"The men will be here at eight on Saturday," Ceyda said. "Anything not in a box goes in the rubbish. That is what they told me."

"I need to go to Beyşehir on Thursday," Nesrin said.

Ceyda put the bread on the window ledge and looked at her. "Beyşehir is six hours away. Why?"

Nesrin held up the envelope so that her daughter could see the writing. She explained it in nine or ten sentences, and she heard how thin the story sounded in the empty room.

"So post it," Ceyda said. "There are stamps in my bag."

"It is thirty years late. A stamp will not fix that."

"Then throw it away, and we will finish the kitchen tonight." Ceyda pulled a length of string off the ball and cut it with her teeth. "Anne, he has probably moved. He might be married. He might be dead."

"Then I will find that out with my own eyes," Nesrin said. "I have marked ten thousand papers in red pen. I can knock on one door."

That evening she telephoned Sevim, who had taught with her in those years and who still remembered every name in the town. Sevim said that the Aydın family had a clock workshop on Fig Street, and that everybody called them the clock people. She said Halit's father had died a long time ago, and that Halit stayed on in Beyşehir. After that she had heard nothing, because nobody writes letters any more.

Nesrin walked to the shop on the corner and bought a bus ticket for Thursday morning at seven. Then she went home, packed the kitchen until midnight, and put the envelope in the side pocket of her handbag, where she kept her blood pressure pills.

# The Early Bus

The bus was warm and smelled of oranges. A young woman with a baby sat beside Nesrin, and the baby slept for four hours without moving, which felt like a small piece of luck. Outside the window the sea disappeared, and then there were hills with pale grass, and a man on a tractor who lifted one hand as the bus went past.

Nesrin did not open the envelope, because she already knew what it said. She spent the first two hours choosing her words for the doorstep instead. She would say hello, she would say her name, and she would say that a letter had waited too long in a box. If he asked her in, she would drink one glass of tea and leave before he had to invent a reason.

At the halfway stop the driver gave everybody twenty minutes for tea. Nesrin sat at a metal table outside, put her cup down, and took the envelope out of her bag. The paper had gone soft with age. She thought about the three brave lines, and she decided she wanted to read them once in the daylight, before she handed them to a stranger with grey hair.

She opened the envelope with the handle of a spoon.

There were two pages, and the handwriting was small and hard and very straight. It was not the letter she remembered. She had written that he was asking her to leave her mother in a house with a broken door. He wanted her to follow him to a town she had never seen, for a job that was all small wheels and springs. She had written that a person who really loved somebody would not ask for that. She had written that his workshop was a little world, and that she had a school and forty children and a life of her own. Near the bottom of the second page she had written one line and underlined it twice: do not write to me again.

Nesrin read the pages a second time. The tea went cold beside her elbow, and the driver began to sound his horn.

She had carried this envelope through four flats and two schools and one funeral. For thirty years she had been proud in a quiet way, because she believed she had once been brave enough to say yes and only unlucky in the year it happened. The proud story was not true. She had said no, and she had said it in the cruellest way she could find, and then she had spent thirty years rewriting it in her head, where nobody could check.

The horn sounded again. Nesrin folded the pages, put them back, and got on the bus with her bag held against her chest.

For the next two hours she looked at the seat in front of her. The letter in her bag was no longer a gift she was delivering. If she put it in his hand now, she would be giving an old man a second cut from the same knife, and she would be doing it after thirty years of silence. That was not a delivery. That was a small act of violence with a stamp missing.

She thought about getting off at the next town and going home. Then she thought about her mother, who used to say that a debt does not get smaller in the dark. Nesrin had one thing left to bring that was actually true, and it was not on paper. She could say the true thing out loud, to his face, if his face still existed. She could say that she had been frightened, and that she had blamed him for her own fear, and that the letter had lied about him.

It was raining when the bus reached Beyşehir. The station lights were on, and the wet road shone, and a boy in a plastic coat was selling umbrellas that clearly did not work.

# Fig Street

Fig Street was still there, but number 14 was not. In its place there was a bank with a cash machine, and behind the bank there was a car park where three cats sat under a van and watched her without interest.

Nesrin went into a bakery on the corner and asked the young woman there about the clock workshop. The woman was too young to know, so she called her mother from the back room, and her mother sent Nesrin to the shop that sold paint and nails, two streets down.

The man in the paint shop cleaned his hands on a cloth and thought for a while. "The clock people," he said at last. "They moved when they knocked the old row down. Twenty years ago, maybe more. He is behind the market now, in the little street with the barber and the fish shop."

"He?" Nesrin said. "Halit Aydın?"

"Halit Bey, yes. He put a new battery in my son's watch in the summer."

Nesrin thanked him twice and walked to the market with her heart going too fast for her legs. The workshop was in the middle of a short street, between a shop with dried fish in the window and a place that cut hair. The door was locked. There was a card inside the glass: open nine to six, closed on Sunday. It was almost seven o'clock, and the rain had started again.

She stood with her face close to the window and looked in. There was a wooden bench under a lamp, with a cloth on it and small tools laid in a row. On the wall behind it there were perhaps thirty clocks, and every one of them showed a different time. It looked like a room where thirty years had been kept in careful order, in case somebody came back for them.

Nesrin took a room for one night above a restaurant near the station. She telephoned Ceyda and said that everything was fine, that she would be home on Friday evening, and that the men could have the kitchen at eight on Saturday.

Then she went out again and bought paper and a new envelope from a shop that sold pens and school books. She sat in a tea house with the paper flat in front of her and wrote three lines. She read them, and she crossed them out. She wrote two more lines about how much she had thought of him, and she crossed those out as well.

She had spent her whole life on paper. Paper was where she was clever, and paper was where she hid; that was exactly how a frightened woman of thirty-two had managed to sound so certain and so hard. If she wrote a beautiful letter tonight, she would post it in the morning and go home, and she would be safe again for another thirty years.

Nesrin tore the page into four pieces, put them in her pocket, and paid for her tea.

# The Clock She Never Collected

At nine in the morning the door of the workshop stood open, and a small boy was driving a toy car along the step. Inside, an old man in a grey pullover sat at the bench with a lamp bent low over his hands. He was heavier than she remembered, and his hair had almost gone, but he held his shoulders in the same way.

"Good morning," Nesrin said. "I am looking for Halit Aydın."

He looked up over his glasses, and for three or four seconds he was polite and blank. Then something moved in his face, and he put down the small tool he was holding very carefully, the way people put down things they might drop.

"Nesrin Hanım," he said.

"I came on the bus yesterday," she said. "From Aksu. I have something for you, and I have to say something as well, and I would like to say it before I lose my courage."

He stood up and brought her a chair, and he told the boy to go and find his grandmother in the market. Then he sat down again, and he waited, and he did not fill the silence with easy words. She had forgotten that about him.

Nesrin took the yellow envelope out of her bag and put it on the cloth between the tools. She told him that she had written it thirty years ago, in the spring, and that she had never posted it. She told him what she had believed for all those years, and then she told him what she had actually read at a metal table beside the road, four hours from home.

"I blamed you for asking," she said. "I called your work small, and I told you not to write to me. My mother was ill and I was frightened, and I put all of that on your head, because that was easier. Then I told myself a kinder story every year until I believed it. That is the whole letter, and that is what I came to say."

Halit turned the envelope over once with two fingers. He did not open it.

"I waited one winter," he said. "In the spring I stopped waiting. I thought you had married somebody from your school." He smiled slightly. "I also asked you badly. I asked you to leave a school where you were the best teacher in the building. And I asked you on a Thursday evening, outside a classroom door, with my coat already on. My father was dying and I was in a hurry, and you were the one who had to pay for my hurry."

"That does not excuse two pages," Nesrin said.

"No. But your two pages are thirty years old, and I have made worse mistakes since Tuesday." He stood up, went to the shelves at the back, and reached for something on the highest one. "Wait. There is something here that belongs to you, and I have never known what to do with it."

He put a small brown clock on the cloth in front of her. A little piece of paper hung from it on a string. On the paper there was a date in blue ink, and two words in his handwriting: Nesrin Hanım.

"Your mother's clock," he said. "You brought it in on a Monday, the week before I left. I mended it that same week. Then nobody came for it."

Nesrin put her hand flat on the top of the clock, and her eyes filled, and she let them. She remembered the kitchen, and the sound of it on the shelf above the bread, and how her mother used to turn the key at the back every Sunday morning.

"It has been fixed for thirty years?"

"It has been fixed and waiting for thirty years," Halit said. "Like a few other things in this room."

He wrapped the clock in newspaper, and he would not take any money for it. Then he opened a drawer beside the bench, and Nesrin saw that it was full of small paper tickets, dozens of them, all with names and dates on them. He put her yellow envelope in with the tickets and closed the drawer.

"Are you not going to read it?" she asked.

"I have heard it from you," he said. "That was what I needed to hear. This one can stay with the other things that were never collected."

His wife came back from the market while Nesrin was standing up. There was a short, easy conversation about the rain and the price of tomatoes and the bus times. Then Nesrin was out in the street again with a clock under her arm.

She took the noon bus and reached home on Friday evening. The truck came at eight the next morning, and the men carried her life down three flights of stairs in ninety minutes.

On Sunday she put the clock on the shelf above the bread in the new kitchen and turned the key at the back until it would not turn any more. Ceyda stood in the kitchen door with a cup in her hand and asked if the whole journey had been worth six hours on a bus.

"I came back with a clock," Nesrin said, "and I put down something heavier than a clock."

It kept perfect time after that, except that it ran three minutes fast, and she never once put it right.
