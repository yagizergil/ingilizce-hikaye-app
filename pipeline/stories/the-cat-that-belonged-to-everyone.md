# Four Plates Under the Stairs

The rain had stopped an hour before, but the steps were still wet when Deniz carried his rubbish bag down to the street. He almost stepped on her. The grey cat was lying in the dark corner under the last stairs, beside the post boxes, and she did not move. She always moved. In two years she had never once let him touch her, although she ate from his hand almost every evening.

Deniz put the bag down and bent his knees until his face was level with hers. "Boss," he said quietly, and then again, more softly, "Boss, it's me."

One ear turned towards his voice, and that was all she gave him. Her sides went in and out much too fast, like a small machine that could not slow itself down. Her eyes were open, but they were not looking at anything, and something grey had dried around her nose.

Then he noticed the plates. His own plastic plate sat exactly where he always left it, and the chicken from the night before had not been touched. Next to it stood a blue bowl that he had never seen in his life. On the other side of the post boxes, half under the pipe, somebody had left a small dish with two pieces of fish in it. And behind the door of the lift, pushed hard against the wall, there was a fourth plate.

Deniz stood up slowly. He had lived in this building for three years, and he had learned everybody's footsteps without ever learning a single name.

He knocked on the door of the ground floor flat, because it was the nearest door and because he could not think of anything better. An old man opened it in a brown sweater with a newspaper in one hand. He looked at Deniz the way people look at strangers who ring the wrong bell.

"Sorry to disturb you," Deniz said. "There is a cat downstairs, under the—"

"Paşa," the old man said at once. "Where is she? She did not come at six."

"She is lying by the post boxes, and she will not get up."

The old man dropped his newspaper on the shoe cupboard and came out into the hall without changing his shoes. He moved much faster than Deniz had expected from a man of his age. He got down beside the cat with one hand flat on the wall, and when he touched her head she did not fight him at all. That frightened Deniz more than the breathing did.

"She is cold," the old man said. "Her ears are cold, and that is a bad sign."

"Do you know her?"

"I have fed her since before you came to this building." He looked up. "My name is Kemal. Ground floor. You are the boy with the telephones."

"Deniz. Second floor." He pointed at the plates under the stairs. "Whose are all of these?"

Kemal frowned at them as if he had never counted them before in his life. "The fish is mine," he said. "The blue bowl is not mine, and that plate by the lift I have never seen in my life."

Above them a door opened, and quick steps came down two floors without stopping once. A woman in a blue nurse's uniform arrived with her bag still on her shoulder, and she stopped hard when she saw the two men on the floor.

"Is that Mira?" she said. "What has happened to her?"

"Who is Mira?" Kemal said.

"The cat." The woman went down on her knees, put two fingers under the cat's chin, and stayed like that for several seconds without speaking. "Her heart is going much too fast. How long has she been like this?"

"I found her about ten minutes ago," Deniz said.

"I fed her yesterday morning, and she was completely fine yesterday morning." The woman stood up and pushed her hair back off her face. "My name is Leyla, and I live on the first floor. There is an animal clinic on Fig Street which stays open until nine. If we leave now, we can still catch them."

"We cannot carry her in our arms," Kemal said. "She will fight us all the way there."

"Then we need a box," Leyla said, "a box and a towel, and we need them in the next two minutes."

A small voice came from the stairs above them. A girl of about nine was standing on the fourth step with both hands on the rail, and she had clearly been listening for some time.

"I have a box," she said. "Karamel sleeps in it when it rains."

# Four Names, One Cat

The girl's name was Zehra, and she lived on the third floor with her grandmother. She came down with a shoe box and a green towel, and her grandmother followed her, still tying her scarf.

"I am Aynur, third floor," the old woman said to the whole group at once. "What is wrong with the animal?"

"Nobody knows yet," Leyla said, as she put the towel in the box and made a small nest with her hands. "Zehra, hold this side for me, and keep your face away from her mouth."

The cat let them lift her, and that was the worst part of the whole evening. Deniz had watched that cat run from an open umbrella, from a plastic bag, and from his own shadow on the wall. Now she lay in a shoe box on a green towel and did not even turn her head.

They went out into the street together, five people and one box, and none of them had ever walked anywhere together before. Kemal wanted to carry the box, so Leyla let him carry it for half of the road. Then she took it back from him without saying anything about his hands, which were shaking badly.

The clinic on Fig Street had two plastic chairs and a strong smell of soap. The animal doctor was a young woman called Ela, with tired eyes and a pen behind her ear. She asked her questions while she was already touching the cat's stomach with the flat of her hand.

"How old is she?"

Everybody answered at the same time, and everybody said something different. Deniz said two, Leyla said about five, and then Kemal said something that made both of them turn round.

"Twelve," he said. "She came to this street in the year my wife died, and that was twelve years ago in the spring."

Ela wrote something down without any change in her face. "And what do you feed her?"

"Cooked chicken from my own dinner, most evenings at about seven," Deniz said.

"Dry cat food," Leyla said. "Half a cup every morning before I go to work."

Zehra looked at her grandmother, and her grandmother looked hard at the floor. "Cheese," the girl said in a very small voice. "And sometimes bread in warm milk."

"Fish from a can," Kemal said. "And soup, when there is soup left over, because she has always liked soup."

Ela stopped writing and looked up at the four of them for a long moment. "Am I understanding you correctly?" she said slowly. "This cat is eating four dinners every day."

Nobody spoke. Behind the wall a dog was making a great deal of noise, and the sound seemed very loud in the small room.

"Five," said Aynur. "The woman in the corner shop puts something out for her as well."

Ela laid her pen down flat on the table. "Please wait outside, all of you," she said. "I need to do some tests, and that will take me about forty minutes."

There were two plastic chairs in the hall outside, which meant that three of them had to stand. For a while nobody said anything at all, because speaking meant admitting what they had just heard.

"Boss," Deniz said at last. "I have been calling her Boss for two years."

"Boss?" Leyla almost laughed. "Why Boss?"

"Because she sits in the middle of the door and decides who is allowed to come in."

"That is exactly right," Kemal said. "That is the truest thing anybody has said tonight."

"I call her Karamel, because of the colour under her chin," Zehra said.

"Mira," said Leyla. "It was the first name that came into my head, and after that it stuck."

"Paşa," Kemal said. "I believed she was a male cat for two years, and then I could not change it."

Aynur shook her head slowly at all of them. "Twelve years," she said, "and I have never once spoken to any of you properly. I know the sound of your door, Kemal Bey, and I know that you cough in the mornings, but I did not know your name."

Deniz thought about the four plates under the stairs, each one pushed into its own dark corner. They had all been hiding the same secret from one another for years, and the secret had been sleeping under the post boxes the whole time.

# What Ela Said

Ela came out with a paper in her hand and asked all four of them to come in, which is not something an animal doctor usually does.

"She is very ill," she said. "Her kidneys are failing, and the kidneys are the part of the body that cleans the blood. Hers are old and tired, and they have been getting worse for a long time. She is also carrying about two kilos more than she should carry."

"But she is a small cat," Kemal said.

"She was a small cat once, and she is not a small cat now." Ela turned the paper round so that they could all see the numbers, although none of them understood the numbers. "I have to be honest with you, because there is no point in being kind about this. Salt is very bad for kidneys like hers, and fish from a can is salt, and soup is salt, and cheese is salt too. Bread and milk give her nothing useful at all."

Kemal sat down on the metal chair by the door, and he did it very carefully, the way people sit when they are not sure their legs will hold them.

"I have been giving her fish for twelve years," he said.

"And you have been keeping her alive for twelve years," Ela said. "A street cat very rarely reaches twelve. But four people have been feeding her, and each of you believed that you were the only one, and the cat was clever enough to say nothing about it." She almost smiled. "They are always clever about that."

"Can you make her better?" Zehra asked.

"I cannot repair her kidneys, and nobody can, but she is not dying tonight if we act tonight." Ela pointed at the list she had written while they were waiting. "She has to stay here for two days with a needle in her leg, so that water and medicine go into her blood slowly. After that she needs a special food, and one pill in the morning and one at night, every day for the rest of her life. She also has to lose weight slowly, which means that somebody must control exactly how much she eats."

"And how much will all of that cost?" Aynur asked.

Ela turned the paper again and pointed at the number at the bottom of the page. Deniz looked at it and felt something drop in his stomach, because it was more than he earned in a week and a half.

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then Leyla took out her phone and began pressing numbers.

"Divided by four," she said, "it is one dinner in a restaurant and a taxi that I do not take."

"Divided by five," Aynur said. "We are two flats and one cat, but there are five of us standing here."

"I do not have my part this week," Deniz said, and his face was hot. "I can pay half of it now and the rest on Friday, when the shop pays me."

"Friday is fine," Kemal said. "Friday is completely fine, and I will pay for the special food. All of it, every month, for as long as she needs it." He was still looking at his own hands. "That is the smallest thing I can do."

"Kemal Bey, you did not know," Leyla said.

"That is exactly the problem," the old man answered. "For twelve years I did not know one single thing about any of you, and I was proud of it. I thought that I was a quiet neighbour, but I was only a closed door."

Ela coughed politely, because a small clinic at nine at night is not the place for that kind of sentence. "There is one more thing, and it is the hard one," she said. "If she goes back to the street, none of this will work. Somebody has to give her two pills a day at more or less the same hours, and somebody has to watch whether she is drinking. She needs a door that opens for her."

The four of them looked at each other, and for the first time that evening nobody spoke at once.

# The Paper on the Door

The list went up on the main entrance door on Thursday, and Zehra drew the cat's face at the top of it in orange pencil.

They argued about the name for twenty minutes in the entrance hall, standing there in their coats like people waiting for a bus. Leyla said that Mira was a proper name for a proper cat, and Deniz said that Boss described her character honestly. In the end Aynur solved it, the way grandmothers solve most things.

"She has answered to Paşa longer than she has answered to anything else," she said. "The child can call her Karamel, and cats do not mind two names."

So the paper said PAŞA at the top, and under that there were rules, and under the rules there were the days of the week. The morning pill and the evening pill both belonged to Kemal, because he was always at home and because he had asked for them. The weighing happened every Sunday and belonged to Leyla, who owned the only scales in the building that anybody trusted. The special food was one measured cup in the morning and one in the evening. The cup lived on the shelf by the post boxes, so that everybody could see whether it had been used.

At the bottom, in big capital letters, Deniz had written the most important line of all. Nothing else: no fish, no cheese, no chicken and no soup. Anybody who feeds her, he wrote, is helping to kill her.

It seemed a hard thing to write, but Kemal read it twice and said that it was correct.

Paşa came home on Saturday in a proper cat box that Leyla had borrowed from the clinic. She walked out of it into Kemal's flat, looked at the heater in the corner, and lay down in front of it as if she had planned the whole thing herself. Kemal put the green towel down beside her. Zehra brought the shoe box up and placed it next to the towel, although everybody knew that the cat would never use it again. The four plates under the stairs went into the rubbish on the same afternoon.

It was not simple after that. Paşa hated the pills and hid behind the washing machine on the fourth day, so Kemal had to telephone Leyla at eleven at night. Leyla came down with a coat over her night clothes and got the cat out with a long stick and a great deal of patience. The woman in the corner shop kept putting fish out in front of her door until Aynur went and explained the problem to her twice, slowly. In the second month the cat lost three hundred grams, and Leyla wrote the number on the paper and drew two lines under it, and Zehra added a small star.

One evening in the autumn Deniz came home late from the shop and found Kemal sitting on the step outside the entrance. The cat was beside him, and she was not in his arms. She was simply sitting near him, close enough to touch, watching the street with her eyes half closed.

"She wanted some air," Kemal said. "Sit down for a minute, because my tea is cold anyway."

Deniz sat down on the step next to him. After a while he put his hand out slowly, the way you do when you expect nothing at all. The grey cat looked at his fingers, and she did not run.

"She is thinner," he said.

"Four hundred grams," Kemal answered. "Leyla wrote it on the door this morning, and you can go and look."

The lights were coming on in the flats above them, first on the third floor and then on the first, and Deniz found that he now knew whose windows those were. He kept his hand on the warm grey head for as long as she allowed it, which was about eight seconds. Both men counted, and neither of them said so.
