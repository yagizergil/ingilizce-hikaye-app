# The List on the Wall

Defne stuck a long piece of paper on the kitchen wall. It had two columns: "Done" and "Not Done." The "Not Done" column was much longer.

Her sister Zeynep was getting married in six days. Defne was the oldest sister, so she was in charge of the lists. She liked lists. Lists made the wedding feel possible.

"Chairs for the garden," she read out loud. "Not done. Flowers for the tables. Not done. Musicians for the henna night. Not done."

Zeynep walked into the kitchen in her pajamas. Her hair was in a messy bun. She looked at the wall and groaned.

"Please stop reading the list," Zeynep said. "It makes me nervous."

"Somebody has to read it," Defne said. "Mom is at the shop. Dad is fixing the car. I am the only free person in this house."

"You are not free. You have a job."

"I took three days off," Defne said. "For you."

Zeynep sat down at the table and put her head in her hands. "Six days," she said. "Six days and we do not have chairs."

"We have a plan for the chairs," Defne said. "Uncle Kemal has forty chairs in his shop. He is going to bring them on Thursday."

"And the flowers?"

"The flower seller in the market. Mom already talked to her."

"And the musicians for the henna night?"

Defne stopped. She looked at the list again. Next to "musicians," she had written a phone number and nothing else.

"I called them yesterday," Defne said slowly. "They did not answer."

"Call again."

Defne picked up her phone and called the number. It rang five times. Then a woman answered, but her voice was strange and quiet.

"I am sorry," the woman said. "We cannot come. My husband is the drummer, and he is sick. Very sick."

"Sick how?" Defne asked.

"He needs to go to the hospital today. I am sorry about the wedding."

Defne put the phone down slowly.

"What did she say?" Zeynep asked.

"The musicians cannot come," Defne said. "The henna night is in three days, and we have no music."

Zeynep put her head back in her hands. This time she did not lift it up.

"Everything is falling apart," she said, her voice small.

Defne looked at the long list on the wall. Under "Not Done," she added one more line: "Musicians — NEW PLAN NEEDED."

She did not know the plan yet. But she knew somebody in this family always found one.

# Every Phone in the Family

Defne called their cousin Mert first. He knew every young person in the town.

"Musicians for a henna night?" Mert said. "In three days? That is very fast."

"I know," Defne said. "But we need something. Even a small group."

"Let me ask around," Mert said. "I will call you back tonight."

Defne also called their grandmother. The old woman listened quietly and then laughed a little.

"Musicians," her grandmother said. "In my time, we did not always have real musicians. We sang ourselves."

"Sang ourselves?"

"Yes. The women of the family sang the old songs. Somebody played a spoon on a plate. It was simple, but it was warm."

Defne wrote this down under the list, in small letters: "Backup plan: family sings?"

That evening, Mert called back. His voice was excited.

"I found a group," he said. "Three young men. They play the saz and the drum. But they are far away, in the next town."

"Can they come?"

"They can come, but they need a car. Their van is broken."

"We can send a car," Defne said. "Dad's van is free on Friday."

"One more thing," Mert said. "They are not cheap. It is short notice."

Defne looked at the money envelope on the table. It was for the flowers and the food, not for musicians.

"How much?" she asked.

Mert told her the price. It was more than they had planned.

Defne walked into the living room, where her mother was folding tablecloths. She explained everything: the sick drummer, Mert's group, the money problem.

Her mother listened and did not speak for a moment.

"We can move some money from the flowers," her mother said finally. "Simple flowers are fine. This is not about flowers."

"Are you sure?"

"A wedding without music is very quiet," her mother said. "A wedding without extra flowers is still a wedding."

Defne felt her shoulders relax a little. She called Mert back and said yes.

That night, she moved numbers between the columns on her list. "Musicians" moved from "Not Done" to "Done," with a small note: "Coming from the next town, Dad's van."

Zeynep came into the kitchen and read the wall.

"You fixed it," she said, surprised.

"We fixed it," Defne said. "Mert, Mom, Grandma, everyone."

Zeynep hugged her sister for a long time.

# The Henna Night

Friday came fast. The garden filled with red lights and long tables.

Defne's father drove to the next town in the morning and brought back the three musicians. They carried a saz, a drum, and big smiles.

By evening, the garden looked like a different place. Uncle Kemal's forty chairs stood in neat rows. Simple flowers, mostly from their own garden, sat in small jars on every table.

Zeynep wore a red dress with gold coins on it, ready for the henna ceremony. Her hands were open, waiting for the henna paste.

The musicians began to play. The saz filled the garden with a soft, old sound. The drum joined in, slow at first, then faster.

Women gathered around Zeynep in a circle. Their grandmother started an old song, and the other women joined her voice by voice.

Defne watched from the side, holding a candle for the ceremony. Her list was finally empty. Every line said "Done."

Her mother came and stood beside her.

"You did a good job this week," her mother said.

"I did not do it alone," Defne said. "Everyone helped."

"That is the whole point of a wedding," her mother said. "One family, many hands."

The henna paste went onto Zeynep's palm, a small circle of green-brown. Someone put a gold coin on top of it and wrapped her hand in red cloth. Zeynep laughed and cried at the same time.

The music grew louder. People began to dance around the tables, their hands high in the air. The three musicians from the next town smiled at each other, happy to be part of the night.

Defne finally sat down. She watched her sister dance in her red dress, surrounded by their whole family. The chairs held everyone. The small flowers looked pretty in the candlelight. The music from a broken van and a sick drummer's replacement filled the whole garden.

She thought about the list on the kitchen wall, now covered in "Done."

In six days, so many things went wrong. But in six days, so many things also went right.

Zeynep found her sister in the crowd and pulled her up to dance too.

"No lists tonight," Zeynep said, laughing. "Just dance."

Defne put her list out of her mind, at least for one night, and danced.
