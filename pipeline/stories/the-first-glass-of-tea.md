# Five in the Morning

Deniz opened the shop door at five in the morning. The street lights were still on. Cold air came in with her, so she pushed the door shut fast.

The tea shop was small. Six tables, twelve chairs, and one big tea machine by the wall. Her father cleaned that machine every night for twenty years.

Last night nobody cleaned it. Her father was at home in bed. He fell down the stairs on Friday and hurt his back. The doctor said one word to him: rest.

"Two weeks," her mother said. "Two weeks with no shop."

"No," Deniz said. "Tuesday is market day."

She was seventeen. She knew the work.

She filled the big pot with water. She pressed the red button on the machine. Nothing happened. She pressed it again and waited. The little light stayed dark.

Deniz stood still and listened. A good tea machine makes a low sound when it works. The shop was quiet, like a room in an empty house.

She checked the plug. She checked the other plug by the door. She turned the lights off and on. The lights worked fine. Only the machine was dead.

She tried the old way next. Her father hit the side of the machine with his hand when it was slow. Deniz hit it twice, in the same place, near the top. Her hand hurt. The machine did nothing.

"Fine," she said to it. "Then we look inside you."

Deniz put her bag down and opened the back of the machine with a small knife. Inside there was dust, an old spider house, and a short black piece of metal. The metal was burnt. She touched it and her finger became black.

She knew this piece. It heats the water. Without it, the machine is only a big empty box.

The clock over the door said twenty past five.

At seven o'clock the market opens in the next street. The men carry boxes of tomatoes and apples from the trucks. They work in the cold from five, and at seven they come here. They come every Tuesday. They stand at the door, they drink two hundred glasses of tea, and they leave the money on the table.

That money pays the rent for the shop. Her father always says this. He says it like a rule, not like a problem.

Deniz thought about the other days of the week. On other days ten or twelve people come in. Old men with newspapers. The woman from the flower shop. Two boys from the school, in the afternoon, with one glass between them. Tuesday is different. Tuesday is the whole week in three hours.

Deniz took her phone out. Her finger stopped over her father's name. She put the phone back in her pocket. He was in bed with a bad back and he was going to get up and come here. She knew him.

She washed the black off her hands and looked at the street.

Across the road there was another tea shop. It was bigger, with new yellow chairs outside. Osman's shop. Osman was her father's friend for many years, and then he was not.

The story was old and short. Three years ago Osman opened his shop across the road. Her father put a sign in the window: TEA, HALF PRICE, ALL WINTER. The market men crossed the road. Osman's yellow chairs stayed empty all winter. After that, the two men stopped talking.

Osman's lights were on now. Deniz watched a shape move behind his window.

She looked at the burnt piece of metal in her hand.

"Not him," she said out loud. "Anybody else."

She put on her coat again and put the metal in her pocket, the pocket with no hole. Then she wrote a note and left it on the table for nobody. It said: BACK AT SIX.

# The Man Who Fixes Things

Fikret's repair shop was five minutes away, next to the bread shop. His window was full of old radios, clocks, and a fan with no cover. A yellow paper on the glass said: OPEN 9 TO 7.

It was half past five.

Deniz walked to the side of the building and looked up. There was a light in the flat above the shop. She found a stone, threw it, and hit the window on the second try.

Fikret opened the window. His hair stood up on one side.

"The shop is shut," he said.

"Fikret Amca, it is Deniz. From the tea shop."

He looked down at her for a long moment. Then his head went back inside and the window closed.

She waited. She was going to throw a second stone. Then the shop light came on and the door opened.

Fikret took the black metal piece from her hand and turned it near the lamp. He smelled it. He put it down on the table.

"Your father's machine is older than you," he said.

"Can you fix this piece?"

"No. It is finished. Look at the middle. It is broken in two."

"Then a new one."

"A new one," Fikret said slowly. He opened a drawer and moved things with one finger. Then he opened a second drawer. Inside there were hundreds of small metal things in old cigarette boxes. He took one out, looked at it, and put it back.

"This machine came from a factory in İzmir," he said. "That factory closed. Nobody makes this piece now."

"There must be one somewhere."

"There is one shop in the city with old parts. It opens at ten."

"Ten is too late," Deniz said. "I need tea at seven."

Fikret sat down on his low chair. He was quiet for a moment.

"There is one more way," he said. "Somebody with the same machine."

"Who has the same machine?"

He looked at her, and then he looked at the wall. He did not want to say it.

"Osman," Deniz said.

"Osman bought his machine from the same factory. Twelve years ago, maybe more. Then he bought his big new one. The old one is in his back room. I saw it there in the summer."

"Is it working?"

"The water part is dead in that one too. But the metal piece inside it is good. I put it in myself, six years ago." Fikret pushed his glasses up. "One good piece and one good machine. In two different shops. Two shops that do not talk."

Deniz picked up the burnt metal and put it in her pocket.

"Thank you," she said.

"Deniz." Fikret stood up. "Do not fight with him. Osman is not a bad man. He is a tired man with empty chairs."

She walked back with the cold wind in her face. The sky was still black over the roofs, but there was a thin grey line behind the tall buildings. Down in the market street the trucks were already there. Men were carrying boxes. One man dropped a box and oranges ran across the road.

Deniz walked past the bread shop. The smell came out with the warm air, and her stomach made a noise. She did not stop. She was thinking about Osman's face on the day her father put that sign in the window. The two men were at a wedding together in the summer, at the same table, and they did not look at each other once.

At the corner she stopped and counted her money. Forty lira and some coins.

Then she crossed the road to the yellow chairs.

# The Piece in the Back Room

Osman was washing glasses when she came in. He was a big man with grey hair and a very clean apron. He did not look up.

"We open at six," he said.

"I am not a customer."

He looked up then. His hands stopped in the water.

"Ah," he said.

Deniz put the burnt piece of metal on the wet table between them. She did not sit down.

"Our machine is dead," she said. "This piece is burnt. Fikret says your old machine has the same piece, and the piece is good."

Osman looked at the metal. He dried his hands slowly.

"So you want my machine."

"One piece from it."

"On market day."

"On market day," Deniz said.

Osman laughed, one short sound with no fun in it. He walked to the window and looked at her father's dark shop across the road. The old sign was still there in the corner of the window: TEA, HALF PRICE.

"Ask me on any other morning," he said. "Not this one."

"My father fell down the stairs on Friday. He is in bed. He does not know I am here."

"Then he is going to be angry with you."

"Yes," Deniz said. "He is."

Osman said nothing. Outside, a truck door closed with a big noise.

Deniz took out her forty lira and put it on the table.

"That is not enough," Osman said. "You know that."

"I know." She pushed the money to one side. "So take my customers too."

He turned around.

"Today the market men drink at my door, like every Tuesday," she said. "Two hundred glasses, maybe more. I make the tea in my shop. You come with your big pots and your boy, and we serve them together. Half the money for you. Every Tuesday until the spring."

"Half," Osman said.

"Half."

"Your father is going to say no."

"My father is in bed. I am opening the shop today." Deniz put her hand on the burnt metal. "And in the spring you can say yes or no again. But your yellow chairs are empty every Tuesday, Osman Amca. I count them from my window."

The clock behind him made a small sound. Ten past six.

Osman walked to the back of the shop and opened a door. Deniz saw boxes, a broken chair, and under a grey cloth, the old machine. He pulled the cloth off. He took a tool from a shelf and gave it to her.

"You open it," he said. "My back is worse than your father's."

She sat down on the floor and worked. The old screws were hard, and one of them cut her finger. Osman stood over her with a lamp and told her which screws to turn.

The good piece came out at twenty-five past six. It was heavy and dark grey and it did not smell of fire.

They ran across the road together. Osman carried the lamp. Deniz opened her father's shop and put the new piece in with her cold hands. Her fingers did not work well. Osman held the light very close and said nothing at all.

She closed the back of the machine and pressed the red button.

The little light came on. A low sound started inside, like a cat under a chair.

"Water," Osman said.

They filled the big pot together. At five to seven the first man put his head in the door and asked for tea. Deniz gave him the first glass. It was too hot and too strong, and he drank it and asked for a second one.

By half past seven there were men at the door, at the tables, and on the step outside. Osman's boy ran between the two shops with a tray. Osman stood at the door and shouted at everybody in a happy voice.

At nine the market got quiet. Deniz sat down for the first time. Her hands smelled of metal and tea.

Her phone rang. Her father.

"Deniz. Somebody says there are men outside our shop."

"Yes, Baba."

"And Osman is with them. In our shop."

"Yes, Baba."

There was a long quiet on the phone. She waited and looked at the empty glasses on the table.

"Two hundred?" her father asked.

"Two hundred and forty," Deniz said. "We are going to count the money tonight."

"We," her father said.

"We," said Deniz.
