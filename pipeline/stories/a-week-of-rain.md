# Forty-Nine Days

Musa pushed his boot into the soil, and the ground broke into grey dust that hung in the air instead of falling.

"Forty-nine days," he said out loud, because by now the number had become a habit.

He stood at the top of the field and looked down the long rows of pepper plants. The leaves near the road were still green, but they were thin, and their edges curled inward like burnt paper. At the low end, close to the dry stream, the plants had already stopped trying. He had counted them that morning: sixty rows still alive, thirty rows finished.

Hale came up the path with two empty cans, one in each hand.

"The tank is empty," she said. "There was only mud at the bottom."

"Then tonight we water the top rows and nothing else."

"That is what you said last night, and the night before."

"It was right last night too."

She put the cans down and pressed her hand against her shoulder, where the metal handles had cut a red line into the skin. She was fifteen, and she had carried water up this path every evening since the middle of July. Musa knew that he should send her back to the house, but he also knew that he could not carry enough water without her.

The stream below the field had been loud when he was a boy. His father used to send him down with a bucket in the morning, and the water was so cold that it hurt his fingers. Now the stream was a line of white stones with a few dark spots between them, and even the birds had stopped coming to it.

His father had also cut a narrow channel across the lowest corner of the field. When the hills sent water down in the spring, the channel took it around the crop and out to the stream, so that the roots never stood in it. Two springs ago Musa had filled the channel in with the tractor and planted two extra rows in the flat, soft ground. Everybody said the same thing that year: the weather is changing, we get less water every summer, use the space.

His phone rang while he was walking back to the house. It was Aydın, the buyer from the city, who took peppers from six farms in the valley.

"The trucks come on the twenty-fifth," Aydın said. "Three tons from you. That was our agreement in May."

"I know what we agreed."

"Can you give me three tons?"

Musa looked back down the rows. "I can give you three tons if it rains this week."

Aydın was quiet for a moment. "Musa, I have heard that sentence from every farmer in the valley today. I need a number, not a wish. If you cannot fill the truck, tell me now, and I will buy from Konya."

"Come on the twenty-fifth," Musa said, and he ended the call before Aydın could ask again.

In the kitchen his wife Sevim had laid out bread and white cheese, and the fan was pushing hot air from one wall to the other. Musa sat down and did the arithmetic in his head, although he had done it a hundred times and the answer never changed. In May he had borrowed money from his brother Ferit for seed and for the repair of the pump. Ferit had not asked for anything in writing, because brothers do not do that, but in September his son was starting school in the city, and he needed that money back.

"Thursday is our day for the channel water," Sevim said. "You should sleep, so that you can open the gate early."

The village took its water from a small dam in the hills, and the main channel carried it down past every farm. Each farm had its own turn: Kadir on Monday, the Bozkurt brothers on Tuesday, Musa on Thursday. Thursday had always been enough. Thursday had been enough for his father, and for his father's father.

He was standing at his gate before the sun came over the ridge. He lifted the metal handle, and the gate opened with the same dry sound it always made.

Nothing came through it. Not a drop, not even the wet smell of stone.

He walked up along the main channel with the sun rising behind the hills, and he found the answer four hundred metres above his own land. Kadir's gate stood open, and it had clearly been open for a long time.

# The Gate at the Top of the Hill

Kadir was in his apple trees with a hose in his hand when Musa came through the gap in the wall.

"It is Thursday," Musa said.

"I know what day it is."

"Then why is your gate open?"

Kadir turned the hose off and set it down between two young trees. He was ten years older than Musa, a thin man with a very straight back, and he had never once raised his voice in forty years.

"Come with me," he said. "I want to show you something, and then you can shout at me if you still want to."

They walked up the hill together without talking. The path was hard as a road, and small stones rolled away under their boots. After twenty minutes they came out above the dam, and Musa stopped.

He had seen the dam low before. He had never seen it like this. The water had pulled back from the walls and left a wide grey ring of cracked mud, and in the middle there was a pool that looked like a puddle after a small rain. The mouth of the main channel sat in the open air, half a metre above the surface.

"I closed my gate on Monday night," Kadir said. "I closed it because it was your turn, and because I have never taken another man's water. Nothing came down on Tuesday either, and the Bozkurt brothers got nothing on their day. The mouth is dry, Musa. There is no water in the system at all."

"Then where does your hose water come from?"

"From my own well, and the well gives me two hours a day before it turns to mud. I use those two hours on the trees, because a tree takes eight years and a pepper plant takes three months." Kadir looked at him. "You would do the same thing."

Musa sat down on a stone. Below them the whole valley was the colour of old bread, and the only green was in narrow lines where men and women had been carrying cans up and down since July.

"I am sorry," he said at last.

"You have been carrying water in your hands for seven weeks. A man who does that says things he does not mean." Kadir was already walking back down. "Watch the sky in the west on Saturday. My knee has been aching since Tuesday, and my knee is better than the television."

That evening Musa took the box down from the top of the cupboard. It held the money for the winter coal, and it was not much. He counted it on the kitchen table while Sevim watched him from the door.

"A truck of water is one full day of watering," she said. "Maybe two, if we are careful."

"Two days keeps the top rows alive."

"And then?"

"And then it rains."

Hale was doing her homework at the end of the table, and she did not look up from her book. "The low corner will flood if there is a big rain," she said. "Grandfather's channel was there for a reason. You told me that yourself."

"We have not had a big rain in three years," Musa said. "Do your homework."

The water truck came on Friday afternoon and filled the tank in eleven minutes. Musa and Hale carried cans until midnight, and the plants drank the water so fast that the soil looked dry again by morning.

Then, on Saturday, the light changed.

It happened while Musa was tying up a fallen plant near the road. The yellow flattened out and turned grey, and the heat lifted off his back like a coat coming off. He stood up slowly. In the west, above the ridge, clouds were building on top of each other, dark blue at the bottom and white where the sun still caught them.

Sevim came out to the gate. Hale came out after her. All three of them stood in the road and looked west, and along the valley other people were coming out of their houses and doing exactly the same thing.

The first drop hit the dust beside Musa's boot and made a small brown crown.

# The Sound on the Roof

Rain on a dry field does not sound like rain. It sounds like small stones falling on cloth, and then, when the ground softens, it stops sounding like anything at all.

They stayed outside for the first ten minutes because nobody wanted to go in. Hale turned in a circle with her arms out and her mouth open. Sevim laughed at her and then laughed at herself, and Musa stood with his hat in his hand and let the water run down through his hair and into his collar. Somebody in the village was ringing a bell, which was ridiculous, and which was also exactly right.

That night the rain on the roof was so loud that they had to speak up at dinner. Musa slept better than he had slept since June. He dreamed of nothing and woke at five, and the sound was still there.

On Sunday it rained all day. Musa walked the rows in the morning with water running off the end of his nose and told himself that the field was drinking. Three days of this and the plants would be twice the size. The peppers would fill out, and the twenty-fifth was still eighteen days away, and Aydın would get his three tons.

On Monday it rained harder. The dry stream came back to life around noon, first as a thin brown line and then as something with a voice. By evening it was over the white stones and pushing at the roots of the willow.

On Tuesday morning Musa came out of the house and stopped on the step.

The bottom third of his field was a lake.

It was not a wet patch. It was a flat sheet of grey-brown water, thirty metres across, with the tops of pepper plants standing in it like a drowned fence. Water from the hills was still coming down the slope and running across the road in a wide, shallow sheet, and every drop of it was going into that corner and staying there.

He knew what he was looking at before he had walked ten steps. His stomach knew it first.

His father's channel had cut across that corner for sixty years, and it had taken the hill water around the crop and out to the stream. Musa had filled it in with his own tractor, on a warm afternoon, in a year when everyone in the valley was talking about how the rain never came any more. He had planted two extra rows in the soft ground and been pleased with himself.

He went down into the water in his boots. It came over the tops of them at the second step, cold and heavy, and the mud under it pulled at his feet. He pushed his hand down to the base of a plant and lifted. The whole thing came up without any weight at all, and the roots hung off it, black and soft and smelling of something dead.

Roots need air. He had known that since he was six years old.

Hale was standing at the edge of the field with her arms wrapped around herself.

"How many rows?" she asked.

"Thirty in the water now." Musa's voice came out flat. "Everything below the middle path if it does not drain by tonight. That is more than half."

"It is still raining."

"Yes." He looked up the slope towards the road, where the hill water was arriving in a wide brown sheet, patient and endless. "It is still raining."

He had waited forty-nine days for this, and he had wanted nothing else in the world, and now it was standing on his crop and killing it, and there was nobody to blame but a man with a tractor and a good idea.

# What the Stones Are For

Musa went to the shed and came out with the long iron bar and the shovel.

The old channel had run from the corner of the wall, past the walnut tree, and down to the stream. He could still see the line if he knew where to look, because the soil there had always settled a little lower than the rest. He put the shovel in and began.

Wet earth is heavy. After twenty minutes his arms were shaking and he had opened three metres. He needed thirty, and he needed them before the water sat on those roots for another night. He kept going because there was nothing else to do, and because standing still was worse.

The rain came down steadily, without wind, in the way that rain comes when it has no plan to stop.

At some point he became aware of lights on the road. Two lights, low and yellow, moving slowly along the wall. He straightened up with mud to his knees and watched Kadir's small tractor turn in through the gate with the blade on the front, and Kadir's son Ali walking behind it with a shovel over each shoulder.

"Your daughter telephoned my wife," Kadir called down. "She said her father was digging alone in the dark, which is a stupid way to die."

"I filled it in myself," Musa said. "Two springs ago. With my own tractor."

"I know. I watched you do it, and I said nothing, because a man's field is his own business." Kadir climbed down and looked at the line of the old channel, then at the lake, then at the slope where the hill water was still arriving. "Show me where your father put it."

They worked until the sky turned grey. The tractor blade took the top and Musa and Ali cut the sides with shovels, and the channel came back to life a metre at a time, black and rough and ugly. The two extra rows went first; Musa cut straight through his own plants and did not slow down. Near the walnut tree they hit the old stone lining that his father had laid, still there under the mud, still in a straight line.

When they broke through the last two metres into the bank of the stream, the water did not run. It jumped. It went down the channel in a brown rope with leaves and pepper plants turning over in it, and the lake began to move, and by full daylight Musa could see the tops of the middle rows standing clear.

Kadir sat on the tractor step and drank tea from Sevim's flask with his hands shaking.

"You have lost the bottom," he said.

"I have lost the bottom," Musa agreed.

He telephoned Aydın at eight o'clock, standing in the doorway with mud drying on his legs.

"I cannot give you three tons," he said. "I can give you one and a half, and they will be good. The rest is in the stream."

There was a long silence on the line. "Every farmer who called me today gave me a story," Aydın said. "You are the first one who gave me a number. Bring what you have on the twenty-fifth."

Then he went to see Ferit, which was harder than the digging. He did not sit down, and he did not explain about the dam or the clouds or the two extra rows.

"I can pay you half in September," he said. "For the other half, I will work your land through the winter, and I will not take money for it. Your olives, your walls, whatever you need. You can tell me no."

Ferit looked at him for a while. "The boy needs the money in the first week," he said. "Bring me half, and come on Saturdays."

The harvest went out on the twenty-fifth, one and a half tons in the back of Aydın's truck, and the peppers were heavy and dark and better than anything Musa had grown in five years. He watched the truck turn onto the main road and then he walked back down to the low corner with a wheelbarrow.

For three days he carried stones from the wall and laid them along both sides of the open channel, the way his father had laid them, the way his father's father must have laid them before that. It took a long time, and his back hurt, and it made the field two rows smaller for ever.

Hale came down on the last afternoon and stood at the edge of the water with her hands in her pockets.

"The clouds are coming back," she said. "From the west again."

"Good," said Musa. He put the last stone in place and pressed it flat with his boot. "Let it come. It has somewhere to go now."
