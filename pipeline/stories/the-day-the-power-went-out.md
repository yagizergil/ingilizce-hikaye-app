# The Lights on Fig Street

The fan above the counter slowed, turned twice more, and stopped.

Elif looked up from the tablets she was counting, and the ceiling light died while the pen was still in her hand. Then the green light on the card machine went out, and behind her the fridge stopped humming. For a moment the street was so quiet that she could hear a baby crying somewhere above the bakery.

She pushed the shop door open. The air outside pressed against her face like a wet cloth. The whole of Fig Street was dark — the bakery, the phone shop, the lawyer's windows on the corner. Only car lights moved, and somewhere far away a car alarm was singing to nobody.

"Not again," Hüseyin said from the bakery door, with flour on his arms up to the elbow.

"How long did it take last time?"

"Twenty minutes." He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand. "But last time it was not forty-one degrees."

Elif went back inside and crossed the shop without switching on her phone light, because she knew exactly where everything stood. Four shelves, a glass door, and a small white thermometer taped to the middle shelf. Mr. Demir had shown her that thermometer on her very first day at the pharmacy. "Two degrees to eight," he said then. "Not one degree less, not one degree more."

She opened the door for three seconds, read the number, and shut it again. Five point one. Then she pressed her hand flat against the glass, as if the cold could be kept inside by holding it there.

She knew the shelves by heart. On the top one there was insulin for six families on the street, including Mrs. Ayla, who came in every Thursday and always asked about Elif's mother. Below that sat the eye drops for people who had just had operations. On the bottom shelf, in a blue plastic box with a paper seal, were the vaccines that the health centre nurse would collect at nine in the morning.

Elif called Mr. Demir. He answered on the second ring, from a hospital in another city, where his wife had been operated on that same morning.

"The power is out," Elif said. "The whole street, maybe more."

"Is the fridge closed?"

"Yes."

"Then leave it closed." His voice was calm, but she could hear him standing up. "A full fridge keeps the cold for about four hours in this heat, if nobody opens it. After four hours we have a problem."

"What kind of problem?"

"The insulin becomes water and the vaccines become rubbish. Elif, listen to me. If the power comes back inside four hours, we lost nothing. If it does not come back, you have to move everything somewhere cold before the four hours end."

She looked at the clock on her phone. It was ten past nine.

The electricity company's number gave her a recorded voice which said, twice, that they were working on the fault. Nothing else. Hüseyin came in with a bottle of water and his own phone in his hand, because his cousin worked for the company and answered family calls even at night.

"The big grey box near the water tower burned," Hüseyin said. "Not a small thing. He says four districts are out, and they will work all night."

"All night?"

"He says before morning is not likely."

Elif took an old price list from under the counter, turned it over, and wrote on the clean side: 21:10 — 5.1. Then she wrote a second line under it, and drew a box around a number she did not like at all.

01:10.

"That is my time," she said. "After that everything in that fridge is finished."

Hüseyin looked at the fridge and then at her. He was a large man who baked bread for nine hours a day and rarely hurried anywhere.

"So," he said, "where are we taking it?"

# The Wrong Door

The delivery boxes were in the back room, three white foam boxes that came with the medicine every Tuesday. Elif had kept them because she hated throwing away anything useful. In the freezer part of the small kitchen fridge there were four flat ice packs, and they had been frozen solid since the morning.

She loaded two of the foam boxes onto the hand cart. Batu, the bakery boy, held the cart while she tied a rope across the top.

"The state hospital," she told him. "They have a generator. I have seen their lights from the roof."

The walk took nineteen minutes, and it felt longer, because the pavements were full of people who had carried their chairs outside. Nobody could sleep in those flats without fans. Old men sat under the dark street lamps, women called to each other from balcony to balcony, and children ran between the parked cars in the dark. Halfway there, Elif turned a corner and saw the hospital sitting in its own square of yellow light, like a ship at sea.

The night pharmacist was a thin young man called Kerem, and he listened to her whole story without interrupting once. Then he shook his head.

"I can't put your stock in our fridge."

"It is one box. It will fit."

"It will not fit, because our shelves are full of our own things, and the generator is only feeding the operating rooms and two fridges." He turned his screen towards her, although it showed her nothing she understood. "And there is the other problem. If the health office checks that fridge tomorrow and finds boxes with another pharmacy's name inside, I lose my job. Those are not my rules."

Elif stood there with the rope still in her hand. She had walked for nineteen minutes with one plan in her head, and the plan had just closed like a door.

"Then give me a paper," she said. "A permission, a form, anything."

"The office opens at nine in the morning." Kerem looked at the clock, and then at her face, and something in him gave way a little. "Sit down. I will tell you what I know instead, and you should write it down."

He talked for six minutes while she wrote on the back of the price list.

Cold was not the same as frozen. Insulin that freezes is dead, he said, and a frozen vaccine is worse than a warm one, because it looks perfectly fine. Ice must never touch the medicine boxes directly, and it must be wrapped in paper or cloth. She should aim for four or five degrees, in the middle of the safe range, so that a small mistake in either direction would not kill anything.

"And write the temperature down every half hour," Kerem said. "With the time. Every half hour, all night."

"Why? Nobody will read that."

"The nurse will read it." He gave her a thermometer with a long thin wire, and two of the hospital's own frozen packs, which she was fairly sure he was not supposed to give away. "Vaccines travel with a temperature record. No record, no vaccines. If you carry that blue box through the night and cannot show what happened to it, the health centre has to throw it away. They will not risk eleven babies on your word."

Elif looked at the paper in her hand, then at the door.

It was five past ten. Her phone showed nine per cent, and she had three hours and five minutes left.

# Ice on Fig Street

By the time she got back, there were more people outside than before, and Hüseyin had already told half the street what was happening. That was the thing about Fig Street. Nothing stayed private for longer than twenty minutes.

"Ice," Elif said. "I need ice, and I need it wrapped, and I need boxes that hold the cold."

Selim, who sold fish two doors down, had shut his shop at seven but had not gone home. He had a metal box in his back room, thick and heavy, and it was still half full of the ice he had bought that morning.

"It will melt by three o'clock," he said. "Maybe four. Without power my machine makes nothing."

"Three o'clock is three hours away. I will take it."

Meral from the ice cream shop brought four foam boxes and two old blankets, and she was almost crying about her freezer, which was slowly dying behind her locked door. Hüseyin carried in a stack of newspapers from under his counter. Mrs. Ayla came down in her slippers with a cooking thermometer, the kind used for jam, and pressed it into Elif's hands as if it were a gift from her own kitchen.

They worked on the pharmacy floor by the light of six phones. Ice went into the bottom of each box, wrapped in newspaper until the packages looked like fish from the market. The medicine went into the middle, standing up, not lying down. Then more newspaper, then a blanket folded twice over the top, and the wire of Kerem's thermometer running out from under the lid like a tail.

At two minutes past eleven, Elif opened the fridge for the last time and moved everything across in ninety seconds. She wrote on the paper: 23:04 — insulin and drops, 5.6. Then: 23:06 — vaccines, 4.9.

"Now we wait?" Batu asked.

"Now we watch."

At twenty to twelve the vaccine box read nine tenths of a degree, and Elif's stomach turned over. She had put too much ice under the blue box, and the cold was going the wrong way. She pulled two packages out, pushed a folded newspaper into the space, and stood there with her hand on the lid, waiting. Three degrees. Then three point six. Then four point one, and she could breathe again.

That was when she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding. Selim's ice would be water by three. The power would not be back before morning. Between three o'clock and sunrise there was a hole in her plan four hours wide, and no shop in the district could sell her a single bag of ice.

She stood up so fast that Mrs. Ayla asked if she felt ill.

"The Hotel Deniz," Elif said. "Two streets up. They have a generator for the kitchen, because their guests must have hot water and cold drinks."

"Do you know anybody there?"

"The night manager buys throat tablets from me every winter. His name is Rauf."

She went with Batu at half past midnight, carrying two empty buckets through streets where the only light came from the hotel itself. Rauf was behind the desk with his tie loose, and he listened, and then he took her through a corridor to a kitchen where a machine was quietly making ice as if nothing at all had happened in the city.

"Every hour," he said, filling the first bucket. "Come to the back door. Do not use the front, because the guests are already asking me hard questions about the price of the rooms."

# The Paper on the Wall

The night became a list of numbers on the back of a price list.

01:00 — 4.4. 01:30 — 4.8. Elif sat on the floor between the boxes with her back against the counter and the thermometer wire across her knee. Hüseyin slept in the plastic chair by the window, and his snoring was the loudest sound on Fig Street.

At twenty to two somebody knocked on the glass. It was a man of about fifty, sweating through his shirt, holding an empty medicine box in front of him like a ticket.

"My mother," he said. "Her insulin finished this evening. The pharmacy on the main road is closed, and everything is dark, and I saw your phone light."

Elif lifted the blanket, took out one box, and wrote a new line on her paper: 01:41 — one insulin out, Cemal, for his mother, box open eleven seconds, 4.6 degrees. The man read the line upside down and did not understand a word of it, but he understood that it was being written for a reason.

"You are keeping a record," he said.

"Somebody will ask me tomorrow. I want to have the answer."

At three she and Batu made the second ice run, and at four they made the third. Rauf came out with them the third time and looked at the boxes on the pharmacy floor for a long moment.

"My father was diabetic," he said, and then he went back to his desk without saying anything more.

The bad moment came at ten past four. The insulin box had fallen to one point eight, because the new hotel ice was much colder than Selim's old ice, and Elif had not thought about that. She took out three packages, put a blanket between the ice and the medicine, and knelt there in the dark with her eyes on the little screen. The number climbed slowly, like something coming up out of deep water. Two point five. Three. Three point four.

Her hands were shaking, and she wrote 04:35 — 3.4 in handwriting she could barely read afterwards.

At twenty past five the ceiling light came on.

It came on with the fan, and the card machine, and the fridge, all at the same moment, and Hüseyin woke up shouting as if the shop had caught fire. Elif did not move anything for eleven minutes. She stood in front of the open fridge with the thermometer inside it, watching the temperature fall from twenty-nine degrees to eight, and then to six, and then to four point two. Only then did she carry the boxes back, one at a time, and only then did she write the last line on the paper.

She was still sitting on the floor at a quarter past eight when the nurse came in, and the floor was wet, and there were blankets and newspapers and empty buckets everywhere.

"Songül Hanım," Elif said, standing up too quickly. "The vaccines are here."

The nurse looked at the mess, and then at the blue box in Elif's hands, and her face closed. "There was no power in this district until dawn. I was told six hours."

"Eight hours and ten minutes."

"Then I cannot take them. I am sorry, but I cannot put anything into eleven babies if I don't know—"

Elif held out the price list. Thirty-four lines, times and temperatures, the words of a hospital pharmacist written across the top corner, one line about a man called Cemal and his mother.

Songül read it standing up. She read it twice, from the beginning, with one finger moving down the column of numbers. Then she took a pen from her bag and signed her name under the last line.

"Who taught you to do this?"

"A man at the hospital who could not help me," Elif said. "He helped me anyway."

Mr. Demir arrived a little before nine, straight from the bus, with his jacket over his arm and his wife's bag in his hand. He stood in the doorway and looked at the wet floor, the newspapers, the buckets, the blankets from the ice cream shop.

Elif waited for him to speak. She had thought, several times during the night, about what she would say if the answer was that she had done everything wrong.

He walked past her, picked up the price list, and read the whole of it while the fan turned above his head. Then he took a piece of tape from the drawer and stuck the paper on the wall beside the fridge, at eye level, where it would be impossible not to see.

"Two degrees to eight," he said. "Do not let anybody throw that away."
