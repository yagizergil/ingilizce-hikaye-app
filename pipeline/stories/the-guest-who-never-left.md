# Room Four in November

The man put the money on the desk and pushed it across with one finger. "One more week," he said.

Nesrin counted the notes twice, because the rain on the windows was loud and she did not want to make a mistake in front of him. It was the seventh week. She wrote the date in her black book, and under the date she wrote his name again: Bekir Aksu, room four.

"Do you want a receipt?"

"You ask me every week," he said, "and every week I say no."

He climbed the stairs slowly with one hand on the rail, and his door closed without any sound at all. He was perhaps sixty-five, thin, and always in the same grey coat. In seven weeks he had never complained about the heating, the noise of the pipes, or the smell of paint in the hall.

In November the town belonged to the wind. The two hotels on the beach road had closed in October, and the tea gardens had carried their chairs inside. The man who sold ice cream by the harbour was driving a bus in another city now. Nesrin kept the Deniz Guesthouse open all winter, because the bank did not care about the season. She had borrowed money to buy the place, and she had to pay the bank on the thirtieth of December, exactly as she paid every year. The bank did not ask for less when the beds were empty.

Six rooms stood empty. Room four did not.

Her son Emre was doing his homework at the end of the desk, because it was the warmest place in the building. He was seventeen, and he had opinions about everything, and most of them arrived without warning.

"He went out again last night," Emre said. "Half past nine. I heard the door."

"He is a guest, not a schoolboy."

"He came back at four in the morning, and there was sand in his shoes. I cleaned it up myself."

Nesrin put the money in the box under the desk and closed it. The guest in room four had a shape to his days, and she knew it well by now. He paid for breakfast and never came down to eat it. He left his towels folded outside his door, so she had not been inside his room since the first week. No letters came for him, and no one ever telephoned. He came down at eight in the evening, drank one glass of tea by the window, and waited for the sky to go dark. Then he went out.

"Nobody walks on the beach in November," Emre said.

"Then he is the only one, and the beach is quiet for him."

She said it in a firm voice, but she thought about it again while she washed the glasses. In her twelve years at the desk she had learned that people who pay in cash usually have a reason. Some of them are shy about their money, and some of them are hiding from a wife. One or two are hiding from something with a longer memory than a wife.

On Thursday afternoon the bell above the door rang, and Cemal came in shaking the rain off his cap. He had been the town's police officer for eleven years, and he had eaten more of Nesrin's soup than most of her guests.

"You heard about the post office?" he said.

"Everybody heard."

"Friday night. They took all the cash out of the back office, and they did not break a single window." He sat down without being asked. "So I am walking around the town asking a stupid question. Do you have anybody new? Anybody who came in October and stayed?"

Nesrin dried her hands on the cloth, and she thought about the grey coat and the folded towels.

"One guest," she said. "An old man. Very quiet."

"Name?"

She gave him the name, and Cemal wrote it in his notebook without much interest.

"Does he pay by card?"

"Cash," she said. "Every week."

Cemal stopped writing and looked up at her, and the room felt colder than the weather outside.

# Sand in His Shoes

After that afternoon Nesrin began to watch her own guest, and she hated herself a little for it.

She noticed that he stood in the hall some evenings in front of the old photograph beside the coats. It was a picture of the town's middle school, taken long before she came here, with rows of teachers in dark jackets and a hand-written date at the bottom. Bekir Aksu looked at that photograph the way people look at a road they used to drive.

"There is a light on that corner now," he said, still facing the wall. "There was no light for thirty years."

"You know the town?"

"I know some streets." He turned round and put on his coat. "The rain has stopped. That is good."

He asked her for a second key that week, because he did not want to wake the house when he came back late. She gave it to him, and he thanked her twice, which was more words than he usually spent in a day. Then she stood at the window and watched him walk towards the harbour road until the dark took him.

On Saturday she cleaned the whole first floor, and she stopped outside room four with the office key in her hand.

She was not a woman who went into guests' rooms. She had never read a letter or opened a suitcase in twelve years, and she had sent a cleaner away once for exactly that. But the thirtieth of December was coming, and the post office money was gone, and Cemal's face kept coming back to her while she carried the sheets downstairs.

The key turned easily, and the room was so tidy that it hurt to look at. The bed was made in a hard, square way, like a soldier's bed, and one shirt hung in the cupboard. A small radio sat on the table, turned off and pushed against the wall. There was no book, no photograph, no bottle, nothing that told her anything.

Then she saw the bag under the bed.

It was an old brown travel bag with a small lock, and the lock was hanging open. She got down on the cold floor and listened to the empty building for a long moment. If she stopped now, she could go downstairs and never think about it again. She did not stop.

Inside the bag there was money. Not a few notes for the week, but bundles with paper bands round them, packed tightly in two rows, more cash than she had seen in her whole working life. Under the money there was a photograph of a young man in a football shirt, laughing at somebody behind the camera. Under the photograph there was a folded paper with a name on it, Hasan Kılıç, and a street, and a number with too many zeros, and two signatures at the bottom.

She heard the front door.

Nesrin folded the paper the way she had found it, closed the bag, pushed it under the bed and locked the room. Her hands were shaking so much that the key sang against the metal plate. She was halfway down the stairs when Bekir Aksu came round the corner into the hall.

He had come back early. He was carrying bread, and there was rain on his shoulders, and he stopped when he saw her on the stairs with the key in her hand.

For three or four seconds nobody said anything. She had time to invent five different sentences and reject all of them.

"Good evening," he said at last.

"Good evening," she said. "I was checking the heating in number six."

He looked at her hand, and then at her face, and then he walked past her up the stairs. His door closed. It closed quietly, exactly as it always did, and somehow that was worse than any noise he could have made.

# What the Harbour Knew

She followed him the next night, and she took Emre with her, because she was not brave enough to go alone.

He left at half past nine and walked down the harbour road with his hands in his pockets. The street was wet and empty, and the closed tea garden by the water looked like a row of teeth with the chairs pushed against the wall. Nesrin and Emre kept fifty metres behind him, in the shadow of the fish market, and the wind covered the sound of their shoes.

At the end of the harbour wall a young man was waiting under the one working lamp.

He was thin, in a jacket too light for the weather, and he moved from foot to foot as though he had been standing there a long time. Bekir Aksu did not shake his hand or kiss him. He took an envelope out of his coat and gave it to him, and the young man opened it and counted the money right there in the light of the lamp. Then he pushed the envelope into his jacket and walked away towards the bus station without looking back once.

The old man stood alone at the wall for a while and watched the water.

"Mum," Emre said in a low voice. "That was money."

"I know what it was."

She did not sleep that night. At eight in the morning she walked to the police station in the rain, and she told Cemal everything. She told him about the cash every week, the walks after dark, the sand, the bag under the bed, the paper bands, the envelope at the harbour. She told him that she had gone into a guest's room, and he wrote that down too.

They came at eleven. Cemal was polite, and the young officer with him was polite, and Bekir Aksu did not argue about anything at all. He put on his grey coat, and he carried the brown bag downstairs himself. At the door he turned and looked at Nesrin for the first time since the stairs.

"You went into my room," he said.

Then he went out through the front door into the daylight, which she had never seen him do, and the police car took him away.

Emre was standing in the hall with a cloth in his hand, in front of the old school photograph, and he had gone completely white.

"Mum," he said. "Come here. Look at the second row."

She looked. The faces were small and grey, but the names were printed under the picture in tidy letters, and one of them was Bekir Aksu, mathematics.

"Dad's school," Emre said. "Dad's teacher. He told me about him. He said he was the one who used to buy shoes for the poor children and tell everybody the shoes came from the school."

Nesrin went back to the police station at four o'clock, and Cemal came out to the waiting room with two cups of tea and no good news.

"He will not explain the money," he said. "He says it is his. That is all he says. There is nothing at the bank, nothing on paper, and the notes are in bands, and the post office money was in bands too." He drank some tea. "The boy at the harbour, he says it was his son. He will not give me a name or an address for him. What can I do with that? We hold him while we check."

"He was a teacher here," Nesrin said. "For thirty-one years."

"I know what he was," Cemal said. "That is the part I do not like."

She walked home along the harbour road in the dark, past the lamp at the end of the wall. She had picked up a man's whole life and broken it against a table, and only one name could put it back.

# The Blue Gate

Hasan Kılıç. The street was one she half knew, up behind the old bakery, where the roads were narrow and the houses had gardens.

She found the number on Sunday morning with Emre beside her. The gate was blue and freshly painted, there was a lemon tree in the garden, and a new car stood where the tree's shade used to fall. A big man in working clothes was carrying a bag of cement across the yard, and he put it down when she called his name over the gate.

"I bought this house in October," Hasan Kılıç said. "Why?"

"Who did you buy it from?"

"The teacher." He cleaned his hands on his trousers. "Bekir Aksu. He taught my sister. He wanted cash, all of it in cash, and he wanted it in one day, and he did not want anybody in the town to know. I thought he was ashamed. I did not ask." He looked at her more carefully. "Has something happened to him?"

"He is at the police station," Nesrin said. "Do you have the paper?"

He had it in a drawer, with the lawyer's stamp and the date and both signatures. He put on a clean jacket and came with them in his own car without asking a single question more. Then he waited two hours in the police station with the paper on his knees.

They let Bekir Aksu out at six in the evening.

He came out with his bag in one hand, and Hasan Kılıç stood up and said, "Teacher," in a small voice. The old man touched his shoulder and thanked him. Then he saw Nesrin by the window, and his face closed like a door.

They walked back to the guesthouse together, because there was nowhere else for him to sleep. Not one word passed between them until they were in the empty dining room with the lights on.

"My son borrowed money," he said finally. "Not from a bank. From men who lend money in cafés and smile while they do it. He is not a bad boy. He is a stupid, frightened boy, and he signed things he did not read."

"And you sold your house."

"If I put that money in a bank account, those men can find it and take all of it in one afternoon. So I carry it, and I pay them in parts, and my son stands there with me while I do it, because he has to see what it costs." He put his hands flat on the table. "I taught in this town for thirty-one years. Half the people in the shops on that road call me teacher when I walk past. Should I go and stand in the daylight in front of my own blue gate and watch another man park his car under my tree?"

"So you waited for the dark."

"The dark is kinder," he said. "That is all my secret was."

Nesrin sat down opposite him, and she made herself say it plainly. A softer sentence would have been for her own comfort and not for his.

"I opened your bag," she said. "And then I went to the police. Both of those things were mine, and I did them, and I am sorry."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind pushed the rain against the window in one long wave.

"You also found Hasan Kılıç," he said. "Nobody asked you to do that."

She offered him back all seven weeks of money. He refused, and he told her that a room is worth what a room costs. He had slept warm every night while the rest of the town froze. Then he took an envelope out of his coat and paid for December and January before the time. She said that two months was too much, and he said that the thirtieth of December was coming for both of them.

On Monday afternoon, at four o'clock, in grey daylight, Bekir Aksu put on his coat. He walked out of the front door with Nesrin beside him, down the harbour road towards the fish market. A woman with a shopping bag stopped in the middle of the wet street.

"Teacher?" she said. "Is that you?"

"It is me," he said, and he stood still and let her see him.

That evening Nesrin wrote the date in her black book, and the name, and room four. Then she wrote a receipt, tore it out carefully along the line, and put it on the desk in front of him.

He read it, folded it once, and put it in his pocket.
