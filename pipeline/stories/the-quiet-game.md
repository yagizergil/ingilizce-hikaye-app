# The Old Board

Elif's grandfather kept a chess board on the small table by the window. The pieces were made of dark wood, and some of them were missing a little paint.

"Sit," he said. "We play before dinner, like always."

Elif sat down and looked at the board. She was eleven years old, and she played chess with her grandfather almost every day. He always won.

"One day I am going to beat you," she said.

Her grandfather smiled. "Maybe. But not today."

He moved a piece. Elif thought for a long time. She moved her horse to a new place.

"Bad move," her grandfather said. "Think again next time. Chess is not fast. It is slow and quiet."

"I do not like slow," Elif said.

"I know," he said. "That is why you lose."

They played for twenty minutes. In the end, her grandfather won again, easily. Elif crossed her arms.

"There is a school competition next month," she said. "In the city. Anyone can enter."

Her grandfather looked up. "A real competition? With other players?"

"Yes. My teacher told us today. The best three players go to the city hall and play all day."

"Are you going to enter?"

Elif looked at the board. "I do not know. I always lose to you."

"You lose to me because I have more years," her grandfather said. "I played chess before your mother was born. The other children your age know less than you."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you already understand the horse moves in an L shape. Most children your age do not understand that yet." He put the pieces back in their box. "Enter the competition, Elif. You have nothing to lose."

That night, Elif did not sleep well. She kept thinking about the black and white squares. She thought about the horse, the king, and the tall piece that moved only in straight lines.

The next day, she asked her grandfather to teach her more. He agreed, but he said something strange.

"I am going to teach you," he said, "but I am not going to play easy anymore. If you want to enter a real competition, you must learn to lose against a strong player first. Then a real competition will not scare you."

Elif did not like this idea. But she understood it.

"Fine," she said. "Play your hardest game."

Her grandfather nodded and set up the board again.

# Learning to Lose

For three weeks, Elif and her grandfather played every evening after dinner. He did not let her win, not even once. Some nights, she lost in fifteen moves. Other nights, she lasted much longer, and once she almost won.

"Almost is not winning," he said that night, but he was smiling.

"I moved my queen too early," Elif said. "I know that now."

"Yes. You are learning to see your own mistakes. That is more important than winning."

Elif started to write down her games in a small notebook. She wrote the moves and the mistakes next to them. She read old chess books from the library, with pictures of famous games from many years ago.

Her grandfather watched her read one evening. "You are working harder than I expected," he said.

"I want to win one game before the competition," Elif said. "Just one."

"Then let us play again."

They played, and this time Elif moved slowly and carefully. She did not rush her pieces forward. She waited for her grandfather to make a small mistake.

He did not make one for a long time. But near the end of the game, he moved a piece too fast, and Elif saw her chance. She moved her tall piece across the board.

"Check," she said.

Her grandfather looked at the board carefully. He moved his king away, but there was nowhere safe to go. Two moves later, the game ended.

"I win," Elif said in a small voice. She did not believe it.

Her grandfather sat back in his chair. "Yes," he said. "You win. Finally."

He did not look sad. He looked proud.

"Now," he said, "you are ready for the competition. Not because you can beat me. Because you know how to wait, and how to see a mistake before it happens."

The competition was in two weeks. Elif practiced every day, sometimes with her grandfather, sometimes alone with her notebook. She learned three new openings, ways to start a game safely. She learned how to protect her king early, so it was never in danger too soon.

On the last night before the competition, her grandfather gave her something small and heavy, wrapped in cloth.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Open it."

Inside was an old chess piece, a small dark horse, worn smooth from many years of touching.

"This was from my first chess set," he said. "Keep it in your pocket tomorrow. Not for luck. Just so you remember you already know how to play."

# The City Hall

The city hall was full of tables and quiet players. Elif's hands were cold when she sat down for her first game. Her opponent was a tall boy who did not look at her, only at the board.

They played for almost an hour. Elif made careful moves, just like she practiced. She lost two pieces early, but she did not panic. She remembered her grandfather's words: slow and quiet.

Near the end, the tall boy made a mistake with his queen. Elif saw it immediately, because she had studied this exact kind of mistake in her notebook. She won the game.

Her second game was harder. The girl across from her played very fast, moving pieces before Elif finished thinking. Elif felt her hands shake a little.

She touched her pocket. The small wooden horse was there.

She slowed down. She stopped watching the other player's fast hands and started watching only the board. Move by move, she found a safe path for her king and a slow attack toward the other side.

The game lasted two hours. In the end, Elif made one final move that trapped the other player's king completely.

"Checkmate," the judge said, looking at the board.

Elif had won her second game. She looked around the room. Only a few players had won two games. She was one of them.

Her third game, the last one of the day, was against an older boy from another school. He played very well, and Elif lost two pawns in the first ten minutes. She thought she was going to lose this game.

But she remembered something else her grandfather said: almost is not winning, but almost is not losing either. She kept playing, slow and careful, watching every move.

The game ended without a winner. Both players agreed it was a draw. Nobody trapped the other king.

At the end of the day, three players received small medals. Elif was not one of them. The best players had won all three games.

She walked outside to find her grandfather waiting by the door.

"I did not win a medal," she said.

"I know," he said. "I watched your second game from the back of the room."

"You watched? I did not see you."

"You were not looking at me. You were looking at the board. Good." He put a hand on her shoulder. "You won two games and drew one, against players who study every week. That is not a small thing."

Elif took the small wooden horse from her pocket and held it up.

"I want to enter again next year," she said.

Her grandfather laughed, a real, happy laugh. "Then we have work to do," he said. "Sit down when we get home. We are going to play, and this time, I am not going easy on you."

"Good," Elif said. "I do not want you to."
