# The Man in the Lobby

The guard called up at twenty past eight, when Elif had been at her desk for less than ten minutes.

"There is a man in the lobby who says he will wait all day, and he refuses to tell me what it is about."

Elif asked whether he wanted to place an advertisement or shout at somebody, since those were the only two reasons anyone came to the building in person any more.

"He has a folder," the guard said, "and in my experience the ones who bring folders are always the serious ones."

She took the stairs instead of the lift, partly to wake herself up and partly because the lift stopped on the fourth floor for the advertising staff. A man of about sixty sat on the bench beneath the wall of framed front pages, holding a plastic folder flat on his knees as though it might slide off. He stood as soon as he saw her, and he did it quickly, like somebody who had practised standing up politely for people who might refuse to listen.

"My name is Halit Emre, and in April 2013 your newspaper printed it on the front page, which is why I have carried that page around ever since."

Elif had spent every morning of that week in the archive, so the date reached her before the name did. The Yıldız textile works had burned on a Sunday night in April 2013, and nine workers had died on the second floor because the fire doors were locked from the outside. The City Record had run eleven days of reporting on the fire, and that reporting had made the reputation of the paper, of its editor, and of everyone who came up under him afterwards.

"Come upstairs," she said, "because whatever is inside that folder should not be opened on a bench in a lobby."

They sat in the small meeting room beside the news desk, where he opened the folder as carefully as a man opening a passport at a border. Inside lay a copy of the front page, soft at the folds, under a headline she could have repeated from memory: SITE MANAGER APPROVED FAULTY CABLES AT YILDIZ. Below the headline, in the second paragraph, sat a name.

"I was never the site manager, whatever that page says, because I operated a cutting machine on the ground floor for five months," he said. "One Thursday the store man was away, so when four boxes of cables came into the yard I signed for them, since somebody had to write a name on the paper."

"And the cables in that delivery were the ones that failed?"

"The cables that failed had been inside the walls for two years before I first walked into that building," he answered, in the flat voice of a sentence worn smooth by use. "But mine was the only name on any document that mentioned cables, so your newspaper printed it, and afterwards no factory in this city would give me work."

Elif turned the page over, although she already knew that nothing was printed on the back. She had been deputy editor for three years, which meant that most of the complaints about old stories eventually reached her desk, and almost all of them fell apart within an hour. This one was not falling apart. The delivery note he laid beside the page carried a date, a signature, and four boxes of cable, and it proved nothing whatever about a fire in a wall built two years earlier.

"Mr Emre, why now, when eleven years have passed and you are sitting in our lobby at seven in the morning?"

"Because on Monday the court decides whether the case against the company can go forward," he said, "and the families are asking for money, while I am only asking to have my name taken out of the record. The company's lawyer has put your front page into his papers as evidence, and my own lawyer told me something I did not want to hear. He said that a single printed correction from the newspaper that first named me would be worth more than anything I can say from a chair in that room."

Through the glass wall she could see Kemal Yalçın arriving with his coat over his arm, stopping at three desks in a row to say something that made people laugh. He had been editor of The City Record for twenty-two years and a reporter on it for eighteen years before that, and on Friday he would walk out of the building for the last time. Elif was writing the tribute for the special section, and she had spent the week reading his best work, with the Yıldız series sitting at the centre of it.

"I will read everything in this folder today," she said, "but I cannot promise you a correction, because I never promise what I have not checked. What I can promise is an answer before Friday."

"Friday," Halit repeated, and something in the way he said the word told her that he already knew what Friday meant inside that building.

After he had gone, she went up to the fifth floor, where the bound volumes and the old source files were kept in a room that smelled of dust and cold paper. The 2013 files stood exactly where she had left them the previous afternoon, and she pulled out the folder marked YILDIZ — DAY 3. She laid its contents in order along the table: the reporter's notes, two photographs, a company statement, a list of the dead. The pages were numbered by hand in the corner, the way Kemal had always insisted, and page three was not among them.

Having checked the folder twice, she went through the two files on either side of it, in case somebody had returned the page to the wrong place. It was not there either, but clipped inside the cover she found a strip of paper carrying four words in handwriting she had been reading corrections in for nine years.

One name only. Print.

# Four Days to Friday

Elif slept badly, and by half past seven the next morning she was on the telephone to Orhan Sarı, who had covered the fire as a reporter and now lived two hours up the coast with a garden he described at length to anybody who called him.

"The Yıldız files," he said, once she had finished explaining. "I always wondered which of you would open them in the end, and I am not surprised that it turned out to be you."

"That is not really an answer, Orhan, and you know it."

"Give me a moment, because I have kept this in a drawer for eleven years and the drawer is stiff." She heard him move something heavy, and when he spoke again he had slowed right down. "On the night before we printed the third day, a letter arrived by hand for Kemal from Bahri Koç, one of the city safety inspectors. I know that, because I signed for it at the desk downstairs and carried it up the stairs myself."

"Did you read it while you were carrying it?"

"I read the first three lines on the way up, which I am not proud of, but I read them," he said. "It said that the wiring in the north wall had been put in during the rebuilding two years earlier, and that the inspector's own report from that time had been changed before it was filed. Then Kemal took the letter, thanked me, and told me that the third day was closed and I should go home."

"And the next morning we printed a machine operator's name on the front page."

"The next morning we printed a name that the company's lawyer had handed us the day before, together with a copy of a delivery note," Orhan said, sounding tired rather than angry. "I asked about the letter twice, and Kemal told me that it did not change the story and that I could see it once it had been checked. I never saw it, and two weeks later we were writing about the fire doors, when the whole city stopped caring about one signature on one piece of paper."

Elif sat with that for a while after she had hung up. At nine o'clock the owner of the paper came out of the lift with two visitors and a folder of her own. Serpil Doğan owned three regional papers and a printing works, and she had the habit of asking questions to which she already knew the answers.

"The retirement section," she said, stopping at Elif's desk. "Twelve pages, and I want the whole of the Yıldız series inside it, because that is the work people in this city actually remember."

"It is already in the plan, and the pages went to production last night."

"Good." Serpil looked at her for slightly longer than the conversation required. "The board meets on Monday afternoon, and I would like your name to be the one I carry into that room. I would also like to be able to tell them that the paper has had a quiet, clean handover. Do you understand what I am saying to you?"

"I understand it well enough to be uncomfortable."

"Then have a good week," Serpil said, and walked on towards the lift without waiting for anything further.

At one o'clock Kemal took Elif to the fish restaurant behind the port, the one where he had taken her on her first day, on the day her mother died, and on the day three years ago when she had almost lost her job.

"You have the face of somebody who is writing about me, which is a terrible thing to ask of any person," he said, breaking bread. "Just write that I was difficult but usually right, and put in the story about the mayor and the lift."

"Four separate people have already told me the story about the mayor and the lift, which is three more than any story needs."

"Then it must be true." He ate for a while before speaking again, and when he did his voice had changed. "Forty years, Elif. When I started here we set the type by hand and the whole building shook when the presses ran, and now I cannot open the file system without help from a boy who was born in the year I became editor."

"You could stay two more years, and Serpil would sign the papers tomorrow morning."

"A newspaper is not a room you sit in until you die," he said, putting down his fork. "You leave while the thing you built is still standing, and you let the next person put a different chair behind the desk. You will hate the first six months, because everybody will tell you what I would have done, and half of them will be lying."

She had a decision in her pocket and a lunch table between them, and she could not make the two things meet. Instead she asked him whether he remembered the water contracts, which was a way of asking something else entirely.

"I remember every correction this paper has ever printed, because remembering them is the job."

"I put a number in that story that was wrong by a factor of ten, and a family had journalists outside their door for a week because of it," she said. "You went into the board meeting and told them that you had approved the copy without checking it properly, which was not true."

"I had approved it, and approval is not a small word."

"You read it after it was printed, and we both know that." Elif turned her glass slowly on the tablecloth. "You took that correction for me, and that is the only reason I still work in this building."

Kemal looked out at the boats for a moment. "You were twenty-eight and you were worth keeping, so that is not a debt," he said. "You cannot run a newsroom on debts, because sooner or later somebody arrives to collect one, and it is always the paper that pays."

Walking back, he stopped where the tram lines crossed and asked her, with no particular weight in his voice, whether the man in the lobby the previous morning had been complaining about the property pages.

"He was complaining about something a good deal older than the property pages."

"They usually are," Kemal said, and crossed the road ahead of her.

# What the Inspector Wrote

Bahri Koç had died in 2019, and his daughter Sevgi worked in the accounts office of a bus company on the east side of the city. She agreed to meet on Thursday morning in a tea garden near her office, because, as she said on the telephone, she did not want the conversation inside a building where people knew her.

"You are eleven years late," Sevgi said, before Elif had properly sat down.

"I know that, and there is no version of this in which I am not."

"My father wrote to your editor and received nothing, then wrote to the ministry and received a letter thanking him for his interest," she said. "After that he was moved from inspections to licences, which for a man like him was the same as being told to sit in a corner until he retired."

She wrapped both hands around her glass and went on more quietly. "He kept a copy of everything, because that was the kind of man he was, and I have carried that copy to two lawyers. Both of them explained to me that a copy without the original is worth very little in any courtroom."

The letter ran to two typed pages, dated the tenth of April 2013, and Elif read it three times before she trusted herself to speak. It stated that the electrical work in the north wall had been carried out during the rebuilding of 2011, by a building firm following a design that the company's own engineer had approved. It stated that Bahri Koç had recorded a fault in that work at the time, that his report had gone up to the district office, and that the version later placed in the file no longer contained the section about the north wall. It stated, in a single line that Elif read aloud without meaning to, that no cable delivered in 2013 had any connection whatever with the fault.

The final sentence was addressed directly to the editor. If you print the name on that delivery note, you will be printing the name the company has chosen for you.

"Why did your father keep coming back to a newspaper, after the ministry and after the lawyers had finished with him?"

"He used to say that a file can be changed but a newspaper cannot," Sevgi answered. "Once a thing is printed, a hundred thousand copies of it exist in a hundred thousand kitchens, and nobody can go around collecting them again. He believed that this made you powerful, and he was very disappointed in you."

Elif returned to the office at noon and learned that Kemal was out until three at a farewell lunch given by the chamber of trade. In nine years she had never once opened his desk, and when she opened it now the third drawer was not locked, which somehow made the whole thing worse.

The original of Bahri Koç's letter lay near the front in a brown envelope, with a page of Kemal's own notes clipped to it in pencil. Having read the notes twice, Elif carried them to the window, because the light at the desk had stopped seeming strong enough for the words on the page.

The notes were dated the eleventh of April 2013 and recorded, in the short flat style he used for everything, an offer made to him that afternoon by the company's lawyer. If the paper printed the name on the delivery note in the following day's edition, the lawyer would hand over the full internal safety file for all four Yıldız buildings, including the maintenance records for the fire doors and the letters about the locks. If the paper refused, the file would disappear into a court case that might run for six years.

Underneath that, in the same pencil, Kemal had written three lines. Doors killed them, and doors are the story. One name only — a delivery note, no crime, and he will be cleared inside a month by anybody who bothers to look.

Nobody had bothered to look, and that was the part that made her hands cold. He had priced a stranger's working life at one month of trouble, he had been wrong, and the file he bought with it had closed all four buildings within a fortnight. It had put two company directors in front of a judge, and it had produced the city rules on fire doors that were now fixed to a plate in every factory stairwell she had ever walked through.

Elif sat down on the floor beside the desk with the envelope on her knees, in exactly the position in which Halit Emre had held his folder.

She was still there when Kemal came in at half past two, earlier than he had promised, smelling of cigarette smoke from the restaurant terrace. He stopped in the doorway, looked at the open drawer, then at the envelope, then at her face, and he did not pretend for even a second.

"You have been to see Koç's daughter, which means you have the letter and probably rather more than the letter."

"I saw her this morning, in a tea garden she chose because she did not want to be overheard."

"She has been writing to me for five years, and I have answered none of it," he said, hanging his coat on the hook with great care, as though that were the only task in front of him. "Get up off my floor, Elif. If we are going to have this conversation, we should have it sitting in chairs like two adults."

# The Offer

"Ask me your questions, because you have earned them, and I would rather answer them than watch you work them out in front of me."

"Did you know that he was innocent on the night you printed his name?"

"I knew that the delivery note proved nothing, which is not quite the same sentence, although it is close enough for this afternoon." He folded his hands on the desk. "I made a calculation, and I have made thousands of them, most of which nobody ever hears about. Nine women died behind a locked door on the second floor, the company intended to bury that door in a court case for six years, and I was offered the file that would stop them. The price was one paragraph naming a man against whom nothing had been proved."

"You wrote in your own notes that he would be cleared inside a month."

"I did, and I have thought about that sentence more often than about anything else I have written," Kemal said, in a voice that stayed entirely level. "I believed the company would produce the site manager's signature within weeks in order to protect itself, that we would then print a small correction, and that the whole business would pass. Instead the company let him carry it, because a machine operator without a lawyer was the cheapest wall they had available."

"You could have printed the correction yourself in any week of the last eleven years."

"That is perfectly true, and I have no answer to it that will satisfy you."

"Then give me the one that satisfies you."

"By the second year the correction was no longer one paragraph," he said, looking away from her for the first time. "By then it was a paragraph admitting that this newspaper had accepted a document from a company lawyer in exchange for a man's name. Print that, and everything else in those eleven days becomes something a defence lawyer can wave in the air, and there are people living in rebuilt buildings because of those eleven days."

Elif said nothing at all, which was harder than speaking.

"There is something else, and you may as well have it now," Kemal continued. "In 2016 I found out that he had a son who had left school at fifteen, because the family could no longer manage. There is a bank order from my own account to a technical college in the east district, which ran for four years, and the college was told that the money came from a fund for the families of the fire."

Something in Elif turned over slowly. "You paid the son's fees in secret while keeping the father's name on our front page."

"You may say it in exactly that tone, because it is the correct tone for the facts."

"I am going to print the correction on Friday," she said. "It will go on page one, with the letter, with the offer from the lawyer, and with your name in it, because your name is the thing that makes it true."

"Friday is my last edition, and you know that better than anybody in this building."

"I know precisely what Friday is, which is why I am telling you now rather than on Friday morning."

Kemal was quiet for a long moment before he answered. "Wait one month, and I will sign a full statement in the week after I leave. It can go to his lawyer, to the court, to the ministry, to anyone you choose, and you will have every word you want. This newspaper will not lose the man who is retiring on the same page as the mistake he made."

"His case is heard on Monday morning, in four days," Elif said. "A statement in a month is a statement about a case that has already been decided."

"Then his lawyer asks the court for more time, which lawyers do every day of the week."

"You believe that will work about as much as I do, which is not at all."

"No," Kemal admitted. He turned his chair slightly, and when he spoke again his tone had become something she had never heard from him and did not want to hear. "Serpil spoke to you on Wednesday morning, and I know that because she spoke to me on Tuesday, when I told her there was nobody else in this building worth putting behind this desk. Whatever she said to you about Monday afternoon, I am the one who put it there."

The room seemed to grow smaller around the two of them.

"That is not fair, and you know exactly how unfair it is."

"It is completely fair, and it is also true, and you have spent three days telling me that truth is the only thing that matters." He raised one hand before she could answer. "I am not offering you money and I am not buying you. I am describing the shape of the thing. Print it on Friday and the board will not hand this paper to the deputy who put a knife through the retirement section. They will bring somebody in from outside, and that person will cut the news desk by four people inside a year, because that is what Serpil has wanted since the summer."

"So the price of one man's name is my job and four other jobs."

"The price is never what you expect, and that is the only useful thing I have to teach anybody." He stood up and looked out at the newsroom, at the young reporters and the empty chairs. "Go and see him this evening, not his lawyer but the man himself. If you are going to spend other people's lives, you should at least look at the face of the person you are spending them on."

She went at eight. Halit Emre lived in two rooms above a repair shop, and he made tea and apologised twice for the noise coming up from the street.

Elif told him everything in order, without protecting anybody, including herself. She told him about the inspector's letter, about the offer from the company lawyer, about the fire doors, and about the college fees paid quietly for four years. When she had finished, he sat for a while with his hands flat on his knees.

"I can print a short correction on page nine, in three lines, with no names and no explanation," she said. "Your lawyer can carry it into court on Monday, and it will still say that this newspaper was wrong about you. It is worth something real, and it costs the paper almost nothing."

"And the other one, the one you have not offered me yet?"

"The other one explains how it happened and what it was traded for. Everybody in this city will read it, and it will end the career of the best editor this paper has ever had, four days before he leaves the building."

Halit went to the shelf and came back with a photograph of a young man in a work jacket, standing in front of a machine.

"My son is twenty-six, and he believes that his college was paid for by a fund for the families of the dead," he said, holding the photograph in a hand that was not steady. "He also believes that his father signed something bad and was careless with nine lives, because that is what was printed, and I could never put a page in front of him that said otherwise. Print your three lines and he will read that a newspaper regrets an error. Print the whole thing and he will read what was done to me, and by whom. I have waited eleven years to stop being careless inside my own house, so please do not offer me the small one again."

# The Late Edition

Friday was the longest day Elif had ever spent inside that building.

She wrote the piece between six and nine in the morning, alone on the fifth floor among the bound volumes, and then she rewrote it twice, because the first version was angry and the second was frightened. The third version was neither of those things. It ran to eleven hundred words, it began with a delivery note for four boxes of cables, and it ended with the date on which the city fire door rules had come into force. Not one line in it suggested that the second fact excused the first.

At ten she carried it to the paper's lawyer, who read it standing up, sat down, and then read it again from the beginning.

"The company will not go to court over this, because everything here is documented and most of the documents are theirs," he said at last. "Koç's daughter will not, and Emre certainly will not. Serpil will not either, because she owns us, so she will simply be extremely unhappy, and she will be unhappy at you rather than at the page."

"Is any single line of it legally wrong?"

"Not one, which is exactly what makes it such a serious problem." He handed the pages back across the desk. "You should know that I told him in 2013 to print the correction and take the loss, and he answered that the loss would land on the series instead. I have wondered ever since whether he was right about that."

Serpil arrived at four, having been telephoned by somebody, and the conversation in the glass office lasted eighteen minutes. She never raised her voice once, which was considerably harder to stand than shouting.

"You are asking me to allow the front page of the retirement edition to say that this newspaper sold a man's name in exchange for a file."

"The front page will say that this newspaper corrects its own record even when the correction is expensive," Elif answered, "which is the only reason anybody keeps a newspaper alive for a hundred years."

"Or you could run it on Monday, after the handover, when it is not standing beside twelve pages of tribute."

"Monday morning is the hearing, and by Monday afternoon it is a piece of history."

"Yes, I did understand that the first time you explained it." Serpil looked down at the pages for a long moment. "I am not going to stop you, and I want that clearly on the record, because in three years, when somebody writes about this evening, I would like to be the person who did not stop you. I am also not carrying your name into the board room on Monday, and we both know that those two decisions belong to the same afternoon."

"I worked that out somewhere around Wednesday, and I came in this morning anyway."

"Then we understand one another." At the door she paused. "He should have printed it in 2013, which does not make you kind. It only makes you right, and the two are not the same thing."

The night desk found out at half past eight, when Ceyda from production came up to ask why page one had been pulled apart four hours before the print run. The newsroom went very quiet as the story travelled around it, and then it stopped being quiet at all. It filled instead with the particular noise of eleven people rebuilding a front page in three hours while pretending that nothing unusual was happening.

Kemal came in at ten minutes to eleven, having spent the evening at his own farewell dinner across the square, with people in coats still waiting for him down in the lobby. He walked to Ceyda's screen and read the page from the top to the bottom with his hands behind his back, exactly as he had read every front page for twenty-two years. Nobody in the room moved while he was reading.

"The second paragraph is too long, so split it after the date," he said. "You have also written that the newspaper decided. This newspaper does not decide anything by itself, so put my name into that sentence, because I am the person who decided."

Ceyda looked across at Elif, and Elif nodded once.

"The rest of it is clean," Kemal said, straightening up. "Print it."

Then he walked into his office, took his coat and two photographs off the wall, and left by the side stairs, and Elif did not see him again that night.

The presses ran at twenty past midnight, and she stayed to watch them from the metal walkway above the machines, with the noise going straight through her chest. At two in the morning she carried a folded copy across the city and pushed it under the door above the repair shop, because she could not reasonably knock at that hour.

On Monday the court accepted the correction into the record and gave the families' lawyers a further six weeks. Halit Emre's own claim against the company is still not settled and may not be settled for years. What changed that morning was smaller than justice and not small at all: in every file, every archive, and every online copy of that front page, his name now travels with a second document attached to it.

On Monday afternoon the board appointed an editor from the paper in the next province, a careful man of fifty who arrived with his own coffee cup. Elif is still deputy editor, and she has kept the news desk at eleven people through two budget meetings, although she expects to lose that argument in the end.

An envelope reached her desk on the Tuesday, in the internal post, with no note inside it. It held two typed pages, undated and unsigned, in the flat short style she knew as well as her own handwriting. It was a correction, and it named the delivery note, the lawyer, the offer and the man. It had never been printed, and from the softness of the folds it had been taken out of a drawer and read a great many times.

She has not asked him about it, and he has not called her. Some corrections take longer than four days, and a few of them are never printed at all. The one that mattered went out on that Friday night in a hundred thousand copies, and nobody in this city can go around collecting them.
