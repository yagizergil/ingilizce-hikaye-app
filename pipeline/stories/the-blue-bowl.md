# The Empty Flat

Deniz put the last box by the door and sat on it. Her arms hurt. The flat looked strange without the chairs. Every sound came back from the walls.

"Is that all of it?" her mother called from the kitchen.

"Two more in Grandma's room," Deniz said.

Her grandmother sat by the window in the only chair. She was eighty-one. She wore her good coat, because she always wore her good coat when something important happened. Today was important. Today she was leaving this flat after forty-six years.

"They are going to take the window too," her grandmother said.

"They are not going to take the window, Grandma."

"They are going to take the building. The window is in the building." She smiled a little at her own joke. Then she looked out at the street again.

Deniz went into the small bedroom. The bed was gone. Only two boxes stood in the middle of the floor, and both were full of clothes. She carried them out, one by one.

Her uncle Kemal came up the stairs at four o'clock. He was always fast and always late at the same time. He looked at his watch, then at the boxes.

"Is this everything?" he said.

"Yes."

"Good. The truck man wants to leave before six."

They carried the boxes down. The stairs were narrow and Deniz hit her knee twice. On the street, a small blue truck waited with its back open. Kemal pushed the boxes inside and closed the door.

"Where is the box from the kitchen shelf?" Deniz asked.

Kemal was already looking at his phone. "Which box?"

"The old one. With the cups and the plates and Grandma's bowl."

"Oh," he said. "I gave that to the man."

Deniz stopped. "What man?"

"The man who buys old things. He came this morning with a cart. He asked about the old kitchen things. I gave him the box and he gave me two hundred lira." Kemal put his phone in his pocket. "It was full of broken cups, Deniz. Nobody wants broken cups."

"The blue bowl was in that box."

"What blue bowl?"

Deniz did not answer. She turned and ran up the stairs.

Her grandmother was still by the window. Deniz stood in the door and tried to breathe normally.

"Grandma, where is your blue bowl?"

"In the kitchen box."

"Are you sure?"

"I put it there myself. On Tuesday. I put a cloth around it." Her grandmother looked up. "Why?"

Deniz sat down on the floor next to the chair. The floor was cold and dusty.

"No reason," she said. "I am going to find it in the truck."

The blue bowl was not a beautiful thing. It was big and heavy and the blue was dark, almost black at the bottom. There was a white bird painted on one side. The bird had a long neck and one eye, because the other eye came off many years ago.

Deniz's great-grandmother carried that bowl from her village in a bag on her back. She was nineteen. The bowl came out of the cupboard once a year, on her grandmother's birthday, and the rice always came to the table in it. Always. Deniz ate from that bowl every year of her life.

Her grandmother's birthday was on Sunday. Today was Thursday.

Downstairs, the truck started. Deniz kissed her grandmother's cheek and went down again. Kemal stood by the door with the key in his hand.

"Uncle Kemal. Where does the man live?"

"Which man? The truck man?"

"The man with the cart. The one who took the box."

Kemal looked at her. "Deniz. It is only old plates."

"Where?"

He put his hands up. "I do not know. He said he has a shop near the old bus station. Behind the bread factory, I think. He gave me a card." He looked in his pockets and found nothing. "I threw it away."

"The old bus station," Deniz said. "Behind the bread factory."

"Deniz—"

But she was already walking to the corner, fast, with her hands in her pockets.

# The Shop Behind the Bread Factory

Umut was in the yard with a ball when Deniz found him. He was her cousin and he was two years younger and he never said no to anything. That was useful today.

"Do you know the old bus station?" she asked.

"Everybody knows the old bus station."

"Can you take me there tomorrow morning? Early."

Umut looked at the ball. "How early?"

"Seven."

"Seven is not morning. Seven is night."

"Your father sold Grandma's bowl," Deniz said.

Umut stopped kicking the ball. "The bird one?"

"Yes."

"I am going to come at seven," he said.

They took two buses. The second bus was old and full and it smelled of wet coats. Deniz counted her money again in her pocket. She had four hundred and twenty lira. She got it in small pieces over two years, from birthdays and from cleaning a neighbour's stairs.

The old bus station was not a station anymore. Grass grew between the stones. Three dogs slept in the sun. The bread factory stood behind it, big and grey, and the whole street smelled warm and good.

They walked past the factory. Behind it they found a row of small shops. Two were closed. The third had things outside on the ground: chairs, a door, a bicycle wheel, a box of shoes.

An old man sat inside on a wooden chair. He was drinking tea from a small glass.

"Excuse me," Deniz said. "Yesterday morning you bought a box. From a flat on Kızılay Street. Kitchen things."

The man looked at her for a long time. "I buy twenty boxes every day."

"There was a blue bowl in it. Big. With a white bird."

"Ah." He put his tea down. "Yes. The bird."

Deniz's heart moved in a strange way. "Do you have it?"

"No."

"No?"

"I sold it. Yesterday, at two o'clock." He stood up slowly and walked to the back of the shop. "I sell fast. That is the work. A thing comes in the morning and it goes in the afternoon."

"Who bought it?"

"A woman."

"Which woman?"

The man smiled a small smile. "You ask a lot of questions."

"Please," Deniz said. "It is my grandmother's bowl. Her mother carried it from a village. It is not a plate for me. It is—" She stopped, because her voice was not working well.

Umut spoke for the first time. "Her grandmother is eighty-one. Her birthday is on Sunday."

The old man looked at the ceiling. Then he took a small book from a shelf and opened it. There were numbers and short words on every page, in blue ink.

"Two bowls yesterday," he said. "One went to a man with a restaurant. He took ten plates too. The other one, the blue one, the woman took." He put his finger on a line. "Sema. The tea place on the market street. She buys from me every month."

"Which market street?"

"There is one market street," the man said. "Go past the mosque and turn left. Small place. Yellow chairs outside."

Deniz was already at the door. Then she turned back.

"How much money did she pay?"

The old man looked at her over his glasses.

"Not much," he said. "But now it is her bowl, and people do not like to give things back. Be careful. Do not shout at her."

"I am not going to shout."

"Everybody says that."

They walked to the market street. It took twenty-five minutes. The sun was high now and Deniz's shirt stuck to her back.

The tea place was very small: one room, four tables inside, six yellow chairs outside on the stones. A woman of about fifty stood at the big table by the wall, washing glasses in hot water. Her arms were red to the elbow.

And there, next to her, on the wooden shelf, sat the blue bowl. It was full of sugar. The white bird looked at the street with its one eye.

Umut opened his mouth. Deniz put her hand on his arm.

"Wait," she said quietly.

"Why?"

"Because I am going to do this the right way."

# Sunday at Two O'Clock

Deniz sat down at a yellow chair outside and ordered two teas. Umut looked at her like she was ill.

"We are drinking tea?"

"We are drinking tea."

The woman brought the glasses on a small round tray. Deniz put the money on the table and looked up.

"Excuse me. The blue bowl on your shelf. Can I ask about it?"

The woman looked at the shelf, then at Deniz. "What about it?"

"Yesterday you bought it from the shop behind the bread factory."

"Yes."

"It is my grandmother's bowl," Deniz said. "My uncle sold it by mistake. He did not know."

The woman dried her hands on her apron. She did not look angry. She did not look friendly either.

"People say that a lot," she said. "Every week somebody wants something back."

"I can pay. I have four hundred and twenty lira."

"I did not pay four hundred and twenty lira."

"Then take four hundred and twenty."

The woman sat down on the next chair. She was tired, and now Deniz saw it — the wet shoes, the long day, the grey in her hair.

"Listen to me," the woman said. "The bowl is good and heavy. Sugar stays dry in it. My old one broke last month. So I need it. Money is not the problem."

Deniz put her hands around her tea glass.

"My great-grandmother carried it from her village," she said. "In a bag, on her back. She walked for two days. She was nineteen and she took three things: a coat, a photograph, and that bowl."

The woman said nothing.

"My grandmother is eighty-one on Sunday. Every year we eat together, and the rice comes to the table in that bowl. Every year of my life. She is in a new flat now. Everything is different. All the furniture is gone." Deniz stopped for a moment. "I want one thing on that table to be the same thing."

A car went past. Somewhere a radio played.

"The bird has one eye," the woman said suddenly.

"The other one came off. My grandmother says her brother did it with a spoon in 1954."

The woman laughed. It was a short, surprised laugh. Then she stood up and walked inside. Deniz watched her through the open door. She took the bowl off the shelf and looked at the bottom of it for a long time.

She came back out with the bowl in her hands, but she did not give it to Deniz.

"I have a problem," she said. "Saturday is my busy day. Market day. Two hundred glasses of tea, and I am alone here. My son is in the army."

Deniz understood. She stood up.

"I can work on Saturday," she said. "All day. Washing, carrying, cleaning tables."

"Me too," Umut said.

The woman looked at the boy. "You are small."

"I am fourteen," Umut said, which was not true.

"He is twelve," Deniz said. "But he is strong and he is going to do what I say."

The woman thought about it. Then she put the bowl on the table.

"Saturday. Seven in the morning to seven at night. You take the bowl home on Saturday night, and you keep your money." She pointed at Deniz. "And do not tell your uncle he is stupid. Everybody sells something by mistake. My mother sold my father's watch in 1998."

Saturday was the longest day of Deniz's life. She washed glasses until her fingers went soft and white. She carried trays outside forty times. Umut broke one plate and then carried the boxes of empty bottles to the back without a word all afternoon.

At seven o'clock the woman gave them each a plate of rice and a big glass of tea. Then she put the blue bowl on the table with an old newspaper around it.

"Go," she said. "It is late."

"Thank you," Deniz said.

"Come back in the summer. I am going to need help again in July."

On Sunday at two o'clock the family sat down in the new flat. It was smaller than the old one, and the light came from the wrong side, and Kemal's chair was too big for the room. Nobody talked about the old building.

Deniz's mother carried the rice in from the kitchen.

Her grandmother looked at the table. She looked at the bowl. Then she looked at Deniz, and Deniz looked back, and neither of them said anything at all.

"Well," her grandmother said at last. "The bird came to the new house."

"Yes."

"Birds do that." She took the big spoon and started to give out the rice. One plate at a time, in the same order, like every year. The youngest first, then the guests, then herself. Her hands were slow but they did not shake.

Kemal ate two plates and said the rice was very good. Nobody told him anything else.
