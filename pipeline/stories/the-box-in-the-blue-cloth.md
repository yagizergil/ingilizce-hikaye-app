# A Promise in the Hospital

The nurse said ten minutes, but Zeynep's grandfather talked for twenty.

He talked about the weather. He talked about the food, which was terrible, and about the man in the next bed, who was also terrible. He talked about the tomatoes on his balcony. Nobody was watering them.

"I am watering them," Zeynep said. "Every second day."

"Not enough."

"Every day, then."

"Too much." He put his head back on the pillow and closed his eyes. "You are like your mother. You go too fast or too slow."

Zeynep was fifteen and she knew this game. She waited. Her grandfather always talked about small things for a long time before he said the real thing.

The room was hot. Outside the window a bird sat on a wire. Somewhere down the corridor a machine made a soft sound, again and again.

"Take my bag," her grandfather said.

"Which bag?"

"Under the bed."

She found it: a brown bag, old and soft, with a broken zip. Inside there were his glasses, a newspaper from Tuesday, and a small box in a blue cloth.

"Give it to me."

She gave it to him. He put it on his chest and held it with both hands. It was a wooden box, about as big as a loaf of bread, dark and smooth on the top.

"Do you know your Aunt Nur's town?" he said.

"Kaya. I go there every summer."

"Yes. Now listen to me." He opened his eyes and looked at her, and his eyes were not old at all. "In Kaya there is a man called Halil Duran. He lives on a street near the water. Number eleven, Firat Street. I want you to take this box to him."

Zeynep looked at the box. "Who is he?"

"My brother."

The bird outside flew away from the wire. Zeynep sat down slowly on the plastic chair.

"You do not have a brother," she said.

"I have a brother. He is seventy-six and he is angry with me since 1983."

"Since—" Zeynep stopped. "Grandad. That is forty-two years."

"Forty-two years and four months," her grandfather said. "I can count."

"Why?"

"That is not your business."

"You want me to take a box to a man and I cannot ask why?"

Her grandfather made a small sound in his throat. Then he moved the cloth off the box with his hurt hand. He lost two fingers at the factory in 1979. Zeynep never asked about that either.

"Our father had a shop," he said. "When he died, there were two things. The shop, and this box. I took the shop. I said I am going to give Halil the box, and then I did not give it to him. I put it in a cupboard. Then I moved house, and the box went with me, and the years went too."

"What is in it?"

"Open it."

She opened it. Inside there were photographs — maybe thirty of them, brown and grey and very old. A man with a cart. A woman in a white dress by a door. Two small boys on a wall, one fat, one thin, both laughing.

"Every picture of our mother is in that box," her grandfather said. "He does not have one. Not one. He asked me for them in 1983 and I said no. I was angry about the shop. I was also stupid. Both of those things are true."

Zeynep closed the box very carefully.

"Take it to him," her grandfather said. "You go to Kaya on Friday with your aunt. I know this, because your mother talked about it on the phone for one hour."

"Grandad, why not the post office? You can send it."

"He is not going to open a box from me. He is going to look at my name and put it in the bin." The old man breathed for a moment. "But he is going to open the door for a girl. That is how he is."

"And if he does not open it?"

"Then you bring it home and I am not going to say anything about it again."

The nurse came to the door and pointed at her watch.

"One thing," her grandfather said, and he held Zeynep's arm. "Do not tell your mother."

"Why not?"

"Because she is going to say no. And then you must say yes to her and no to me, and that is not fair to you."

Zeynep put the box in her school bag. It was heavier than it looked.

"Ten minutes," she said. "You talked for twenty-two."

"I am old," her grandfather said. "Nobody stops me."

# The Wrong Number Eleven

Kaya smelled of salt and hot stone. Zeynep's aunt lived in a flat above a bakery, and the whole building smelled of bread from four in the morning.

On Saturday Zeynep said she wanted to walk to the harbour. Her aunt gave her a hat, some money and a long list of things not to do. Then Zeynep walked, with the box in her bag, past the bakery and the school and the small green mosque, down to the water.

Firat Street was easy to find. It went along the back of the harbour, narrow and grey, with washing hanging between the windows.

Number eleven was a blue door with a broken bell.

She stood in front of it for a long time. Then she made her hand into a fist and hit the door three times.

A young woman opened it. She had a baby on her arm and flour on her hands.

"Yes?"

"Excuse me. Is Halil Duran here?"

"Who?"

"Halil Duran. An old man."

"There is no old man here," the woman said. "Only us. Three years now."

Zeynep's mouth went dry. "Do you know where he went?"

"I do not know that name." The woman looked back into the flat, then at Zeynep again. "Ask at the tea house on the corner. The old men sit there all day. They know everything and they are always wrong."

The tea house had six tables and five old men. They stopped talking when Zeynep came in, and the whole room looked at her, and she wanted to run away.

"I am looking for Halil Duran," she said.

Nobody answered. A man with a white beard moved his glass in a small circle on the table.

"Whose girl are you?" he said.

"My aunt is Nur Aydın. Above the bakery."

"Ah." Something changed in the room. Two men started talking again. "And what do you want with Halil?"

"I have something for him. From my grandfather."

The man with the beard looked at her for a long moment.

"Halil sold that flat," he said. "His legs are bad now. He lives with his daughter, up the hill. Behind the school. The house with the lemon tree." He turned back to his tea. "But he is not going to talk to you."

"Why not?"

"He does not talk to anybody. Not for years." The man made a small movement with his hand, like a man pushing a fly away. "Go home, child. It is hot."

The hill was steep and the sun was directly above her. Zeynep walked past the school. She found three lemon trees. Two of them were in front of the same house. She stood in the road, looked at the windows, and felt stupid.

Then she saw the old man.

He sat in the shade at the side of the house, on a wooden chair. Next to him was a table with a bowl of beans on it. He was cleaning the beans, slowly, one at a time. He was thin, and his shirt was grey, and his hands moved exactly like her grandfather's hands.

Zeynep walked up to the low wall.

"Excuse me. Are you Halil Duran?"

He did not stop his work.

"Who is asking?"

"My name is Zeynep. Zeynep Aydın." She took a breath. "My grandfather is Osman Duran."

The old man's hands stopped. He put the bean down on the table. Then he stood up, and he was slow but he was not weak, and he walked to the door of the house.

"Go home," he said.

"Please, I only want—"

The door closed.

# The Bird and the Beans

Zeynep sat on the wall for eleven minutes. She knew because she looked at her phone every thirty seconds.

Then she got up and walked back down the hill.

She did not go home. She went to the harbour and sat on the stones with her feet over the water and put the box on her knees. Small fish moved under the surface in a grey cloud.

She thought about her grandfather in the hot room. The food was terrible and the man in the next bed was terrible. He was waiting for a phone call.

Then she opened the box and looked at the photographs again. She looked at every one. At the bottom, under all the pictures, she found something new. It was a small brown envelope, closed. One word was on the front in old ink.

Halil.

Zeynep did not open it. She sat there and looked at the word for a long time. The water moved. A boat came in with two men and a lot of shouting.

On Sunday morning she went back up the hill.

The old man was in the same chair with the same bowl. This time there were more beans.

Zeynep did not go to the wall. She stopped in the road, about ten steps away, and she talked to the air in front of her.

"I am not going to knock on your door," she said. "I am going to say four things and then I am going to go away."

The old man said nothing. His hands kept moving.

"One. My grandfather is in the hospital in Ankara. His heart. He is not dying, the doctor says, but he is seventy-nine."

A bean went into the bowl. Click.

"Two. He kept a box for forty-two years. There are photographs of your mother in it. He says you do not have any."

The hands stopped.

"Three. He knows he was wrong. He did not say that to me. He cannot say things like that. But he told me the story, and he called himself stupid twice." Zeynep's voice went strange and she waited a moment. "Four. There is an envelope at the bottom with your name on it. I did not open it. Nobody opened it."

She took the box out of her bag and put it on the low wall, on the flat stone at the top.

"I am going to leave it here," she said. "You can take it inside or you can leave it in the sun. That is your business. Goodbye."

She turned and started to walk down the hill.

She was maybe twenty steps away when she heard the chair move on the stones behind her.

"Girl."

Zeynep stopped.

The old man stood by the wall with the box in his hands. He did not open it.

"The two boys on the wall," he said. "The fat one and the thin one. Is that picture in here?"

"Yes."

"Which one is fat?"

Zeynep almost laughed. "The one on the left."

"That is Osman," the old man said. "He ate my bread every day for four years and then he told our mother that I ate his." He looked down at the box. Then he looked at the house, and at the road, and at the beans on the table.

"Are you hungry?" he said.

They sat in the shade and cleaned beans together for an hour. He did not open the box. He put it on the chair next to him. Every few minutes he touched the top of it with two fingers. He wanted to be sure it was still there.

He asked questions. Is your grandfather still angry about football? Does he still put too much salt on everything? Does he still sing badly in the morning? Zeynep said yes to all of them, and after the third question the old man started to smile, and after that he did not stop.

At four o'clock his daughter came out with tea and looked at Zeynep with big eyes and said nothing at all.

Zeynep went home on the bus with an empty bag and a paper with a phone number on it.

Her grandfather came out of the hospital eleven days later. He sat on his balcony with his tomatoes, which were half dead, and he was very angry about that for a while.

Then the phone rang, and Zeynep watched him look at the screen, and she watched his face change.

"Go inside," he said.

She went inside. She stood by the kitchen window and she did not listen, or she tried not to listen. Her grandfather talked on the balcony for one hour and ten minutes. Sometimes he shouted. Once he laughed so much that he had to hold the wall.

When he came in, his eyes were wet and he was angry about that too.

"He is coming in September," he said.

"Here?"

"Here. He is going to sleep in your room. You are going to sleep on the sofa. And I do not want to hear anything about it."

"Fine," Zeynep said.

Her grandfather sat down. He picked up the water bottle for the tomatoes and then he put it down again.

"The envelope," he said. "Did you look inside it?"

"No."

"Good." He was quiet for a moment. "It was a bad letter. I wrote it in 1983. I am very glad he did not read it in 1983."

"What is he going to do with it now?"

"He burnt it," her grandfather said. "On Tuesday. In the garden, with the beans."

He picked up the water bottle again and went out to the balcony. This time he watered the tomatoes exactly the right amount. He did not say another word all evening.
