# The Big White Bag

Selin took a white bag from the top shelf. It said "sugar" on the front in her mother's writing. She smiled and put it on the table.

Today was her grandmother's birthday. Selin wanted to make a cake by herself, without help. Her mother was at work until six. The guests were coming at seven.

She opened her grandmother's old cookbook. The pages were soft and a little yellow. She found the cake recipe and read it two times.

"Flour, eggs, butter, sugar," she said to herself. "I can do this."

She broke three eggs into a big bowl. She added the butter and mixed it with a fork. Then she opened the white bag and put two cups of the white powder inside.

The kitchen smelled warm and sweet already, from the butter. Selin put the cake in the oven and set a timer for forty minutes.

She sat at the table and called her friend Duru.

"I am making a cake for my grandmother," Selin said. "A surprise cake."

"That is nice," Duru said. "Can I come and see it?"

"Come at six. It will be ready."

Duru arrived at six exactly. She wore a green dress for the party. The two girls opened the oven together and took out the cake. It looked perfect — golden and tall.

"It smells amazing," Duru said.

Selin cut a small piece for each of them. They both took a bite at the same time.

Duru's face changed fast. She put her hand over her mouth.

"What is wrong?" Selin asked.

"Taste it," Duru said. "Just taste it."

Selin took a bite. The cake was not sweet at all. It was salty, very salty, like the sea.

"That is not sugar," Duru said slowly. "I think that is salt."

Selin ran to the kitchen and looked at the white bag again. She turned it around. On the back, in small letters, it said "salt — big bag, for winter soup."

Her grandmother always put salt into old sugar bags. She saved the paper bags and used them again. Nobody told Selin.

"Oh no," Selin said. "The guests are coming in one hour."

She looked at the clock. Six o'clock. She looked at the salty cake on the table. Her plan was already broken, and the party had not even started.

# One Hour and No Sugar

Selin ran to the cupboard and searched every shelf.

Flour. Rice. Tea. Old bread. No sugar anywhere.

"Maybe your neighbor has some," Duru said. "The old man with the small dog."

"Mr. Tekin! Yes!" Selin said. "He always has tea with a lot of sugar."

They ran downstairs together. Selin knocked on the door fast, three times.

Mr. Tekin opened the door slowly. His small dog barked once and then stopped.

"Selin? Is everything OK?" he asked.

"I need sugar," Selin said. "A lot of sugar. My cake is a disaster."

Mr. Tekin laughed, but he did not ask more questions. He went to his kitchen and came back with a big bag of sugar.

"Take it all," he said. "I do not bake."

"Thank you! Thank you so much!"

"Wait," Mr. Tekin said. "You do not have time to bake a new cake. It is too late for that."

Selin stopped at the door. He was right. A cake needed time in the oven, and she had only forty minutes left.

"What can I do then?" she asked.

Mr. Tekin thought for a moment. "My wife used to make a fast dessert," he said. "Bread, milk, sugar, and a little cinnamon. It does not need the oven. It only needs time in the fridge."

"Can you show us?"

"Come up," he said. "Bring your bread."

They went back to Selin's kitchen with the sugar and Mr. Tekin. He cut the old bread into small pieces and put it in a bowl. Duru warmed milk on the stove, not too hot. Selin mixed sugar and cinnamon in a cup.

"Now we pour the milk over the bread," Mr. Tekin said. "And we let it sit."

They poured the sweet milk over the bread. The bread became soft and yellow. Selin added the sugar and cinnamon on top.

"Into the fridge," Mr. Tekin said. "Thirty minutes, no more, no less."

The clock said six-thirty. The dessert needed thirty minutes. The first guests were going to arrive at seven.

Selin looked at the salty cake still on the table. She had one more idea.

"Can we save the cake too?" she asked. "Maybe with something on top?"

# A Party With Two Desserts

Duru had an idea. "What about honey?" she said. "Honey is very sweet. Maybe it can fix the cake."

Selin found a jar of honey in the cupboard. She cut the salty cake into small pieces and put a little honey on each piece. She tasted one.

"It is strange," she said, "but it is not bad. Salty and sweet together."

At seven o'clock the doorbell rang. Selin's grandmother walked in first, with a big smile and a purple scarf. Behind her came aunts, uncles, and cousins with presents.

"Happy birthday!" everyone said together.

Selin's grandmother hugged her tight. "Something smells like cinnamon," she said. "And something else too. What did you make?"

"Two desserts," Selin said. "A cake, and a surprise."

The family sat around the big table. Selin brought out the milk-and-bread dessert first, cold from the fridge. Everyone tried a spoonful.

"This is delicious," her uncle said. "Sweet and soft."

Then Selin brought the salty cake with honey on top.

"I have to tell you something," she said. "I used salt instead of sugar in this one. By mistake."

Her grandmother laughed loudly. "That old sugar bag! I use it for salt every winter. I am sorry, Selin. I forgot to tell your mother."

"Mr. Tekin helped us fix it," Duru said. "With honey."

Everyone tried the salty cake with honey. Selin's grandmother took a big piece.

"It tastes like the cake my mother made," she said. "She always put a little salt in her sweet cakes. It makes the sweetness stronger."

"Really?" Selin asked.

"Really. You made my mother's recipe by accident."

Selin looked at the table. Two desserts, one small disaster, and one big surprise. Her mother arrived at seven-thirty and found the whole family eating and laughing.

"What did I miss?" her mother asked.

"A story," Selin's grandmother said. "A very good one."

Later, Selin wrote the salty-cake recipe on a new card. Under the title she wrote: "Do not use the winter salt bag." She put the card inside the old cookbook, next to her great-grandmother's page.

Mr. Tekin came up for tea before he went home. His small dog sat under the table and waited for cake.

"Next time," he told Selin, "read the small letters on the bag."

"Next time," Selin said, "I will ask you first."
