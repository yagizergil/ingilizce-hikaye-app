# The Wrong Bag

The bus was already full at the second stop. Sinem pushed in behind an old woman with two shopping bags. The doors closed on somebody's coat and opened again.

She held her black bag against her stomach with both arms.

Inside the bag there was a brown envelope. Inside the envelope there was the rent money for the shop. Her aunt gave it to her last night at the kitchen table.

"Twelve o'clock," her aunt said. "Kenan Bey comes at twelve on the first day of the month. He is not a bad man, but he waits for nobody. Ten years in that shop, Sinem. Ten years and we always pay at twelve."

Her aunt was in the hospital now with her sister. She was going to come back on Thursday. Until Thursday the small shop with the nuts and the dried fruit was Sinem's shop.

Sinem was nineteen. She started work in that shop at twelve years old, in the summer holidays. She knew the price of every box. She knew the names of the old men and their afternoon tea. But her aunt always carried the rent envelope herself. Always.

The bus stopped again. More people came in. A man with a grey beard stood on Sinem's foot and said sorry three times. A boy with a school bag pushed past her arm.

Somebody was singing quietly at the back. The windows went white with warm air. Sinem saw nothing outside, only grey shapes and lights.

At the fourth stop the bus filled with market people and their boxes. A woman put a box of green beans on the seat next to Sinem. Two men carried a long carpet in through the back door and everybody shouted at them.

Sinem's bag went down to her side. There was no room in front of her anymore. She put her hand on the handle and held it there.

Next to her, on the wet floor, there was another black bag.

The bus turned into Fig Street and everybody moved at the same time. Sinem put her hand down, took the handle of a black bag, and pushed to the door with all the other people.

She got out into the cold. The bus went away and the street was suddenly quiet.

Sinem walked to the shop and opened the metal door. She turned the lights on and put the bag on the table by the money box. Then she stopped.

The bag was heavier than her bag. And it made a noise. A hard, metal noise.

She opened it.

Inside there were tools. A big one with a black handle. Two smaller ones. A yellow lamp with a wire. A pair of dirty gloves. Under everything, a small blue paper with numbers on it.

No brown envelope. No rent money. No phone.

Sinem stood there with her hand still on the bag. The shop clock said five past nine.

She ran out and down to the bus stop. The next number 42 came in six minutes. She did not get on it. She looked at every face at the door. She looked for a man with her black bag in his hand.

Nobody. The bus went away and left grey smoke in the cold air.

Sinem sat down on the bus stop seat and put her head in her hands for one minute. Then she got up. Sitting was no good.

She went back to the shop and took the blue paper out of the bag. She turned it to the light.

It was a bill from a small shop. At the top there were three words in big letters: AYDIN ELECTRIC — REPAIRS. Under the words there was an address.

Number 9, Fig Street.

Sinem looked up. Fig Street was her street.

# Number Nine

She locked the shop door and ran.

Number 9 was five minutes away, past the bread shop and the school. It was a very small shop with a green door and a window full of old lamps. The lights were off. There was a card on the glass: BACK SOON.

Sinem knocked. Nobody came. She knocked again with her hand flat.

"He is not there."

A woman was standing in the flower shop door next to it with a cigarette. She had a red apron and wet hands.

"Do you know him?" Sinem asked. "The man with the tools?"

"Hakan. He opens at eight, then he goes out to jobs. He comes back for lunch." The woman looked at Sinem's face. "Is there a problem?"

"He has my bag. And I have his."

"Ah," the woman said. She threw the cigarette into the water by the wall. "Then you must find him fast, because he does not answer his phone. Nobody in this street can find that man."

"Then tell me where he is now," Sinem said. "Please. There is money in my bag and I must pay the rent at twelve."

The woman put her wet hands on her apron.

"How much money?"

"Enough," Sinem said.

"Ah," the woman said again, in a different voice. She turned and shouted into her shop: "Emre! Watch the door!" Then she came out into the cold and looked up and down the street.

"This morning he went that way with a ladder on his shoulder. To the grey building at the end, on the corner. The tall one with the blue windows."

"How do you know?"

"Because I saw him," the woman said. "And because the people in that building call him every month. Old wires, new machines. Always a problem."

Sinem said thank you and started to run again.

The grey building was not one building. It was three, all together, with three doors. Sinem stood in front of them and looked up. Blue windows, eight floors, more than eighty flats.

She started with the first door. A man was cleaning the stairs with a wet cloth.

"Is there a workman here? With a ladder?"

"Not in this one," the man said. He did not stop cleaning.

The second door was locked. She pressed six different buttons. A woman answered and said no in an angry voice.

At the third door a small boy was sitting on the step with a bicycle wheel in his hands.

"A man with a ladder," Sinem said. "Did you see him?"

The boy looked at her for a long time. Then he pointed up with the wheel.

"Fifth floor," he said. "He is making a noise up there. My mother is angry."

Sinem looked at her phone. Twenty past ten.

"Is there a lift?"

"It is broken," the boy said. "He is fixing that too."

She went up the stairs two at a time. On the third floor she stopped and held the wall. Her legs were hot and her mouth was dry. On the fourth floor she heard it: a hard, high noise, like a drill in a wall.

# Five Floors Up

The door of flat 12 was open. Inside, a man in a blue jacket was standing on a ladder with his arms up in a hole in the wall. Dust was falling on his shoulders. On the floor, next to the ladder, there was a black bag.

Her black bag.

"Excuse me," Sinem said.

The man did not hear her. The drill was too loud. An old woman came out of the kitchen with a glass of water and saw Sinem in the door.

"Hakan!" the old woman shouted. "Girl! At the door!"

The noise stopped. The man looked down.

"That is my bag," Sinem said.

Hakan came down the ladder fast. He was younger than she expected, maybe thirty, with white dust in his hair.

"I know," he said. "I know, I know. I opened it at the second job. I saw the money and I was sick." He put his hands on his head. "I went back to the bus stop. I asked at three shops. Nobody knows a girl with a black bag."

"I have your tools."

"My tools." He laughed one time and looked at the sky through the window. "I was using a screwdriver from this lady's kitchen drawer all morning."

He picked up her bag and gave it to her with two hands. Sinem opened it there in the door. The brown envelope was under her book, in the same place. She counted the money with her back to the old woman. It was all there.

"Count it," Hakan said. "Count it in front of me. Please."

"I did."

"Count it again."

She counted it again. Then she pushed the envelope deep into the bag and held the bag against her stomach.

"Where are your tools?" Hakan asked.

"In the shop. Number 22, the nut shop, by the school."

"Your shop?"

"My aunt's shop. She is in the hospital." Sinem looked at her phone. Half past eleven. "I must go. The rent man comes at twelve."

"Kenan Bey," Hakan said. "Grey coat, small glasses."

"You know him?"

"Everybody in this street knows him. He owns four shops here." Hakan took his jacket from the ladder. "Come. I am coming with you. My tools are there and my next job is at one."

They went down the stairs together and out into the street. The wind was strong now and the sky was low and white. They walked fast, past the flower shop, past the bread shop with its warm door.

"I want to ask you something," Sinem said.

"Ask."

"You saw the money at nine in the morning. In a bag with no name."

Hakan walked a few steps and said nothing.

"My father worked in a market for thirty-one years," he said. "People put their bags down all day. Money, phones, keys, everything. Not one thing went away from his table. Not one." He pushed his hair back and dust came out of it. "That is the job. You look after other people's things."

The shop door was still locked and cold. Sinem opened it and turned on the light. She gave him the tool bag and he looked inside it and closed it again with a happy face.

At three minutes to twelve a man in a grey coat came in and cleaned his glasses with a cloth. Sinem gave him the brown envelope. He counted the money slowly and wrote her aunt's name in a small book.

"Same day, same hour," Kenan Bey said. "Ten years. Tell your aunt to get well."

He went out. Sinem sat down on the chair behind the money box and looked at her hands.

Hakan was at the door with his bag on his shoulder.

"Wait," she said.

She filled a paper bag with nuts from the big box, the good ones, and she put a spoon of dried apricots on the top. She held it out to him.

"For the drill noise," she said. "And for the fifth floor."

Hakan took the bag. At the door he stopped.

"Number 42 is always full," he said. "Take the 44. It is slow, but you can put your bag on your knees."

Then he went out into the wind, and Sinem opened the shop for the day.
