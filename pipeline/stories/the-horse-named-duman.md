# The Slow Horse

Zeynep ran to the field before school every morning to see Duman.

He was a big grey horse, old now, with soft eyes and a slow walk. Zeynep's grandfather bought him many years ago, before Zeynep was born.

"In his young years, he pulled the biggest cart in the village," her grandfather said often. "Nobody was faster than him."

That was hard to believe now. These days, Duman walked slowly across the field. He stopped often to rest. When he pulled the small cart to the market, the trip took twice as long as before.

This morning, Zeynep found her grandfather standing by the fence, looking at Duman with a serious face.

"Good morning," she said.

Her grandfather did not answer at first. He watched Duman walk slowly to the water.

"He is tired all the time now," her grandfather said. "He eats less. He works less. Winter is coming, and a horse who does not work costs a lot of money."

"He still works," Zeynep said. "He pulled the cart yesterday."

"It took him one hour to go where it took twenty minutes before." Her grandfather rubbed his face. "I talked to a man at the market yesterday. He wants an old, calm horse for his farm, up in the hills. He offered a good price."

Zeynep's stomach felt cold. "You are going to sell Duman?"

"I did not say yes," her grandfather said. "But I am thinking about it. This farm is small, Zeynep. We need a horse that can work, and food for a horse that cannot is expensive."

"Duman can still work," Zeynep said quickly. "He just needs more time."

"Time is not free," her grandfather said. "Neither is hay."

Zeynep walked into the field and stood next to Duman. He put his big head down near her shoulder, slowly, the way he always did. She touched his neck.

"You are not tired," she said quietly. "You are just old. That is different."

At school that day, Zeynep did not think about anything else. During lunch, she told her friend Mert about the problem.

"My grandfather wants to sell Duman," she said. "To a man in the hills."

"Is that bad?" Mert asked. "Maybe the man is going to take good care of him."

"The man wants a working horse," Zeynep said. "He does not know Duman is slow now. What happens when Duman cannot work fast enough for him, either?"

Mert thought about this. "Then you must show your grandfather that Duman is still useful here," he said. "Not for pulling the cart. For something else."

"Like what?"

"I do not know," Mert said. "But horses are not only good for carts."

That evening, Zeynep did not go straight home. She walked around the village, looking at the small shops, the school, the market square, thinking about Mert's words. She stopped near the school gate, where small children waited for their parents every afternoon.

A young mother stood there with a boy of about four years old. The boy pointed at something across the road and shouted with excitement.

Zeynep followed his eyes. It was a farmer's horse, pulling a cart of vegetables to the market.

"Horse!" the boy shouted. "I want to see the horse!"

His mother smiled. "Maybe next time," she said. "We must go home now."

Zeynep watched the boy's face fall. An idea began to grow in her mind, small and simple, like a seed.

She ran home fast, her school bag hitting her back with every step.

# A New Job for an Old Horse

"I have an idea," Zeynep told her grandfather that evening. "About Duman."

Her grandfather sat by the fire, tired from the day's work. "I am listening," he said.

"Duman does not need to pull heavy things anymore," Zeynep said. "But he can still walk. He can still carry small weight. Children in this village never see a real horse up close, except from far away."

"What are you saying?"

"On market days, I can bring Duman to the square. Slowly. Children can sit on him for a minute, with an adult next to them. People can pay a little money for it, like a game."

Her grandfather looked at her for a long moment. "People pay money to sit on a horse?"

"In the city, yes. My teacher told us about it. Here, maybe people pay less. But it is something. And Duman does not need to be fast for that. He just needs to be calm."

"Duman is calm," her grandfather said slowly. "He was always calm, even as a young horse."

"So can we try it? Just one market day. If it does not work, you can still sell him after."

Her grandfather was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the trees, and Duman made a soft sound in the field.

"One market day," he said finally. "But I am not promising anything after that."

The next market day, Zeynep and her grandfather brought Duman to the square early, before most people arrived. Zeynep put a small sign near him: "Meet Duman. Two minutes on a real horse."

At first, nobody stopped. People walked past, busy with their shopping, their eyes on their lists, not on the old grey horse standing quietly by the fruit stand.

Zeynep felt her plan failing already. Her grandfather did not say anything, but she saw his face, and she knew what he was thinking.

Then the same young mother from the school gate walked by with her son.

"Look!" the boy shouted, pulling his mother's hand. "The horse! The horse from the road!"

The mother laughed and walked over. "How much?" she asked Zeynep.

"A small coin," Zeynep said. "For two minutes."

The boy sat on Duman's back, held safely by his mother and Zeynep's grandfather. Duman did not move much. He stood calm and steady, as if he understood the job perfectly.

The boy's face was full of joy. "I am riding a real horse!" he said loudly, to everyone and no one.

Other children nearby turned to look. Within ten minutes, three more families stopped. By the end of the morning, eight children rode on Duman's back, slow and safe, around a small circle in the square.

Zeynep's grandfather counted the coins that evening. It was not a lot of money. But it was something, and Duman did not need to run at all.

"Interesting," her grandfather said, looking at the coins in his hand.

"So we try it again next market day?" Zeynep asked.

Her grandfather looked out at the field, where Duman stood eating quietly under the evening sky.

"We try it again," he said.

# The Job That Fits

By the end of autumn, "Meet Duman" was a regular part of market day.

Zeynep stood by the horse every week with her small sign, while her grandfather watched from nearby, ready to help with the smallest children. Duman seemed to enjoy it, standing calmly while small hands touched his neck and small voices talked to him.

"He likes the attention," Zeynep's grandmother said one evening. "Old animals are like old people. They like to feel useful."

Not everyone in the village agreed with the plan. One neighbor, a man with a strong young horse of his own, laughed when he saw the sign at the market.

"A horse for children to play with," he said. "In my time, a horse worked or it did not eat."

"Duman still works," Zeynep said. "Just a different kind of work."

The man shook his head and walked away, but Zeynep did not let his words bother her too much.

One cold morning in late autumn, the man from the hills came back to the farm. Zeynep's heart jumped when she saw him talking to her grandfather by the fence.

She ran over quickly. "Are you selling Duman?" she asked, out of breath.

Her grandfather looked at her, then at the man. "I told him we changed our plans," he said. "Duman has a job here now. A good one."

The man from the hills looked confused. "A horse that does not pull a cart? What good is that?"

"He makes children happy," Zeynep's grandfather said simply. "And happy children bring happy families to the market. Happy families buy more vegetables from my stand, too."

The man laughed, but not in an unkind way. "Farmers," he said, shaking his head, and he walked back to his own cart.

That evening, Zeynep sat with Duman in the field as the sun went down. She gave him a piece of bread and rubbed his big, grey head.

"You did it," she said. "You found a new job. Even at your age."

Duman made a soft sound and pushed his head against her hand, the way he always did when he was happy.

Her grandfather walked over and stood beside them, watching the sun disappear behind the hills.

"You had a good idea," he said. "I almost sold him too fast. I only saw an old horse who did not pull a cart anymore."

"And now?"

"Now I see a horse who still has work to do," her grandfather said. "Just not the work I expected."

Winter came soon after, cold and quiet, and Duman rested in the warm part of the barn, eating well, growing stronger for spring. On the first market day of the new year, Zeynep put up her sign again. Duman walked slowly into the square, ready for his job.

A small girl ran up first, her eyes wide with excitement.

"Can I ride the horse?" she asked.

"Yes," Zeynep said, smiling. "That is exactly what he is here for."
