# The Red Notebook

Hulya closed the shop door and turned the small sign to "Closed."

Her fabric shop was quiet now. Rolls of cloth stood along the walls, blue and green and yellow. In the morning, they looked beautiful. At night, after a bad day of sales, they only looked like money she did not have.

She opened her red notebook and looked at the numbers again.

She owed money to Mr. Fikret, the cloth seller in the next town. Two months ago, she bought new cloth for the summer season. Good cloth, bright colors. She thought people were going to buy it fast.

They did not.

The number in her notebook was big: three thousand lira. Mr. Fikret gave her ten days to pay, but that was one week ago. Three days left now.

Her phone rang. She looked at the name and felt her stomach drop.

Mr. Fikret.

"Hulya," he said, when she answered. "How is business?"

"Slow," she said, honest. "But it is going to get better. People buy more cloth before the school season starts."

"That is next month," he said. "I need my money this week."

"I know," she said. "I have some of it. Not all."

"How much?"

She looked at the box under her table, where she kept small notes from each day's sales.

"One thousand," she said. "I can give you one thousand now."

Mr. Fikret was quiet for a moment.

"I have my own family to feed," he said. "I gave you time already. Ten days became almost three weeks."

"I know, and I am thankful," Hulya said. "But if you take my last money now, I cannot buy new cloth. Then I sell nothing, and you never get the rest."

"That is your problem, not mine."

"It can be both our problem," Hulya said, "or we can find an answer together."

There was a long silence on the phone.

"I am coming to your shop tomorrow," Mr. Fikret finally said. "We are going to talk face to face. Have a real plan ready, Hulya. Not just words."

He hung up fast. She did not have time to answer.

Hulya sat down on her small chair behind the table and put her head in her hands. Outside, the street lights turned on, one by one. Her daughter, Zeynep, was at her aunt's house that night. Hulya was alone with her notebook and her rolls of cloth.

She thought about closing the shop. Her mother started this business thirty years ago. Hulya grew up between these same rolls of cloth, playing with fabric pieces on the floor. She did not want to be the one who lost it.

She opened her notebook to a clean page and started to write. Numbers first: what she owed, what she had, and what she was able to sell this week. Then a list: which customers always came back, which colors sold fast, and what people asked for but she did not have.

By midnight, she had three pages of notes and one plan. It was not perfect, but it was something real. She closed the notebook and turned off the shop lights.

Tomorrow, she was going to need every word of it.

# Face to Face

Mr. Fikret arrived at eleven o'clock, exactly on time.

He was a tall man with grey hair and a serious face. He looked at the shop first — the rolls of cloth, the small chairs, the old sewing machine in the corner.

"Business does not look bad," he said.

"It does not look bad," Hulya agreed. "But it is slow. Summer cloth sells slowly this year. Everyone waited for the sales."

"So sell it at a lower price," he said. "Get your money faster."

"I thought about that," Hulya said. She opened her red notebook. "But look here. If I sell everything now, at a low price, I get maybe two thousand lira total. That pays you most, but leaves nothing for new cloth. My shop stops after that."

Mr. Fikret sat down across from her.

"What is your plan, then?"

"I give you the one thousand I have today," she said. "Then I give you five hundred every week, for four weeks. That is the rest, plus a little more for your trouble."

"Four weeks is a long time."

"It is," Hulya said. "But it means the shop stays open. And when the shop stays open, I buy cloth from you again next season. Not from another seller in another town."

Mr. Fikret looked at her carefully.

"You are saying I lose a little now, to keep a customer later."

"Yes," Hulya said. "I checked prices from three sellers last year. Yours were the best, and your cloth never had problems. I want to keep buying from you for many years, not just this one bad season."

Mr. Fikret stood up and walked around the shop. He touched a roll of blue cloth, then a green one.

"This blue is good cloth," he said. "Why is it still here?"

"People want green and yellow this year," Hulya said. "The blue came in wrong. My mistake. I bought too much of it."

"I can take the blue back," he said suddenly. "Not for money. For less debt. I know a shop in the city that wants blue cloth for uniforms."

Hulya sat up straight. "How much less debt?"

"Eight hundred lira, if the cloth is in good shape."

She walked to the rolls and checked them fast. Clean, no marks, no water damage.

"They are perfect," she said. "Take them."

Mr. Fikret looked at his own notebook, doing numbers in his head.

"One thousand today," he said. "Eight hundred from the blue cloth. That leaves twelve hundred. Four weeks, three hundred each week. Not five hundred."

"I said five hundred," Hulya said, surprised.

"I know what you said," he replied. "But you gave me a real plan, and you gave me the blue cloth without arguing. Three hundred a week is easier for a shop that is trying to grow again."

Hulya felt something loosen in her chest, like a rope she did not know was tight.

"Thank you," she said.

"Do not thank me yet," Mr. Fikret said. "Pay every week, on time. If you miss one week, we talk again, and I am not going to be as easy next time."

"I understand."

He picked up the blue rolls of cloth and carried them to his van outside. Hulya wrote the new numbers in her red notebook: twelve hundred left, three hundred a week, four weeks.

For the first time in ten days, the numbers looked possible.

# What the Shop Taught Her

The next four weeks were hard, but not impossible.

Hulya put a small sign in her window: "New colors for school season." She ordered a little green and yellow cloth from another seller, and she paid with her last money. She stayed open one hour later each evening.

Zeynep helped after school, folding cloth and talking to customers while her mother worked with numbers in the back.

"Mama," Zeynep asked one evening, "why did Mr. Fikret take the blue cloth instead of money?"

"Because it helped both of us," Hulya said. "I did not need the blue cloth anymore. He knew someone who did. That is better than the cloth sitting here forever."

"Is that a bargain?"

"Yes," Hulya said, smiling. "A good bargain is not about winning. It is about both people getting something they need."

Every Friday, Hulya walked to Mr. Fikret's shop with three hundred lira in an envelope. The first week, he counted it slowly and wrote a note in his own book. The second week, he counted faster. By the third week, he just took the envelope and said, "Good."

On the fourth Friday, Hulya walked in with her envelope and her red notebook.

"This is the last one," she said. "Three hundred lira. After this, I owe you nothing."

Mr. Fikret counted it and wrote the final number. Zero.

"You did it," he said. "Many people do not."

"I almost did not," Hulya said. "You did not take all your money that first week. I think that saved my shop."

"I thought about it," he admitted. "But you did not hide from me. You answered your phone. You had a real plan. That is rare."

He walked to the back of his shop. He came out with a small roll of new cloth, dark red with gold thread along the edge.

"For the school season," he said. "A gift, not a debt. Sell it, keep the money."

Hulya held the cloth carefully. "I cannot take this for free."

"You can," he said. "Call it thanks, for being honest when it was easier to disappear."

She carried the red cloth home that evening, Zeynep walking beside her.

"Are we okay now, Mama?" Zeynep asked. "With the money?"

"We are okay," Hulya said. "Not rich. But okay. And your grandmother's shop is still open."

She opened her red notebook one more time that night. She turned to a new page and wrote a new list. Money for cloth, money for the family, and a little money saved for the next hard season.

This time, the numbers felt like hers again, not a weight on her shoulders. She closed the notebook, turned off the lights, and locked the shop door, ready for a new morning.
