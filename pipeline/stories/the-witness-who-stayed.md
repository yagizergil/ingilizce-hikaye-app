# The Form That Means I Saw It

The witness had been answering questions for nineteen minutes before Derya noticed the first thing he was keeping back.

She sat at a narrow table to the left of the witness box, close enough to hear the old man breathe between his answers. Her job was easy to describe and hard to do: whatever the state lawyer asked in the language of the court, she carried into the island language, and whatever the witness answered, she carried back. Nothing else in that room belonged to her. She had been taught the rule at every training course she had ever attended, and she had repeated it to younger interpreters who imagined the work was about words rather than about limits.

The witness was called Yusuf Aral, sixty-one years old, a night guard at a storage building on the harbour road. His jacket had been pressed so carefully that the creases looked painful. Whenever he spoke he looked down at his hands, as though the answers were written somewhere on them and he did not entirely trust what he read.

"On the night of the fourteenth of March, where were you at eleven o'clock?" the state lawyer asked, in the soft voice of a man who had the whole afternoon and no strong feelings about any of it.

Derya turned the question, waited, and gave the court the six words she had been given: he had been in the guard room at the east gate. When the lawyer asked what he had seen, Yusuf said there had been smoke above the roof of the north hall. She said it in the same number of words, because a good interpreter does not improve a witness or tidy him.

For several minutes the questions moved slowly over the smoke, the wind, and the time the fire service had taken to arrive. Yusuf answered each one with seven words or fewer, and each answer crossed the table without any trouble at all. Then came the twentieth minute, and the question about the north door.

"Was the north door locked that night?" the lawyer asked, turning a page as though the answer hardly mattered.

Yusuf said, in the island language, that the north door had been closed. But the verb he chose carried the ending that the islanders put on an action they have taken part in themselves. Derya's pen stopped above her notebook.

People who had never studied the island language assumed it was a rough version of the mainland one, and every islander she knew was tired of explaining that it was not. In that language a speaker cannot simply state that a thing happened, because the verb itself must show how he knows it. Did he see it with his own eyes, or was he told, or were his own hands inside the event? There is no neutral way to speak, and a child learns the difference before he learns to count.

Without meaning to, Yusuf had just told the court that he had shut the north door himself. Then, inside a single breath, he changed the ending of the verb to the form used for something heard from other people, and finished the sentence by saying that this was what everybody had told him afterwards.

Derya translated it exactly, because the court language had no way of carrying the correction. In the record, therefore, the correction did not exist. It had passed through her and vanished, the way a coin vanishes through a hole in a coat pocket.

Nobody looked up. The judge, a thin woman with her reading glasses pushed into her hair, wrote one line and turned the page, while the state lawyer moved on to the question of the insurance claim. Derya moved with him, because a hearing does not stop while an interpreter thinks.

It was during the next answer, while Yusuf described the colour of the smoke, that she let herself look properly at the man in the dock for the first time.

She had arrived that morning with forty minutes to read a file she had never asked for, because her colleague Mehmet had been taken to hospital on Sunday with chest pains. The agency had telephoned at seven, and she had read the summary standing in a corridor with her coffee going cold on the window ledge. The accused was named there as B. Onat, owner of Onat Storage, charged with setting fire to his own building and with making a false claim afterwards. Onat is not a rare name. She had turned the page without feeling anything at all.

The man sitting between two officers, twenty steps away, was Bekir Onat, who owned the building she lived in and who had not taken her rent for two years.

He looked older than she remembered and, strangely, smaller, since she had always thought of him as a large man. He held a folded paper in both hands. He was watching her with the unhurried calm of somebody who had recognised her long before and had been waiting to see how much time she would need to catch up.

The state lawyer asked his next question, and her mouth turned it into the island language while some other part of her stood outside her body and watched.

The correct action was as clear to her as her own address. She should raise her hand, ask the judge for a moment, and declare a personal connection to the accused. The hearing would stop while the court looked for another interpreter, and there were perhaps nine people in the country who could work this pair of languages, two of whom lived on the islands.

Yusuf answered another question, Derya translated it, and the clock above the door went on moving.

At half past four the judge thanked the interpreter for her work and set the next session for Thursday morning. Derya packed her notebook, kept her eyes on her bag, and walked out through the side corridor where the light was always grey. She had said nothing. She understood, with the clarity of a person who has just made a decision by not making one, that every hour from now on would make speaking harder.

# Two Years of Rent

The flat was on the third floor of a yellow building two streets back from the coast road, and the stairs smelled of cooking oil and wet stone in every season of the year.

Her mother was awake, sitting near the window with the television on and the sound turned off, which was what she did when her back hurt and she did not want anybody to know about it. Derya put the shopping on the counter, filled the kettle, and stood for a moment with her hand flat against the cold metal of the sink.

"You're late," her mother said, without looking away from the silent screen.

"Court. The big one, on the hill."

Her mother made the small noise she kept for anything connected to the government, then turned the sound up two steps, as if the news deserved more attention than her daughter's day.

Two years earlier, almost to the month, Derya had sat at that same table with three letters in front of her and no plan for any of them. Her mother's kidneys had been failing for a year by then, which meant two trips a week to the city hospital, medicine that the state paid for, and medicine that it did not. There were also whole months when Derya turned down court work, because somebody had to sit in the corridor and wait.

The rent had been four months late when Bekir Onat came up the stairs himself, instead of sending the man who usually collected it. He had stood in the doorway with his hands in his coat pockets and looked past her at her mother on the sofa, and when he asked how she was, Derya said only that she was tired.

He had nodded slowly, in the way men nod when they are deciding something and would rather not be watched while they decide it. Then he had told her that the rent could wait, and that she should stop leaving notes under his office door, because the notes made him feel like a man who took money from a sick woman.

She had expected the arrangement to last two months, and it had lasted two years. Twice she had tried to start paying again, and both times he had told her, without warmth and without cruelty, that he would tell her when.

Her brother Onur had found work with the same company eight months later, driving one of the small trucks between the harbour and the depot outside the city. The work was steady and it came with a contract, and in a city where half the young men were paid by the day, a contract was a kind of address.

Derya waited until her mother had gone to bed, then opened her laptop at the kitchen table and read the case file properly for the first time.

The fire had begun shortly before eleven on the fourteenth of March, in the north hall of the storage building on the harbour road. The state's argument had a clean shape to it. Onat Storage owed money to two suppliers and to the bank, and the north hall had been almost empty of stock while it was insured as though it were full. A man had been seen near the fence at ten forty, and the fire had started in three separate places, which does not happen by accident.

Derya read that part twice, because whatever else might be true, somebody had walked around that building with a can in his hand.

She scrolled down, and the file stopped being a matter of business.

On the night of the fire, four men had been working a late shift in the north hall, unloading a delivery that had arrived hours behind schedule. Three of them escaped through the yard gate. The fourth, a loader called Kemal Dogan, aged forty-three, had run the other way, towards the door on the north side, because that was the nearest exit and because the fire drills had always sent him there.

The door had not opened, and Kemal had stayed at it for eleven minutes.

The file said that he had burns to both hands and both arms, that he had lost most of the use of his right hand, and that he would never load anything again. There was a photograph of the door taken the following morning, black and twisted, with a chain and a padlock still in place across the outside of it.

Derya sat back while the kitchen tap dripped twice, the way it always did before it stopped.

The door was closed, Yusuf had said, with his own hands inside the verb. Then he had pulled his hands out again and given the sentence to somebody else.

She thought about the shape of the state's case, and found, without wanting to, that there was a gap in it. The state claimed that Bekir Onat had burned an empty hall for the insurance money, but the hall had not been empty, and one of the men inside it was now learning to hold a cup with his left hand. No lawyer had yet explained why a careful businessman would set fire to a building with his own workers in it, on a night when his own guard sat fifty metres away at the gate.

There was another explanation, and it cleared nobody. Somebody had lit the fire and somebody else had chained the door, and those might easily be two different people. The second might be sitting in the ground-floor office of her own building, and the third might be a night guard with painful creases in his jacket, who answered every question in seven careful words.

She closed the laptop. Down in the street a car door shut, and she caught herself listening for Onur's boots on the stairs before remembering that he was on the night run and would not be home until six.

Whatever she did on Thursday, she would be doing it to this flat, to her mother's chair by the window, and to her brother's contract. The truth was not a thing that happened only inside a courtroom, because afterwards it came home with you and asked where it was supposed to sleep.

# The Canteen on the Fourth Floor

On Thursday the hearing ran until eleven and then broke for an hour, and Derya found Yusuf Aral in the canteen on the fourth floor, alone at a table by the window with a glass of tea he was not drinking.

The room was almost empty. She put her own glass down opposite him and sat without asking, which was rude, and which she did on purpose.

"You should not be talking to me," he said in the island language, still without looking up. "They will say that you helped me, and they will say worse things than that as well."

Derya answered that she knew, and that she had thought about it all the way up the stairs and had come anyway.

He turned the glass a quarter turn on the table, while outside the window the roofs went down the hill in grey and red steps towards the water. Far out, a container ship sat on flat water with nothing at all happening around it.

"You closed the north door yourself," she said.

His shoulders moved once, very slightly, in the way a man's shoulders move when the thing he has feared for weeks finally arrives and turns out to be an ordinary size.

"You heard it," he said. "The verb told you, because the verb tells anybody who speaks the language properly."

"Nobody in that room speaks it properly." He said this without pride and without complaint, as a simple fact about the world. "For three days I have handed them the truth in a language they cannot read, while the clerk writes down whatever your mouth gives him, and everybody goes home satisfied. Do you understand how strange that feels?"

Derya told him that she understood it better than anybody else in the building, and he looked at her then, with pale tired eyes and a small white scar through one eyebrow.

"I chained that door every night for six years," he said, "not once, not sometimes, but every single night, because the manager told me to in my second week."

There had been thefts from the north side, he explained, and the yard camera did not reach that far, so the chain went on at eight in the evening and came off at six in the morning. He had written it in the guard book each time, and the book held six years of his handwriting.

"On the fourteenth of March there were still four men inside," Derya said.

His hand tightened around the glass. "The delivery was late, and I put the chain on at eight because I have always put the chain on at eight. The shift changed at nine, and nobody came to tell me." He stopped, and then added, more quietly, that nobody had needed to tell him, because he had seen the lights burning in the north hall, had thought about them for a moment, and had gone back to the gate.

Derya let the silence sit, since in court silence was a tool she was forbidden to use, and here she was free to use it.

"If I say that in the room," Yusuf said at last, "then I am the man who burned Kemal Dogan's hands. Not the fire, but me. His brothers know where I live, my wife is on the island, and my permit says night guard, which is a job nobody will give me afterwards."

She answered that if he did not say it, then Bekir Onat was the man who had set the fire, and Yusuf asked her, quite calmly, whether he had.

"You were the one at the gate," she said. "I am only the mouth in the middle."

"I saw a car on the coast road at twenty to eleven with its lights switched off, and two men at the fence, neither of them fat and neither of them old." He pushed the glass away from him. "That is everything I saw, and I have said it, and the lawyer wrote down two men and moved on, because two men are no use to anybody. He wants one man, he has one man, and the one man is sitting there in a good jacket looking at the floor."

"Then say the rest," she told him.

"Which rest?" He leaned forward across the table. "You want me to stand up and say that the owner did not light the fire, but that the owner's chain held the door shut, and that I am the one who turned the key. You want the whole thing at once. I would give one man his freedom and buy myself twenty years of a different sort of prison."

Derya said that she wanted the court to have what he had, nothing more than that, and Yusuf laughed one dry sound with no shape to it.

"You and I do the same work, daughter, because everything has to pass through us, and we decide what crosses. You have been deciding since Monday afternoon. Do not sit there telling me that your hands are clean because they are made of words."

Derya walked down four floors instead of taking the lift, and she was still walking when her telephone rang inside her bag.

Onur did not say hello, and asked instead whether she was interpreting in the Onat case. She stopped on the second landing, where the window looked out at a wall, and asked him who had told him.

"Bekir Bey's lawyer came to the depot this morning to talk to the drivers about the delivery book, and he asked me whether the interpreter was my sister." Her brother's voice had a flat, careful quality that she had heard perhaps three times in her life. "Did you know it was him before you sat down?"

"Not before I sat down. I have known since Monday afternoon."

"Then say nothing," Onur said. "You go in, you translate, you go home, and that is the whole job, which is what you have told me a hundred times."

When she said that there was a problem with the witness, he answered that the witness's problem belonged to the witness, and his voice rose and then came down again behind the noise of a truck engine.

"Do you know what that man did for us?" he asked. "Do you know what happens to Mother in a building with a new owner and two years of unpaid rent written down in somebody's book? Do you know how many drivers he keeps on that he does not actually need, including one who was carrying vegetables for cash eighteen months ago?"

"Onur."

He cut across her and said that Bekir Onat had kept them in that flat, and that if she wanted to be honest she should be honest about that as well.

He hung up. Derya stood on the landing with the phone still against her ear for several seconds after the line had died, looking at the wall and thinking that her brother was completely right, and that being right changed nothing at all.

# A Note for the Record

She went to the court building at eight the next morning, before the corridors filled, and asked the officer at the desk whether the judge would receive her in her room.

It took forty minutes, which she spent writing in small letters on two sheets of paper. By the time she was called she had made three drafts and torn up two of them.

Judge Aydin's room was narrow and smelled of paper. She neither offered coffee nor sat down, which Derya understood as a message about how much of the morning this was allowed to take.

"You are the interpreter."

"Derya Sen. I have to declare a personal connection to the accused, and I should have declared it on Monday afternoon."

The judge's face did not change, but something behind it went still and attentive, and she said only that Derya should go on.

Bekir Onat owned the building where she and her mother lived, and two years ago, when her mother became ill, he had stopped taking their rent and had never started again. Her brother had been driving for his company for eighteen months. She had not known that the accused was that Onat until he was brought into the courtroom, and by then the session had already started, so she had promised herself that she would raise it at the break.

"I did not raise it at the break," she said. "I am raising it now, because I have interpreted six hours of evidence in a case in which I am not neutral, and the court needs to know that."

The judge took the glasses out of her hair, turned them over once, and asked whether the interpreting itself had been affected.

Derya answered that this was not for her to judge, and that she believed every word she had given the court was accurate. She also believed that she had stayed in that chair for three days for reasons which had nothing to do with the court.

"You understand what happens now."

"You will report me to the interpreters' board, the record will be examined, and I may lose my permit."

"You may," the judge agreed. "You have also cost this case a week, and there are very few people in this country who can do your languages." She said it without heat, as one professional to another describing the size of a hole in a wall. "Is that everything?"

"No." Derya put the two sheets of paper on the desk.

The note was a formal statement from the interpreter for the record, and it concerned two answers given by the witness Yusuf Aral on Monday and on Wednesday. The judge read the first page standing, then sat down and read both pages again, more slowly the second time.

The note accused nobody of anything. It explained that the island language marks every statement for the source of the speaker's knowledge, and that the mainland language has no equivalent form, so a faithful translation loses the mark completely. It stated that in two answers, both concerning the north door, the witness had begun with the form used for an action in which the speaker took part, and had then corrected himself to the form used for information received from others. It concluded that those answers were ambiguous in the source language and should be put again, with the ambiguity explained to the witness, by an interpreter who had no connection to any party.

"You are telling me that the witness lied," the judge said.

"I am telling you something narrower. A question was asked in one language and answered in another, and the answer contained something the question left no room for." Derya kept her hands flat on her knees. "That happens in every hearing in this building, and usually it does not matter. Here I believe it does."

Judge Aydin looked at her for a long moment before observing that she could have written the note after the trial, posted it, and kept her permit.

"He is going to be found guilty on Tuesday," Derya said. "A note in the post next month would have been about me rather than about him."

The judge put the pages into the file and told her that she was released from the case, and that she would not enter the courtroom again while it continued. The board would contact her in writing, and she should wait outside, because a signature would be needed.

The corridor was filling up. Derya sat on the wooden bench under the window while two young lawyers argued cheerfully about a parking space, and she discovered that her hands were shaking now, which seemed unfair, since the difficult part was already behind her.

At twenty to ten they brought Bekir Onat down the corridor, and when he saw her on the bench the officer let him stop, because a polite man in a good jacket is easy to be kind to.

"They have sent for another interpreter, from the island," he said. "Three days, they told me. That was your doing."

"Yes."

He looked at her the way he had looked past her in the doorway two years earlier, measuring something that was not in front of him, and then he asked whether her mother was well.

"She is the same."

Bekir Onat nodded slowly, said that he had not burned his own building, and walked on down the corridor without waiting to see whether she believed him.

# What the Fire Left

The new interpreter arrived from Karaada on the Tuesday ferry, a heavy young man with a soft voice, and Derya heard the rest of it as everybody else did, in pieces, from other people.

Yusuf Aral was recalled on the Thursday, and the question about the north door was put to him four times, with the difference between the two verb forms explained to the court in plain language. On the fourth time the old man stopped answering in seven words and talked instead for nineteen minutes.

He described the chain and the padlock, the instruction given to him in his second week by a manager who had since moved to another city, and the guard book with six years of his handwriting inside it. He described looking at the lights burning in the north hall at eight o'clock on the fourteenth of March and then going back to the gate. He also described the car on the coast road with its lights switched off and the two men at the fence, repeating that neither of them was Bekir Onat, and this time somebody wrote it down properly.

The guard book was found in a plastic box in the depot office. The court accepted that the chain had been ordered by the company and used every night for six years, and that the company had known that four men were working inside a building with one chained exit.

In the end Bekir Onat was found not guilty of setting the fire and not guilty of the false claim, because the evidence about the empty hall fell apart once the delivery notes were read properly. He was found guilty under the workplace safety law, and the case about Kemal Dogan's hands is still running, and will run for years, though everybody says the company will pay in the end. Nobody was ever charged with lighting the fire. Somewhere there are two men and a car, and there probably always will be.

Derya's permit was suspended for three months. The board's letter said that her declaration had been late but voluntary, that no error had been found in her translation of any answer, and that a suspension was the lightest measure available to it. She read the letter twice, folded it, and put it in the drawer with her mother's hospital papers, where the important things lived.

Bekir Onat came to the flat himself, as he had the first time, and stood in the doorway with his hands in his coat pockets while he told her that the building was being sold. She had until the end of the following month, and he would not ask for the two years.

"You could have kept quiet for one week," he said, "and nobody would ever have known."

"So could he. He kept quiet for three days, and in those three days you came very close to being finished."

He thought about that for a while, standing on the landing where the light from the stairwell was always grey. Then he said that her brother's job was safe, that he wanted her to know it, and that it was not a favour to her.

They found two rooms further up the hill, smaller, with a longer walk to the bus. Onur carried the boxes and did not speak to her for eleven days, and on the twelfth day he asked whether she wanted tea, which was as close to forgiveness as either of them was going to manage that year.

In August she saw Kemal Dogan in the corridor of the big court on the hill, waiting outside a room with a woman who must have been his sister. His right hand was inside a soft glove and he held a paper cup in his left, and he had no idea who she was. She did not tell him. She thought instead about eleven minutes at a door that would not open, which is not a long time unless it is happening to you.

Her permit came back in September, and her first case was a small one, a dispute about a fishing boat between two brothers who had not spoken for four years and who now had to explain a contract to a judge.

She sat at the narrow table with her notebook open and listened to the older brother begin. There it was in his second sentence, the ending that means I saw this with my own eyes, and there was no word for it on the other side.

Everything must pass through us, and we decide what crosses. Yusuf had said it as an accusation, and she had carried it around for six months before deciding that he was right about the first half and wrong about the second. She did not decide what crossed. She decided only whether the court was told that something had not.

"Your honour," Derya said, "before I translate this answer, the court should know that this language does something that ours does not."

The judge looked up, mildly surprised, and told her to go on.
