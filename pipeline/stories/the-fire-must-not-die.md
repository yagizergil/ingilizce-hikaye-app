# The Big Order

Selin pushed the shop door open with her foot. She carried two bags of flour, one on each arm. Her face was hot and her back hurt.

Her uncle Faruk stood by the long table. He was reading a small grey paper.

"Put them down," he said. "Then come here."

She dropped the bags by the wall. White dust went up into the light.

"Two hundred," her uncle said.

"Two hundred what?"

"Breads. Saturday morning, before eight." He gave her the paper. "The Yılmaz family. Their daughter is getting married."

Selin read the paper twice. On a normal day they made forty breads. Sometimes fifty, on the first day of the month.

"Two hundred is a lot," she said.

"It is. And we are going to make them." Her uncle sat down on the low stool by the wall. He moved slowly today. "They are going to pay on Friday. I need that money for the roof."

Selin looked up. There was a dark line in the ceiling above the flour shelf. Water came through it in April. Her uncle put a bowl under it and said nothing for two weeks.

"How much wood do we need?" she asked.

"More than we have." He smiled a little. "You are learning."

The oven took up half of the back room. It was old and round and made of stone. Her grandfather built it before Selin was born. It had one small iron door in front, black from forty years of fire.

The rule of the shop was simple. The fire never goes out. They fed it wood in the evening and again at night. In the morning the stones were hot all the way through. Cold stones make bad bread.

"I am going to call Hasan," her uncle said. "He can bring wood tomorrow."

"Is his wood dry?"

"It is always dry."

That night Selin stayed late. She washed the tables. She counted the bags of flour and wrote the number on the wall in pencil. At eleven she opened the iron door and put four pieces of wood inside. The heat hit her face like a hand.

She stood there and watched the fire for a minute. Orange light moved on the stone. She liked this part of the day, when the street was quiet and the room was warm.

Her uncle came to the door with his coat on.

"Go home," he said.

"I am going."

"Selin." He waited. "Your father says you must go back to school in September."

"I know what my father says."

"He is not wrong."

She closed the iron door and stood up. "Two hundred breads first," she said. "Then we can talk about September."

Her uncle laughed. It turned into a cough, and the cough went on too long. He put his hand on the table.

"Are you sick?" Selin asked.

"I am fifty-nine," he said. "It is the same thing."

On Wednesday the wood came. Hasan brought it in an old blue truck and put it in the yard behind the shop. It made a small mountain by the wall. Selin touched a piece. It was light and dry and it smelled good.

"Cover it," Hasan said. "There is rain coming on Thursday."

"There is always rain coming," her uncle said.

"This time from the sea. Big rain." Hasan got into his truck. "Cover it, Faruk."

They found the old green plastic sheet in the back room and put it over the wood. Selin pulled the corners down and put stones on them. Her uncle watched from the step.

"Good," he said. "Now go home and sleep. Thursday and Friday are going to be long days."

Selin walked home under a clear sky. She did not think about the wood again. She thought about two hundred breads, and about her father, and about September.

# The Night of the Rain

The rain started on Thursday at four in the afternoon.

It came fast and hard, with wind. Water ran down the street like a small river. Selin and her uncle stood inside the shop and watched the window go white.

"The sheet is on the wood," she said.

"The sheet is on the wood," her uncle said.

They made bread until nine. Fifty breads for Thursday, and the rest of the flour ready in bowls for the night. Selin's hands were white to the elbow. The room was hot and full of good smells.

At nine her uncle put his hand on the table again. This time he sat down on the floor.

Selin went cold all over.

"Uncle?"

"It is nothing." His face was grey. "Give me some water."

She gave him water. He drank it and put the glass down and did not stand up. Selin ran to the door of the shop next to theirs. The old man there had a car.

They took her uncle to the hospital at half past nine. The doctor was young and very tired. She asked many questions. She said the word "heart" three times and then she said, "He must stay here tonight."

"For how long?" Selin asked.

"Tonight. Maybe two nights. We are going to see."

Her uncle lay on the bed with a plastic thing on his finger. He looked small. Selin never thought about him as small before.

"The order," he said.

"Do not talk about the order."

"Selin." He moved his hand a little. "Call the Yılmaz family. Tell them no. It is fine. We can find the roof money another way."

"Sleep," she said.

She walked back through the rain. It was almost midnight. The streets were empty and shining. Her shoes were full of water.

She thought about the paper in her pocket. Two hundred breads, Saturday, before eight. She thought about the bowl under the dark line in the ceiling.

Then she stopped in the middle of the road.

Nobody put wood in the fire at eleven.

Selin ran. She ran badly, because of the water, but she ran. She opened the shop and went straight to the back room and pulled the iron door open.

Inside there was grey ash and one red line, thin as a hair.

She put two pieces of wood on it. She lay down on the floor and blew on the ash until her head hurt. The red line grew a little. Then it grew more. A small flame came up and took the wood.

Selin sat back on the floor and laughed at nothing.

"Now the rest," she said out loud.

She took the big basket and went out into the yard for more wood.

The green sheet was in the corner of the yard, against the fence. The wind pulled it off some time in the night. It sat there like a dead thing.

The wood was black with water.

Selin stood in the rain and looked at the mountain of wet wood. She picked up one piece. It was heavy and cold and it left water on her hand.

She tried it anyway. She put two wet pieces on the small fire. The flame went down. Smoke came out of the iron door and filled the room, white and bitter, and Selin coughed until she cried.

She pulled the wet wood out with the iron stick and threw it in the yard.

The clock on the wall said ten past one. She needed a hot oven at four in the morning. Two hundred breads take four hours, and the family comes at eight.

She had a fire the size of her hand, and no dry wood in the world.

# What the Grandfather Built

Selin sat on the floor by the iron door and made herself think.

Wet wood was no good. Wet wood killed the fire. So she needed dry wood, tonight, in the rain, at one in the morning.

She wrote a list on the back of the Yılmaz paper.

Hasan lived in the next town. No phone, and no road in this rain.

The wood shop by the bridge opened at eight. Too late.

Her neighbours had wood, but they used it for their own stoves in winter, and it was July. Their yards were empty.

She looked at the list. Then she looked up.

The back room had shelves on three walls. Big shelves, deep and wide, brown with age. Her grandfather made them from the same wood as the shop door. Her uncle put oil on them every spring. He did it slowly, with an old cloth, and he never let Selin help.

Selin stood up.

She was in the yard for a long time with a hammer. The first shelf did not want to come off the wall. She hit it and pulled it and hit it again. Something in her shoulder began to hurt.

Then the wood came away with a loud, dry sound, and she almost fell.

She carried the pieces inside. The wood was old and light and completely dry. She broke it small on the step, with her foot.

The fire took it and grew.

By two o'clock the flames were high and yellow and the stones began to change colour. Selin fed the fire and mixed the flour and water in the big bowls. Her arms shook. She put more shelves in.

At half past three she was on the second wall. At four she stopped and put her hand near the oven stone.

The heat pushed her hand back.

"Good," she said.

She worked all night in a straight line. Mix, wait, cut, put in, take out. She burned the first six breads because she was tired and the fire was hotter than usual. She threw them in the bin and did not look at them.

The seventh one came out brown and round and right.

At six in the morning she had eighty breads on the long table. Her feet were not working well anymore.

The door opened. Selin turned around with the iron stick in her hand.

It was Hasan. His hair was wet and there was mud on his trousers to the knee.

"Faruk is in the hospital," he said. "The old man from the shop told me. What are you doing?"

"Two hundred breads," Selin said.

Hasan looked at the table. Then he looked at the yard door, and at the shelves, and at the empty walls of the back room.

He was quiet for a moment.

"Where is my wood?" he asked.

"In the yard. Under the sky. Since Thursday."

Hasan closed his eyes. "Ah," he said.

"Yes."

"My truck is outside," he said. "I have four bags of dry wood in the back, for my own stove. Do you want them?"

Selin sat down on the stool for the first time in six hours.

"Yes," she said. "Please."

They finished at ten past eight. The Yılmaz family came in two cars. The father counted the breads twice and paid in old notes, and the mother took Selin's hand and said thank you four times. Nobody asked about the walls.

At eleven Selin went to the hospital with money in her sock. Her uncle was sitting up in bed and eating white cheese with a plastic fork.

"You are here," he said. "So you called them and said no."

Selin put the money on the small table.

Her uncle looked at it for a long time. He put the fork down.

"How?" he said.

"The fire went out."

"The fire went out?"

"The wood was wet. Everything was wet." Selin sat down on the chair by the bed. "So I burned the shelves in the back room."

Her uncle said nothing. Selin looked at the floor.

"All of them?" he asked.

"Almost all. Two are still on the wall."

He was quiet again. Then he made a small sound, and Selin looked up, and she saw that he was laughing without any noise at all.

"He built those shelves in one week," her uncle said. "He was very proud of them. He always said the same thing about that wood."

"What?"

"'It is good wood. It burns well.'" Her uncle wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. "Forty years I put oil on them. Forty years."

"I am sorry."

"Do not be sorry." He picked up the fork again. "Buy new shelves with the roof money. No. Buy the roof. We can make new shelves ourselves, you and me, in September."

Selin looked at him.

"September," she said.

"September," her uncle said, and he did not look at her, and he ate his cheese.
