# Five in the Morning

Selma pushed her cart through the market gate at five in the morning, the way she had done for eleven years. The stones were wet from the night rain, and her wheels made their usual complaining noise. She stopped at the corner, lifted the metal door of her stall and began to build her wall of apples. Red at the bottom, green above, and the small yellow ones in a bowl at the front, where children could reach them. Then oranges, then the last grapes of the season, then lemons in a line like yellow stones.

By six the hall smelled of bread and wet wood. Osman Bey, who ran the market office, walked past with his list and his little pencil. He counted the sellers every morning, as if some of them might have run away in the night.

"Cold enough for you?" he asked.

"It will get colder," Selma said.

She had the best place in the market, and she knew it. Everybody who came through the gate had to pass her table before they saw anything else. Eleven years ago she waited nine months for that corner, and when old Rıza gave it up she took it and never let go. The permit cost four thousand lira a year, and hers ran out on the last day of October. That was five weeks away. She had two thousand six hundred lira in a box under her bed, and the rest would come from the autumn selling, the way it always did.

At seven she heard hammering. Two men were building a table in the narrow space between her corner and the gate, where the office had kept broken boxes for years. They worked fast. By eight there was a roof of plastic sheet over the table, and a young man was carrying wooden boxes in from a small truck.

He was perhaps twenty-two, thin, with his sleeves pushed up to the elbow even in the cold. He set out pears, then more pears, then a few boxes of apples. He wrote his prices on brown paper with a thick black pen, and he wrote them very large.

Selma read the paper from where she stood. Pears, twenty lira. Her own pears were thirty-five, and they were good pears from a farm she had used for six years.

"Good morning," the young man said, when he saw her looking. "I am Kerem."

"You are in my light," Selma said, and turned back to her apples.

The morning customers came through the gate at half past eight, the way they always did. Mothers with empty bags, old men with nothing to do, two cooks from the restaurant on the main road. They passed the gate, and they stopped at the first thing they saw, and the first thing they saw was now a table of pears at twenty lira.

Selma sold to her regulars. Nesrin came and bought her usual two kilos of apples and stayed to talk about her son in the city. A man in a grey coat bought lemons. But the river of people that had always flowed past her table now broke in two before it reached her, and half of it never arrived.

At one o'clock the boy sold his last box of pears. He did not shout or call to anybody. He simply stood there and let people come, and they came.

Selma cut an apple in half with her small knife and ate it slowly, and it tasted of nothing.

She closed at six and counted her money on the metal table, note by note, the way her mother had taught her. Nine hundred and forty lira. On a normal Tuesday in October she took more than two thousand.

She counted again, because a person should always count twice. The number did not change.

Outside, the boy was tying his plastic sheet down against the wind. He lifted his hand to her when she passed. Selma pushed her cart out through the gate and did not lift hers.

# Twenty Lira a Kilo

On Wednesday she dropped her pears to thirty. On Thursday she dropped them to twenty-six and put out a sign of her own, and the sign did not help, because his said twenty and hers did not.

"You cannot sell below your cost," her son told her on the telephone. "You know that better than I do."

"I know what I know," Selma said, and changed the subject to his wife's cough.

She began to watch the boy the way you watch water coming under a door. He arrived at half past six, an hour after her, which meant he did not buy from the wholesale market at four like everybody else. His fruit came in a small blue truck driven by a man he called uncle, and the boxes were not the printed boxes of the big farms. They were old wooden ones with no name on the side. His pears were large and soft and ready to eat that day. They were not fruit that could sit on a table for a week.

By Friday her takings were under nine hundred again. She did the arithmetic on the back of a paper bag, and the arithmetic was not friendly. If October went on like this, she would have three thousand two hundred lira on the last day of the month, and she needed four thousand.

Nesrin leaned on the table and lowered her voice.

"They say he is somebody's nephew," she said. "That space has been full of broken boxes since before your time. Then a boy walks in and gets it in one morning? Money talks, Selma."

"Whose nephew?"

"Who knows. Somebody's."

Selma thought about that all afternoon, and the thought grew, the way such thoughts do. A rich family. A cheap price to push the old woman out of the corner, and then the corner would be free, and then the prices would go up again. She had seen this happen to a man who sold cheese, years ago, in another market.

On Thursday of the second week she walked into the market office.

Feride, the clerk, was eating soup at her desk with the papers pushed to one side. Selma asked, in a light voice, who had taken the space by the gate. Feride, who liked to be helpful more than she liked to be careful, turned the big register round on the desk so that Selma could see it.

The permit for the new stand was written in a neat hand. Vedat Aydın. Fruit and vegetables. Paid in full.

"That is not the name of the boy who stands there," Selma said.

"Isn't it?" Feride looked at the page and then at the door, and her face changed a little. "Well. The permit is in that name."

Selma knew the rule, because everybody in the market knew the rule. A permit belonged to one person, and that person had to stand at the table. You could not lend it to a cousin or hand it to a friend. Osman Bey had closed a stand in the spring for exactly that, and the man had shouted in the corridor for an hour and then gone home.

One sentence to Osman Bey. That was all. If she said it, the boy would be gone by Monday and the river of people would come back to her corner.

She stood there long enough for Feride to look up from her soup.

"Was there something else?"

"No," Selma said. "I was only asking."

She walked back to her table and sold lemons to a man in a grey coat, and the sentence sat in her mouth all afternoon like a stone.

That evening the boy was moving his boxes under the roof because the sky had gone the colour of old metal. He straightened up and rubbed his back with both hands, and for a moment he stood very still, looking at nothing.

"Rain tomorrow," he said to her.

"There is always rain in October," said Selma, and went home.

# Rain on Wooden Boxes

The rain came at eleven the next morning and did not stop. It came down through the market's old roof in three places, and the sellers moved their tables and swore at the ceiling. Nobody shops in weather like that. By noon the hall was almost empty and the only sound was water.

The space by the gate had no proper roof at all, only the plastic sheet. Selma watched the wind get under it and lift one corner, and then the corner tore, and the water went straight down into the wooden boxes.

The boy was not there. He had run out to the street for something, and his fruit was standing in the rain.

Selma told herself it was not her business. She told herself it for perhaps twenty seconds. Then she came out from behind her table with her arms full of empty sacks, and started dragging the wet boxes back under the edge of her own roof, one after another, and her shoulder complained about every one of them.

She was lifting the fourth box when the papers fell out of it.

They had been pushed down the side, between the wood and the fruit, and they were soft with water. Hospital papers. A long list of numbers, a date in August, a name printed at the top. Vedat Aydın. Selma read the total at the bottom of the page before she could decide not to.

"Please, don't—"

Kerem was standing in the gate with a roll of plastic under his arm and rain running off his hair. He crossed the floor fast and took the papers out of her hands, and then he saw the boxes she had already moved, and he stopped.

"You moved them," he said.

"Fruit is fruit," said Selma. "Nobody should let good fruit rot."

He put the wet papers inside his jacket. Then he sat down on the box he had just been given, as if his legs had decided for him, and he told her, because the papers had already told her half of it.

His father had fallen from a tree at the end of August, on their own land, picking their own fruit. The fall broke something in his back. He had been in the city hospital for six weeks, and there had been two operations, and there would be a third. Kerem's mother stayed there with him and slept in a chair. Kerem went up on the bus twice a week and came back the same night, because somebody had to be at the table in the morning.

"The fruit was ready in September," he said. "There was no one to pick it slowly, and no money to pay anybody, so we picked everything at once. My uncle brought it down in his truck. It is all ripe. If I keep it a week it goes bad, so I sell it in one day, and I sell it cheap, and cheap is better than nothing." He looked up at her. "I did not know it would take your customers. I am sorry. I truly did not think about it."

"And the permit," Selma said quietly. "It is in your father's name."

His face did not move for a moment. Then it did.

"You went to the office."

"I went to the office."

"He paid for the year in July," Kerem said. "It is his money. It is his fruit. He cannot stand at a table with a broken back, so I stand there instead." He stood up. "Say it to Osman Bey if you want. He will close me. Then I will sell from the truck at the side of the road, and the police will move me on, and I will sell somewhere else."

The rain hit the roof. Somewhere down the hall a man was banging a bucket into place under a leak.

"How much more do you need?" Selma asked.

"For the third operation, thirty thousand." He said the number the way people say a number they have said too often. "I have nine."

Selma thought about the box under her bed with two thousand six hundred lira in it, and about a permit that ran out on the last day of October, and about eleven years of standing in one corner in the cold.

"Tie your sheet down properly," she said. "It will blow all night."

# Two Names on One Paper

On Monday she was outside the market office at seven, before Feride had even taken her coat off.

Osman Bey listened with his hands together on the desk. He had heard a thousand complaints in that room and his face was ready for another one.

"The space by the gate," Selma said. "It is a bad space. There is no roof, and the water comes in off the street, and last Friday the whole of it was standing in the rain. Somebody will slip on that floor and it will be your problem."

"That space has been broken boxes for nine years, Selma Hanım."

"Then close it," she said. "Put the boxes back."

Osman Bey put his pencil down and looked at her properly for the first time.

"And the boy?"

"The boy comes to my table. I have twelve metres and I use eight. He can have three metres at the end, near the gate, and he sells his family's fruit there as my helper. The permit stays in my name. If he makes a mistake, it is my mistake, and you can come to me."

"A helper takes wages. A helper does not keep the money."

"Then he takes wages," Selma said, "and I take a share of what he sells, and what happens after that is between two adults who can count."

Osman Bey leaned back. Outside the office window a man was pushing a cart full of onions past the glass, very slowly.

"You had the best corner in this market for eleven years and you never shared it with anybody," he said. "Not once."

"I never had a reason."

He looked at her for a while. Then he opened the register, drew a line through one entry and wrote two lines under another, and turned the book round for her to sign, exactly as Feride had turned it for her ten days before.

"Four thousand on the thirty-first," he said. "Not the first of November. The thirty-first."

"I know the date," Selma said.

Kerem said almost nothing when she told him. He carried his boxes down to the far end of her table and built his pears into a low wall, and he wrote his price on brown paper again, and this time she let him keep it at twenty.

Something strange happened that week. The people who came through the gate no longer had to choose between the first table and the second, because there was only one table now, and it was long and full. They bought his cheap ripe pears for that evening, and then they took two kilos of her hard green apples for the week, and they bought lemons because the lemons were right there. Nesrin bought both and said she had known all along that it would end like this, which was not true, and nobody argued with her.

The takings climbed. Not to the old level, but they climbed.

On the last day of October, Selma counted at half past five with the money spread across the metal table. Three thousand eight hundred and twenty. She had counted twice already that afternoon and the number had not been kind either time.

Then two women from the restaurant on the main road came through the gate in a hurry, looking for oranges for a wedding meal, and they took the whole box.

She carried four thousand lira into the office at ten to six, and Feride wrote the receipt slowly, in her neat hand, while Osman Bey pretended to be busy with something else.

The market was almost empty when she came back. Kerem was tying down a proper roof now, a real one, bought with three weeks of pears, and the plastic sheet had gone into the rubbish where it belonged.

"They say my father will come home in the spring," he said. "He will walk with a stick, but he will walk. He asked me who the woman is that gave me the end of her table."

"And what did you say?"

"I said she is the one who has the best place in the market."

Selma pulled the metal door of her stall halfway down and stopped.

"When he is well enough to stand," she said, "he can stand at that end with you. The table has always been too big for one person."

They both knew that this was not true. Neither of them said so.
