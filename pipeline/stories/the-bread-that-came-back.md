# Bread After Seven

Elif turned the key in the front door at seven o'clock, then looked back at the counter behind her. Nine paper bags of bread were still sitting there, and none of them would be worth selling in the morning. She counted them twice, as if the number might change.

"Throw them out and go home," Burak said. He was washing the big metal trays, and his arms were white with flour up to the elbow. "You do this every night, so why is tonight any different?"

"Because tonight I counted them," Elif said.

She had opened the bakery six years before, on the corner where Mill Street meets the bus road. In the first months she baked twenty bags a day and sold nineteen of them. Now she baked fifty, sold forty, and carried ten out to the grey boxes behind the building. The bread was often still warm when she threw it away, and that was the part she had never got used to.

"There is a shelf in the back room," she said. "The one that used to hold the flour sacks."

"It has a broken leg," Burak said, "and the broken leg is the reason it lives in the back room."

"Then fix the leg."

He dried his hands slowly, because he knew that sound in her voice. The work took him fifteen minutes, two nails and a flat piece of wood. While he hammered, Elif filled the nine bags and folded the tops down neatly, exactly the way she did it for paying customers.

They carried the shelf out together and set it in the doorway, close to the window, where the roof kept the rain off. Elif wrote on a piece of brown paper: BREAD FROM TODAY. PLEASE TAKE. She taped the paper to the wall above the shelf, then stepped back into the street to look at it.

"Everything will be gone in ten minutes," Burak said. "And half of it will end up on the ground."

"Then at least it will be on the ground and not in a box," Elif said, and she locked the door.

She came back at half past four the next morning, when the street was still dark and the air still cold. The shelf was empty. She had expected that, and yet the sight of the bare wood made her happy in a way she could not explain. Then she noticed something lying in the middle of the shelf.

It was a wooden spoon. Someone had cut it by hand from a single piece of pale wood, and the handle was as smooth as glass under her fingers. When she turned it over, she found a tiny bird carved into the end of the handle, no bigger than her thumbnail, with two thin lines for wings.

Elif stood in the doorway holding the spoon while three cars went past and the lights came on in the flower stall across the road. Then she went inside, lit the ovens, and put the spoon on the shelf above the flour, where she could look at it while she worked.

Burak found it at six. "Somebody paid you," he said.

"Nobody paid me anything. The bread was free."

"Then somebody did not want free bread," he said, "and that is a completely different problem."

All morning, between customers, Elif thought about who might have taken the bags. There were men who slept near the bus stop in summer, and there were families in the tall grey buildings behind the market who counted every coin. Any of them could have carried nine bags away. But a spoon like that was hours of quiet work, and nobody gives away hours of quiet work by accident.

At seven that evening she filled seven bags and carried the shelf out again.

# The Rule About Streets

The bread went every night, and every night something small was left behind on the empty wood.

On Tuesday it was a bird made of dark wood, standing on a flat base, with its head turned as if it had heard a noise. On Thursday it was a small box with a lid that slid open, and the inside still smelled of fresh sawdust. On Saturday it was a comb, and on Monday a wooden ring that fitted the top of her tea glass. Elif put them all on the shelf above the flour, in a neat row, and Burak started calling that shelf "the bank".

She began asking questions. Şirin, who ran the flower stall, opened at six and had never seen anyone at the bakery door. Hakan parked his taxi on the corner until two in the morning, and he swore the shelf was still full when he drove home. So her visitor came after two and before half past four, in the coldest, emptiest part of the night.

On Wednesday Elif decided to wait. She sat inside in the dark with a glass of tea and her coat on, watching the doorway through the glass. Nothing happened for four hours. At two she went home to sleep for a while, and when she came back at half past four the bags were gone and a wooden whistle lay on the shelf.

On Friday she stayed until four without sleeping at all. Nobody came. The bread sat there untouched until half past five, when two boys on their way to work took a bag each.

"They can see you," Burak said. "Or they can see the light. If somebody wants to stay hidden, they will notice a woman sitting in a dark shop with a glass of tea."

Elif was still thinking about that on Monday, when a man in a grey jacket stopped in front of the shelf at eleven in the morning and began writing in a notebook.

"Mrs. Elif?" he said. "Kaya. I am from the town office."

He was polite, and he was not enjoying himself, which somehow made everything worse. There had been a complaint, he explained, and complaints had to be answered. He read the rule out loud from a folded paper: no goods, boxes, tables or furniture may stand on the street in front of a shop. He put a metal tape measure against the shelf, wrote down two numbers, and looked up at her.

"But it is my doorway," Elif said.

"The doorway is yours. The ground under the shelf belongs to the town." He closed the notebook. "You have seven days. After that there is a fine, and the fine is more than you earn in a week. I have seen your window prices, so I know that is true."

"And if the bread is free?"

"Free is not the problem. Furniture is the problem." He hesitated, and for a moment he sounded like a man rather than an office. "Put the bags on the counter inside. Tell people to come in. That breaks no rules at all."

He walked away towards the shoe shop, and Elif understood at once who had complained. Tarık had sold shoes on that corner for twenty years, and he complained about everything: the bus, the rain, the music from the barber's radio.

"So we stop," Burak said that evening, wiping the counter. "It was a nice month. Nine bags a night, thirty nights, that is a lot of bread that nobody threw away."

Elif looked at the row of wooden things on the shelf above the flour. A bird, a box, a comb, a ring, a whistle, a spoon.

"Six days," she said. "I have six days, and before that shelf comes inside, I am going to find out whose hands made these and thank them."

# Four Ten in the Morning

She did it properly on Tuesday night. There was no tea, no light, and no coat over the chair by the window.

Elif spread two empty flour sacks on the floor of the back room, lay down on them at ten o'clock, and set the alarm on her phone for three. Burak locked her in and took the key home with him. She slept badly, the way people sleep in their own workplaces, and she was already awake when the alarm went off.

She moved to the front of the shop and knelt behind the counter, below the level of the window. The street outside was orange under the lamps. A cat crossed the road twice. At ten past four she heard a sound she knew well, the small scratch of paper bags being lifted, and she raised her head.

There was a boy in her doorway. He was perhaps twelve years old, in a jacket too big for him, and he was putting all seven bags into a large cloth sack with the speed of someone who had done it many times.

Elif pulled the door open. "What are you doing?"

The boy jumped so hard that the sack hit the wall. Two bags fell out and rolled across the stones, and a folded piece of paper dropped from his pocket. He looked at her for one second with pure fear on his face, and then he ran towards the bus road and was gone before she had reached the corner.

Elif walked back, picked up the bread, and unfolded the paper under the street lamp. It was a list, written in careful pencil letters. Number 3, Mrs. Nurhan. Number 5, the two brothers, night shift. Number 7, the family with the baby. Number 11, leave at the door, do not knock.

She read it four times. She thought about a market two streets away where somebody could easily sell good bread for half price at seven in the morning, and she felt her face go hot. Beautiful wooden birds, and a boy who cleared her whole shelf in thirty seconds, and a list of houses. Perhaps Burak had been right, and perhaps she had spent a month feeling generous while somebody ran a small business out of her doorway.

She was angry all Wednesday, and she was still angry at seven in the evening, when she filled seven bags anyway and carried the shelf out. But that night she did not hide inside. She waited in Hakan's taxi on the corner with her hood up, and Hakan drank tea from a metal cup and asked no questions, which was why she liked him.

The boy came at twenty past four. He looked up and down the street twice before he touched the bread, and he filled his sack quickly, and then he walked away fast with the weight banging against his leg. Elif followed him on foot, two hundred steps behind.

He went past the market, past the water tank, and into the tall grey building behind it, the one with no lift and a broken light in the entrance. Elif caught the door before it shut and went up after him.

On the first floor he set a bag against a door and knocked once, softly. On the second floor he left one bag at number 5 and one at number 7. Then he climbed to the third floor, where he put a bag down in front of number 11 and did not knock at all, exactly as the list said.

When he turned round, Elif was standing on the stairs.

The sack in his hand was almost empty now. He did not run this time. He stood very still, with the light of the broken bulb flickering above him, and he looked much younger than he had looked in her doorway.

"Are you going to stop it?" he said.

# The Shelf That Folds

His name was Emre, and he lived on the ground floor with his grandfather, in two rooms that smelled of wood and of soup.

The old man was sitting by the window in a chair with a blanket over his knees. A cloth was spread on the table in front of him, and on the cloth there were tools with worn handles, a half-finished bird, and a pile of pale curls of wood. He looked at Elif, then at his grandson, then at the last bag in the sack.

"You brought the baker," he said.

"She followed me, Grandpa."

"That is not the same as bringing her, but sit down anyway." He moved his hand towards the other chair. "Osman. Forty-one years I made doors and windows in this town. Then two years ago my legs stopped agreeing with me, so now I make small things instead of big ones."

Elif sat down. She put the folded pencil list on the table between them, and Emre went red to the ears.

"He takes it all," she said. "That is what I saw. I thought he was selling it."

"He takes it all because Mrs. Nurhan is eighty-three and cannot walk to your corner," Osman said calmly. "The brothers at number 5 finish work at four in the morning. The people at number 11 would rather die than let a neighbour see them take free bread, so he leaves it and does not knock." He picked up the half-finished bird and turned it in his fingers. "I told him one rule. We do not take anything we cannot answer. So he takes bread, and I answer with what my hands can still do."

Elif looked at the tools, and at the boy standing in the doorway with the empty sack, and she found she had nothing sensible to say for several seconds.

"The town office came on Monday," she said at last. "There is a rule about furniture on the street. I have four days, and then the shelf comes inside or I pay a fine."

"Then we meet you at the back door," Emre said quickly.

"No. A back door is for you, and the shelf is for everybody. There is a man at the bus stop who will never knock on my back door."

Osman was not listening any more. He was looking at her hands as if they were a drawing. "Tell me about your doorway," he said. "How deep is it? Two steps back from the street? Three?"

"Two long steps."

"And the wall inside the doorway, on the left, under the window. Is it yours?"

"Every brick of it."

The old man reached for a piece of newspaper and a pencil, and he began to draw. A shelf, he said, does not need legs if it has a wall. This one would hang on her own wall, inside her own doorway, on two metal pins, so that nothing whatever touched the ground of the town. In the daytime it would fold up flat against the bricks like a closed shutter, and at seven in the evening she would fold it down and load it with bread.

"Nothing standing on the street," he said. "Nothing sticking out past your door. Let your man from the office measure it as long as he likes."

Elif took the newspaper to the town office the next morning and put it on Mr. Kaya's desk. He studied the drawing for a long time, tapped the pencil lines twice, and told her that if the shelf hung on her wall and stayed behind her doorway, it was not his business. Then he added that food given away must be in closed bags with the date written on them, and Elif said that she had been writing the date on her bags for six years.

She went to see Tarık after that. It turned out his complaint had never been about bread at all: people had been standing in front of his window at eleven at night, arguing over the bags and dropping paper on the stones. So Elif promised him that the shelf would be empty by five, and that she would sweep the corner herself every morning, and Tarık, who had not expected to win anything, agreed to say nothing more.

Osman built the shelf in three days. He would not take money for it, so Elif took twelve of his birds and spoons instead and put them on her counter with a small price card, and she carried the money to him every Friday. She also asked Emre for a copy of the pencil list, because a baker who knows the number of doors can bake for them instead of guessing.

On Friday evening she folded the new shelf down, and it held eleven bags without a sound.

She baked fifty-two the next morning, and she threw none of them away.
