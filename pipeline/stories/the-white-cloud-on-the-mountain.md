# Forty-One Sheep

Ferhat counted the sheep twice.

The first time he got forty-one. The second time he got forty-one again.

He stood in the gate and let the last animals push past his legs. They smelled of rain and grass. They went into the yard and started to eat from the box by the wall.

"Forty-one," he said.

His grandmother was on the step with a bowl in her hands. She stopped moving.

"There are forty-two," she said.

"I know."

"Count them again."

"I counted twice, Nene."

She put the bowl down. She came across the yard slowly, with one hand on the wall. Her right leg was bad since the winter. The doctor in the town said one word: rest. She did not rest, but she walked like this now, slow and careful, like a person on ice.

She looked at the sheep. She did not count them like Ferhat, one, two, three. She just looked.

"The young grey one," she said. "With the black face."

Ferhat felt something cold in his stomach.

The young grey one was the smallest animal in the group. It was five months old. It always walked at the back, and it always stopped to eat things it did not need to eat.

"Where did you go today?" his grandmother asked.

"Up to the flat rocks. Then along the water and down."

"Past the old wall?"

"A little past it."

His grandmother looked at the mountain.

The mountain stood behind the village, big and grey and green. In the morning it was clear. Now, at six o'clock, there was a long white cloud across the top of it, and the cloud was coming down.

"That is bad," she said quietly.

Ferhat's father worked in the city and came home twice a year. His mother died when he was four. There were two people in this house and forty-two sheep, and now there were forty-one.

"I am going to go up," Ferhat said.

"You are thirteen."

"I know how old I am."

His grandmother did not shout. That was the worst part. She just stood and looked at the mountain, and she pushed her hand into her side.

"That cloud is going to be on the flat rocks in one hour," she said. "Inside a cloud you cannot see your own feet. People die on that mountain. Not old people. Young people, in a hurry."

"Then I am going to be fast."

"You are not going to be fast. Fast is how people fall." She turned and went into the house.

Ferhat stood in the yard. He was angry and his face was hot. Then his grandmother came out again with things in her arms.

She put them on the wall, one by one, and she said the name of each thing.

"Your grandfather's coat. It is too big. Good. Bread. Water. Rope — the short one, from the wood. Matches, in a bag, dry. This lamp. Two batteries." She stopped. "And this."

It was a small metal box.

"Open it at the top of the field," she said. "Not before."

"What is inside?"

"Open it at the top of the field."

Ferhat put everything in the old bag. His grandmother watched his hands.

"Now listen to me," she said. "The mountain has three rules and you must not forget them. One: never walk down a place you cannot walk up. Two: if the cloud comes, you stop. You do not walk in a cloud. You sit down and you wait for it to move."

"And three?"

"Water always goes down to the village. Always. If you are lost, find water and follow it."

"Water always goes down," Ferhat said.

"Say it again."

"Water always goes down to the village."

She held his face in her two hands for a second. Her hands were hard and warm.

"Come home," she said. "With the animal or without it. Do you hear me? With it or without it."

Ferhat went out of the gate and up through the village. Dogs barked behind walls. An old man on a step lifted his hand. The road stopped being a road and became a small hard line in the grass.

At the top of the first field he stopped and opened the metal box.

Inside there was a small mirror, and a folded piece of paper.

He opened the paper. There was a drawing on it in old pencil. He saw the mountain, the rocks, the water, a line of trees. Next to a small square was one word: HOUSE.

His grandfather made this map. His grandfather died six years ago.

Ferhat looked up. The white cloud was lower now. It covered the top of the mountain like a hand over a mouth.

He put the paper in his coat, next to his heart, and he started to climb.

# Inside the Cloud

The path went up beside the water.

It was not a big river — just a fast, cold line coming down over the stones, loud in some places, quiet in others. Ferhat kept it on his left hand. The grass was wet and his shoes were wet in ten minutes.

He called as he walked.

"Hey! Hey!"

His voice went up and came back off the rocks. Nothing answered.

After forty minutes he reached the flat rocks. They were big and grey and flat like tables. From here he saw the whole village below. Small brown houses, small white smoke, the school with the blue roof.

He turned around and looked up.

The cloud was there.

It was not above him anymore. It was in front of him, on the ground, a grey-white wall across the mountain. He watched it move. It came down over the stones slowly, like water going into a room.

"Stop when the cloud comes," his grandmother said. "Sit down and wait."

But the young grey one was five months old, and the night was coming, and the wolves in these mountains were not a story. They took two sheep from the village last year.

Ferhat walked into the cloud.

The world went away.

It happened faster than he expected. In ten steps the flat rocks behind him were grey shapes. In twenty steps they were nothing. The light did not go dark — it went white, a thick white with no direction in it. He put his hand out and he saw his own fingers, and past them, nothing.

He stopped. His heart hit hard.

Then he heard the water on his left, and he was fine again. He kept walking with the sound of the water on his left. Left. Left. He counted his steps out loud.

At two hundred steps, the sound of the water was on his right.

Ferhat stood still.

He turned his head slowly. Water on the right. He turned his body. Water in front of him. He walked five steps towards it and it was behind him.

The cloud took the sound and moved it around. There was nothing to look at. He did not know which way was up the mountain and which way was home.

He was lost.

He sat down. He sat down hard, on a wet stone, because his legs told him to.

"Do not walk in a cloud," he said. "Do not walk in a cloud. Do not walk in a cloud."

He took out the bread and he ate a piece, and his hands stopped shaking. He put the lamp on the stone next to him, but he did not turn it on. He was going to need it later.

The cloud moved past him. Sometimes it opened a little and he saw twenty metres of grey rock, then it closed again.

He waited. He did not know for how long. There was no sun and there was no shadow.

Then, in one of the small open moments, he heard something.

It was not water. It was a short, thin sound, and it came twice.

Ferhat stood up. "Hey!"

The sound came again. It was an animal. It was very close and it was below him.

He went down on his hands and knees and he moved forward, very slowly, touching the ground in front of him. The cloud opened a little more.

Two metres in front of his hands, the mountain stopped.

There was an edge — a straight drop, and white cloud under it, and nothing else. Ferhat pulled himself back and lay on the wet grass with his face against the ground.

The sound came again, from under the edge.

He put his head over, carefully, and he looked down.

Three metres below him there was a narrow shelf of rock, not much bigger than a table. On the shelf, with its back against the stone, stood a small grey sheep with a black face.

It looked up at him and it made the thin sound again.

"I see you," Ferhat said. His voice did not sound like his voice. "I see you. Do not move."

# Water Always Goes Down

The rope was short.

Ferhat pulled it out of the bag and looked at it, and he knew the answer before he measured it. It was maybe four metres. Enough to go down. Not enough to go down, tie an animal, and pull it up while he stood on wet grass at the edge of a drop.

And he was not strong enough for that anyway. He was thirteen and the young sheep was heavy.

He lay on the grass with his head over the edge and he thought.

"Never walk down a place you cannot walk up," he said.

Then he looked along the edge, to the left, into the moving cloud.

The rock was not the same everywhere. Here it was straight. But five metres away it went down in three big steps, like a broken wall.

Ferhat crawled along the edge to the steps. He turned around, put his feet down first, and went backwards, slowly, with his hands full of wet stone. Step one. Step two. Step three.

His feet touched the shelf.

The sheep pushed straight into his legs and almost pushed him off the rock.

"Hey! Hey, stop." He held it with both arms. It was shaking. There was blood on one of its back legs, but the leg moved, and the animal stood on it.

Ferhat sat down on the shelf with his back against the mountain. The animal was against his chest. For a moment he did nothing at all.

Then the cloud opened.

It opened wide, for maybe half a minute. Ferhat saw the whole side of the mountain. And he saw that the shelf was not a shelf. It was the top of a long green line of grass. The grass went down and down, between two walls of rock, all the way to the trees.

And in the middle of that green line, there was water.

Water always goes down to the village.

The cloud closed again. But now he knew.

He took the rope and tied it around the animal's body, under the front legs, the way his grandfather tied things on a horse. Then he tied the other end around his own hand.

"You go first," he said. "I hold you. If you fall, you take me too. So do not fall."

The way down was long.

It was not a path. It was wet grass on stone. In some places Ferhat went down sitting, on his back. His feet were in front of him and the rope was tight in his hand. The sheep did not like it. Twice it stopped and did not want to move. Both times he sat next to it and waited. Then he pulled softly, and it came.

The dark arrived while they were still in the trees. Ferhat turned on the lamp. It made a small yellow circle, and outside the circle everything was black, but he did not mind. The water was on his left now and it was loud. It stayed on his left for a long time. Every ten minutes the ground went down a little more.

At about nine o'clock the trees stopped and he was in a field with a stone wall.

He knew that wall. He walked along it with his hand on the stones like an old woman.

Somewhere in front of him, dogs started to bark.

Then he saw lights — not one, many. There were lamps and telephones and moving yellow points all along the bottom of the mountain. Voices were calling a name. The name was his.

Ferhat stood in the field and he did not shout. He was too tired to shout.

Instead he took the small mirror out of his pocket. He put it in front of his lamp. He turned it, left and right, left and right.

The lights below stopped moving.

Then all of them, together, started to come up the hill.

His grandmother was not with them. She was in the yard, on the step, exactly where he left her. Her leg was bad. And she was too proud to sit inside.

The men brought him into the yard at half past ten. Somebody carried the sheep. Somebody else carried Ferhat's bag and gave him tea in a glass. The man told him he was a stupid boy. Then he put a hand on his head.

His grandmother stood up.

She looked at the sheep first. She looked at the cut on its back leg, and she made a small sound with her mouth, and she said, "That is nothing. Two weeks."

Then she looked at Ferhat.

"You went into the cloud," she said.

"Yes."

"After I told you."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"And then I stopped," Ferhat said. "I sat down on a stone and I ate bread and I waited. And I found water and I followed it down."

His grandmother was quiet for a long moment. The men in the yard were quiet too.

"Good," she said.

She put her hand out. Ferhat gave her the piece of paper from his coat. It was his grandfather's map, wet now, and soft at the folds.

She looked at it for a long time in the light from the door.

"Keep it," she said, and she gave it back. "You are going to need it. There are forty-two sheep in this yard, and that grey one is never going to learn."
