# The Bench by the Fountain

Old Kemal sat on his bench near the fountain, like every morning.

He fed the birds first. Small pieces of bread, one at a time. Then he watched the water fall and thought about nothing much.

That morning, he saw something under the next bench. Brown, flat, half open. He walked over slowly and picked it up.

A wallet.

He opened it. Inside were many banknotes, more than he made in a month from his small pension. There was also a card with a name and photo: a young man with dark hair.

Kemal looked around the park. Nobody was running back, nobody was searching the ground. Only two children playing near the trees, and a woman walking her dog.

He sat back down with the wallet in his hands.

His own money problems came to his mind fast. His roof leaked when it rained. His daughter needed money for her son's school books. His pension was small, and prices were high this year.

Nobody saw him find it, he thought. Nobody was going to know.

Then he thought about the young man in the photo. Maybe this money was for rent. Maybe it was for medicine. Maybe the young man was searching for it right now, feeling sick with worry.

Kemal put the wallet in his coat pocket and stood up.

He walked to the small police station near the market. It was only ten minutes away, but his legs were slow that day. He passed the bread shop, the shoe shop, and the old cinema, now closed.

Inside the station, a young police officer sat behind a desk, writing something.

"I found this," Kemal said, and put the wallet on the desk. "In the park, under a bench."

The officer opened it and counted the money. He wrote numbers on a paper.

"That is a lot of money," the officer said. He looked at Kemal carefully. "You brought all of it?"

"Yes."

"Many people keep it and say nothing."

"I thought about that," Kemal said, honest. "For maybe one minute. Then I thought about the man's face on the card. He looks young. Maybe he needs it more than he knows."

The officer wrote Kemal's name and address on his paper.

"We are going to call the owner," he said. "His name and number are on the card. If he does not come, the money is yours after some months. That is the law."

Kemal thanked him and walked home slowly, his pocket lighter, his mind strange and quiet.

That night, he told his daughter, Ayla, about the wallet.

"You gave it all back?" she asked. "Baba, we needed that money. The school books, the roof—"

"I know," Kemal said. "But it was not my money."

Ayla did not look happy. She looked at her son's old school bag, torn at one side, and said nothing more.

# The Man from the Photo

Three days later, someone knocked on Kemal's door.

Kemal opened it. A young man stood there, the same man from the photo, but tired, with red eyes.

"Are you Kemal?" the man asked. "The police gave me your address. I am Burak."

"Come in," Kemal said. "Sit down."

Burak sat at the small table in Kemal's kitchen. His hands moved fast, nervous.

"I want to say thank you," Burak said. "That money was not mine. It was my mother's money, for her operation. I lost it two days before the hospital date. I thought her operation was not going to happen."

Kemal poured tea for both of them.

"Is your mother okay now?" he asked.

"She had the operation yesterday," Burak said. "Because of you." His voice broke a little. "I want to give you some of the money. Please."

Kemal put down his tea cup.

"No," he said. "I did not find it for money. I found it because it was not mine to keep."

"But you have problems too," Burak said. "The police officer told me. Your roof, your grandson's books."

Kemal's face went red. He did not know the officer said this.

"That officer talks too much," he said, but not angry.

"Please," Burak said again. "My mother wants to meet you. She wants to say thank you herself."

Kemal thought about it. He thought about his own mother, many years ago. She taught him one thing above all. Never take what is not yours, even when nobody is watching.

"I can visit," he said. "But I do not want money for it."

The next day, Kemal walked to the hospital with Burak. Burak's mother sat up in her bed, weak but smiling.

"You are the man," she said. "The honest man."

"I only did the right thing," Kemal said.

"Many people do not," she said. "My son searched all night. He called the police, he called his friends. Nobody had good news. Then, in the morning, they called and said an old man brought it back, every note."

She reached for a small bag next to her bed and pulled out a book.

"This was my husband's," she said. "He passed away last year. He loved birds, like you feed in the park. Burak told me you sit by the fountain every morning."

Kemal took the book carefully. It was old, with pictures of birds from this country.

"I cannot take your husband's book," he said.

"You can, and you must," she said. "It is not money. It is a thank you from my heart."

Kemal held the book against his chest and did not argue anymore.

# What the Book Held

Kemal walked home with the bird book under his arm.

Ayla was waiting at the door when he arrived.

"Where were you?" she asked. "I was worried."

"At the hospital," Kemal said. "Visiting the family from the wallet."

He sat down and opened the book on the table. Pages and pages of birds, painted by hand, old and beautiful. Between two pages, something fell out.

A small white envelope.

Kemal opened it. Inside was money — not as much as the wallet, but still a good amount — and a short note.

*For the roof. And for your grandson's books. Please take this. It is a gift, not a payment. — Burak's mother.*

Ayla read the note over his shoulder, her eyes wide.

"Baba," she said, "you have to take it now. It came in a book, not in your hand. That is different."

Kemal laughed a little, the first real laugh in days.

"Maybe you are right," he said. "A gift is not the same as a reward."

He looked at the money for a long moment. Then he thought about Burak's mother, sitting up in her hospital bed, smiling at him like an old friend.

"I am going to take it," he said. "But I am going to write to her first. She must know I did not ask for this."

He wrote a short letter that evening. Ayla helped him find the words. He thanked the family, said workers were going to fix the roof soon, and asked about her health.

The next week, workers came and fixed Kemal's roof. His grandson got new books, with clean pages and no torn corners. Life did not change much more than that, but it felt lighter somehow.

Every morning, Kemal still walked to the bench by the fountain. He fed the birds, one small piece at a time, and now he read from his new bird book between the pieces of bread.

One morning, a boy from the neighborhood sat down next to him.

"Is that a good book?" the boy asked.

"It is," Kemal said. "A friend gave it to me."

"What kind of friend?"

Kemal thought about how to answer that.

"The kind you make," he said, "by doing the right thing, even when it is hard."

The boy did not understand fully, but he said okay and watched the birds eat the bread. Kemal turned the page and smiled at a painting of a small brown bird, wings open, flying home.
