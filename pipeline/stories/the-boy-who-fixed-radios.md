# Three Radios, One Voice

The third radio was the one that made Kerem stop working.

It was a brown wooden box from the sixties, with broken glass in the front and a smell of dust inside. A taxi driver had left it on the desk that morning and said that it made a high noise instead of speaking. Kerem had cleaned the switches, changed two wires that had gone soft with age, and closed the back again. Now, at nine in the evening, he turned the little wheel slowly across the numbers to hear his own work.

A woman's voice came out of the speaker, low and calm, as if she were sitting in the next room.

"This is Deniz," she said. "Tuesday. The lamp is lit. If you can hear me, please answer."

There was a long silence, and then the same words came again, in the same order, with the same small pause in the middle. After that there was nothing but a soft sound, like rain falling on a roof.

Kerem did not move, because he had heard those words twice already that week, and both times he had told himself that it meant nothing.

He pulled his notebook out from under the desk and turned to the last page. Saturday, nine o'clock: the grey kitchen radio that belonged to Mrs. Aydın, two streets away. Monday, nine o'clock: the old ship's set from the box of broken things behind the door. He had written both times in pencil, with a question mark after them, because he had not wanted to believe his own ears.

Three radios. Three different sizes, three different ages, three different owners. One voice.

The shop had stood on Fener Street for forty years, and the sign above the window still said SELIM RADIO AND REPAIR in tired blue letters. Kerem was sixteen, and he came down after school every day. He could find a bad wire faster than his uncle now, though he never said so out loud. He had lived in the flat above the shop for three years, since the night his father's boat did not come back. His mother worked in a hospital two hundred kilometres away, and she telephoned every Sunday at six.

Kerem wrote the new line in his notebook and looked at it for a long time.

If the voice had come from one radio, he would have blamed the radio. Old sets pick up rubbish; everybody in the trade knows that. But three machines with nothing in common could not all make the same woman say the same sentence at the same minute.

Somebody real was speaking. Somebody real was waiting for an answer.

He turned the wheel back to the place where he had found her. The soft sound came, and then a Greek station, and then music from a ship somewhere out in the dark. He listened until half past eleven, and the voice did not return.

The next morning his uncle came down at seven and put water on to boil behind the desk. Selim was a heavy, slow man with grey hair and burnt fingers, and he liked to eat bread and cheese standing up while he watched the street.

"Uncle," Kerem said, "who is Deniz?"

The water began to boil. Selim did not turn around.

"Why do you ask me that?"

"There is a woman on the air at nine o'clock every night. She says, this is Deniz, and then she says the lamp is lit, and then she asks somebody to answer her."

Selim put down his bread. When he finally turned, his face had gone very still, the way it did when a customer asked for a repair that could not be done.

"Old sets pick up strange things," he said. "Voices travel a long way at night. Leave it alone, Kerem."

"But it is the same words every time."

"Then it is a machine playing a tape somewhere, and it is nobody's business." Selim picked up his tea and went to the door. "You have five radios waiting on that shelf. Start with the green one."

Kerem started with the green one. All morning, while his hands worked, his head was somewhere else. He did not want the puzzle explained away. He wanted a name, a street, and a house with a light in the window.

# A Loop of Wire

The trick was older than the shop. Selim himself had taught it to him two winters before, without knowing what it would be used for.

A loop of wire does not hear the same in all directions. When the flat face of the loop points at the sender the voice comes in strong, and when the edge points at it the sound almost dies. The quiet place is easier to find than the loud one, so you turn the loop until the voice nearly disappears, and then you know the line that the signal is travelling along. Stand in a second place, take a second line, and the two lines cross. Where they cross, somebody is talking.

Kerem built his loop over two evenings from a broken picture frame and thirty turns of wire. He joined it to a small battery radio, wrapped the whole thing in an old shirt, and hid it behind the boxes in the back room.

On Wednesday his uncle found it.

"What is this?"

"A loop," Kerem said. "For checking the old sets."

Selim held the frame in both hands and looked at it for a long moment. He did not shout. That was somehow worse, because Selim always shouted about small things and went quiet about large ones.

"Some signals are not for us," he said at last. "A man can spend his whole life following a voice. At the end he finds a dead machine in an empty room."

"Then I will find a dead machine and I will stop."

His uncle put the loop down on the bench, very carefully, and went back to the front of the shop. He did not say yes, and he did not take the frame away either. Kerem decided that this was as close to permission as he was going to get.

On Thursday he went out at twenty to nine with the loop under his coat.

The town was quiet. The shops on Fener Street were shut, the ferry had stopped running, and the wind carried the smell of fish and oil up from the water. Kerem climbed the steps to the school yard, which stood high enough to see the whole bay, and he waited there with the radio pressed against his ear.

At nine exactly, she spoke.

"This is Deniz. Thursday. The lamp is lit. If you can hear me, please answer."

His hands shook so much that he almost dropped the frame. He turned the loop slowly, listening, and the voice grew thin and faded until it was only a shadow of itself. He held it there and read the direction off the school wall, using the corner of the building as a line. North-east. Towards the hill.

He ran down through the empty streets to the port wall and waited eleven minutes, and she spoke again. This second line also pointed north-east, a little to the left of the first one. Where the two crossed on his rough map there was only one thing marked: the old weather station on the hill above the town.

Kerem went home, slept badly, and thought about a light burning in a building that everybody said was empty.

He climbed the hill on Friday night. It took him fifty minutes, and the last part was loose stone, and twice he sat down in the dark to get his breath back. The weather station was a low grey building with no glass in the windows, and its door had fallen off the frame years ago. Inside there was a broken chair, a great deal of dust, and the cold smell of a place where nobody has been for a long time. No wires. No power. No lamp.

He sat down on the step at nine o'clock, and the woman's voice came out of the little radio in his pocket, as strong as ever, telling him that the lamp was lit.

Kerem laughed once, and then he felt sick. Everything he had done that week was wrong. He walked home with the loop banging against his knee and did not look up until he reached the shop.

He was almost asleep when he understood his mistake.

He had listened from two places on the same side of town, only three hundred metres apart. Two lines that start close together and run in nearly the same direction do not really cross; they lie down beside each other for kilometres. Any small mistake in the first line becomes a giant mistake at the far end. He had drawn two lines that were almost the same line, and then he had believed the place where they touched.

He got out of bed, lit the lamp, and looked at his map again. If he wanted to know where the voice lived, he had to stand somewhere completely different.

Across the water, for example.

# Two Hundred Metres

The first ferry on Saturday left at half past seven in the morning, which was useless, because Deniz only spoke at night. So Kerem told his uncle he was going to help a friend carry boxes. He took the last ferry across the bay at eight and sat on the cold stone of the fish market on the other side, with his coat over the loop.

From there the town looked small and yellow, a handful of lights between the hill and the sea.

At nine o'clock the voice came, patient as always, and Kerem turned the loop until it nearly died away.

The line did not point at the hill. It pointed low and to the left, into the middle of the town. When he drew it on his map the next morning, it cut across his two old lines in a place that made him put down his pencil.

The crossing point was on Fener Street. It was less than two hundred metres from the desk where he had first heard her.

He walked the street that evening with the radio turned down low. This close, the loop was almost too good. The voice was loud everywhere, and he had to use the quiet edge very carefully. Past the closed shops. Past the shut bank. The signal held steady outside the bread shop, where the smell of hot bread still hung in the air at nine at night. Then it rose suddenly at the door beside it, the door with the four bells and the broken glass.

Kerem knew that door. He had carried a radio through it himself, in the spring, when it was raining too hard for an old woman to walk home with a box in her arms.

Third floor. Mrs. Nesrin.

She brought a set in almost every month, always the same one, a small green table radio that never had much wrong with it. Selim charged her half price and made tea, and Kerem had always thought that she came because she was lonely.

He stood in the street for five minutes, and then he went up.

The stairs smelled of bread and soap. When he knocked, there was a long silence, then the sound of something being switched off, and then the door opened on a chain.

"Kerem," Mrs. Nesrin said. "Is the shop on fire?"

"I can hear you," he said. "On the radio. Every night at nine."

The old woman looked at him through the gap. She was small and straight, with white hair tied up, and her eyes went to the shape under his coat.

"You made a loop," she said.

"Yes."

"Your father made one too, when he was younger than you." She took the chain off. "You had better come in before the whole street learns my business."

The flat was warm and very clean. She led him past the kitchen to a back room, and when she opened the door, Kerem stopped and stared.

There was a table under the window, and on it stood a sending machine, built out of ship parts, grey metal and glass and hand-cut wood. A thick wire ran up the wall and out through a hole in the frame to the roof. And on a small shelf by the window, burning quietly in a glass bowl, there was an oil lamp.

"The lamp is lit," Kerem said.

"It is a true sentence," Mrs. Nesrin said. "I never saw the point of saying false ones."

She made him sit down, and she told it plainly, the way people tell things they have carried for a long time.

The Deniz was a fishing boat, and her son Yusuf had worked on it for nine years. It went out on a clear evening in November and did not come back. The sea gave up nothing at all: no wood, no coat, no name written on anything. Four men. She said their names in order, and the third name was Ahmet, and Ahmet was Kerem's father.

"I know who you are," she said. "I have known since the day you were born. Why do you think I bring that green radio down every month, when there is nothing wrong with it?"

Kerem looked at his hands.

"Do you think they can hear you?" he asked.

"No." She said it without any weight, as if he had asked whether it would rain. "Yusuf is under the water, and so is your father. I am not a fool, and I am not waiting at the window. But I sat in this room for four months and said nothing to anybody. Then one day I climbed up to the roof and put that wire there. Now I speak for two minutes every night, and the words go out over the sea. Somewhere they are still travelling."

She turned the lamp down a little.

"Also," she said, "twice a stranger has answered me. Once a boy in Bulgaria, and once a ship near Cyprus. That was a good year."

On the table, under her reading glasses, lay a folded letter with the port office mark on the corner.

# A Clean Line

"They came on Monday," Mrs. Nesrin said. "Two young men in a car. They were polite."

Kerem read the letter twice. It did not name her, because they did not know her name yet. It was a general notice to the street: a signal without permission had been cutting across the port channel at night, and a fishing boat had missed a weather message because of it. A van with loops on the roof would search the area from Friday.

"I will stop," she said. "I am seventy-four years old. I am not going to fight the port office."

"Nobody has to stop," Kerem said, and he was surprised at how sure he sounded. "Your machine is moving."

He opened the back of it while she watched. It was beautiful work, honestly, but the part that held the signal steady had been made from whatever Yusuf could take off a dead boat, and it had grown old. Warm parts, cold nights, old wire: the signal had been wandering across the numbers for months. It moved a little further every week, until it walked into the channel that the port used. She was not sitting on the boats; she was pressing against them, slowly, and she had never known it.

"If we hold you in one place, and a clean place, you never touch them again," he said. "And we cut the power. You do not need to reach Bulgaria. You need to reach the sea and the next few streets."

"And where does a boy find such a part?"

"In a locked drawer," Kerem said, "under my uncle's desk."

He went home at midnight and turned on the light over the bench. Selim came down the stairs before Kerem had even got the drawer open.

They looked at each other for a while.

"Nesrin," Selim said. It was not a question.

"You knew."

"For two years." His uncle sat down heavily on the wooden chair by the bench. "I found her the same way you did, with a loop, in the winter. I never knocked on her door. I thought that if she wanted company, she would have asked for it." He put a hand over his eyes. "And I did not tell you, because you were thirteen and you had stopped eating. I was afraid of what it would do to you to hear a woman say your father's name into the dark every night."

"It did not do anything bad to me," Kerem said. "It made me build a loop."

Selim laughed, and the laugh turned into a long breath. Then he got up, opened the drawer with a key, and took out a flat wooden box. Inside, in rows, lay the small parts that hold a radio to one exact point, each one marked in ink with its number. Ship parts, thirty years old, and legal, because Selim had had permission to send signals since he was twenty-two.

"This one," he said, picking one out and holding it up to the lamp. "Nothing there but weather and empty air. Bring my green bag."

They worked in Mrs. Nesrin's back room on Thursday night for four hours. Kerem fitted the new part and rebuilt the holder so that the heat could not move it. Selim made the roof wire shorter and cut the power down low, and he checked the whole thing with the shop's tools while the old woman made tea. She told them both, again and again, that they were making a great deal of noise for two men who were trying to be quiet.

At ten past two in the morning, Selim stood back and cleaned his hands on a cloth.

"It will not move again," he said. "Not while I live, and probably not while she does."

The van came on Friday. Kerem watched it from the shop door as it went slowly up Fener Street, with its own loop turning on the roof. He made himself keep cleaning the floor. Twenty minutes later the van came back down, turned towards the port, and did not stop anywhere at all.

At nine that evening, Kerem sat at the desk and turned the big set to the new number.

"This is Deniz. Friday. The lamp is lit. If you can hear me, please answer."

The voice was clean, with nothing under it, closer than it had ever been. Kerem looked across the shop at his uncle. Selim was standing by the shelf with a cloth in his hand, and he had not moved for the whole two minutes.

Then Kerem reached over and switched on the sending machine that his uncle had not used in eleven years. He pulled the small speaking box towards him and pressed the switch.

"Deniz, this is the shop on Fener Street," he said. "We hear you."

The air was quiet for so long that he thought the machine had failed. Then a small sound came out of the speaker.

"Say that again," Mrs. Nesrin said.

He said it again. Behind him, without a word, his uncle carried a second chair over to the desk and sat down.
