# Forty Boxes

Baran counted the boxes in the big room. Thirty-nine, forty. His whole life went into forty boxes.

Tomorrow his family was moving to a new city, three hours away. His father found a new job there. Baran was going to start a new school in one week.

He did not want to think about the new school. He wanted to think about one thing only: his grandfather's clock.

The clock stood in the corner of the big room. It was tall, with a yellow face and a slow, quiet tick. His grandfather made it with his own hands, many years ago, before he died.

"That clock goes in my van seat," Baran said to his mother. "Not in a box."

His mother was writing names on boxes with a black pen. "It is very old, Baran. It needs a soft box and a lot of paper around it."

"No boxes," Baran said. "I want to hold it myself, all the way there."

His mother looked at him for a moment. She understood.

"OK," she said. "You can hold it there. But be careful. It is heavy."

That evening, a big yellow van arrived outside their building. Two men in blue shirts got out and started carrying boxes. Baran's father directed them, calling out which room each box came from.

"Careful with the clock!" Baran said, again and again.

"We know, we know," said the taller man, Ihsan. "We do this every day."

By nine at night, every box was inside, along with the beds, the table, and the sofa. Only the clock stayed in the empty room, wrapped in a soft blanket.

"We leave at six in the morning," Ihsan said. "We do not wait long in this city. Too many other jobs today."

Baran slept badly that night, on a mattress on the floor. He kept thinking about the clock, alone in the dark, empty room.

At five-thirty, his alarm rang. He got up fast and ran to the big room.

The blanket was there. The clock was there. He breathed out slowly.

"Good," he said quietly. "You are still here."

At six o'clock, the yellow van started its engine outside. Baran picked up the clock, wrapped in its blanket, and carried it downstairs himself. Every step felt important, like carrying something alive.

He put the clock on the seat between him and his mother. He held it with both hands.

"Ready?" his father asked from the front seat.

"Ready," Baran said.

They pulled away from the building. Baran looked back at his old home one last time. Then he looked down at the clock in his arms.

Three hours to the new city. Three hours, and then a new life.

# Somewhere on the Highway

They stopped after one hour, at a small gas station. Everyone got out to stretch their legs.

"Fifteen minutes," Ihsan said. "Then we go again."

Baran put the clock down on his seat, still wrapped in the blanket, and walked to the small shop for water. His mother went with him. His father talked with Ihsan about the road ahead.

When Baran came back, the second worker, a man named Cem, was moving boxes around in the back.

"I am making more space," Cem explained. "Some boxes shifted during the drive."

"Careful," Baran said. "There is a clock on the front seat."

"I saw it," Cem said. "Do not worry."

They got back in and drove another hour. Baran talked with his mother about the new apartment, the new school, the new street. He almost forgot to check on the clock.

Then he looked down at the seat.

The blanket was there. But it looked flatter than before.

"Mom," Baran said slowly, "where is the clock?"

His mother turned and looked. She lifted the blanket. Under it were two small boxes, not the clock.

"Stop the van!" Baran shouted.

Ihsan pulled over at the side of the road. Everyone got out and opened the back doors. Boxes filled every space, packed tight and high.

"The clock must be here somewhere," his father said. "It cannot disappear."

"Cem moved boxes at the gas station," Baran said. "Maybe he moved the clock too, by mistake."

Cem's face turned pale. "I moved a big soft package," he said. "I thought it was a pillow box. I put it near the back."

They started pulling boxes out onto the grass beside the road. One box. Two boxes. Ten boxes. Cars passed fast on the highway, and the noise made it hard to think.

"It has to be here," Baran said, his voice shaking. "It has to be."

His mother put a hand on his shoulder. "We are going to find it," she said. "Keep looking."

Baran pulled out another box, then another. His hands moved faster and faster. The pile of boxes on the grass grew taller than him.

Near the very back of the van, under three boxes of kitchen things, he saw a soft, blanket-wrapped shape.

"There!" he shouted.

# The Clock Ticks Again

Baran pulled the package out carefully and unwrapped the blanket a little, just enough to see the yellow face.

The glass was not broken. The hands still pointed to the right time. He held it against his chest and closed his eyes for a second.

"It is fine," he said. "It is really fine."

Cem looked happy too. "I am sorry," he said. "I was moving fast. I did not check carefully."

"It is OK," Baran said. "We found it."

They put all the boxes back in, this time with the clock on top, close to the doors. Everyone saw it there. Baran held it in his arms for the rest of the trip.

Two hours later, they turned onto a quiet street with new, strange buildings. Baran looked out the window at his new home for the first time. It was smaller than the old building, with a small balcony and a red door.

The workers carried the boxes inside, one by one. Baran carried the clock himself, straight to the corner of the new room. He set it down carefully and stepped back.

"It needs a new spot," his mother said. "But this corner looks right."

The clock did not need electricity or batteries. It only needed to sit still for a moment, and then its slow tick started again, the same sound from his old home.

His father came in and stood beside him, listening.

"Your grandfather is going to be happy about this," his father said, and he smiled at his own words.

Baran laughed a little. "I think he knows," he said.

That night, they ate dinner from paper plates, sitting on boxes instead of chairs. The apartment was full of unopened boxes, strange shadows, and new sounds from outside. But under all of it, Baran heard one thing he knew well: tick, tick, tick, from the corner of the room.

He walked past it before bed and touched the wood softly with one finger.

"We made it," he said quietly. "Both of us."

Outside, the yellow van drove away into the dark, on its way to another family's move. Inside, the clock kept its old, steady time in its new home.
