# A City with No Words

Selin arrived at the hotel at ten at night.

The city was new to her. She did not speak the language here, only a few words she learned from a book on the plane. Hello. Thank you. Where is the bathroom.

The man at the front desk did not speak English or Turkish. He looked at her ticket and her passport, and he pointed at a form on the desk.

Selin filled in her name, slowly. The man watched, then smiled and gave her a key with a metal number on it. Twelve.

"Room twelve," Selin said, to check.

The man nodded and pointed up, then to the left. Selin understood: up the stairs, then left.

She found room twelve at the end of a long hall. The door was old and heavy, with a small light above it. She put the key in and turned it.

The door did not open.

She tried again, turning the key the other way. Still nothing. She pushed the door with her shoulder. It moved a little, but it did not open all the way.

Selin looked at her watch. Half past ten. She was tired from the trip, and her legs hurt from walking all day. She just wanted to sleep.

She went back down to the front desk, but the man was gone. A young woman stood there now, writing something in a book.

"Excuse me," Selin said. "My door. Room twelve. It does not open."

The young woman looked at her with a confused face. She said something in her own language, fast, and Selin understood nothing.

Selin held up the key and pointed at the number twelve. Then she made a pushing shape with her hands. She showed a door that did not move.

The young woman's face changed. "Ah," she said, and picked up a phone.

Two minutes later, a tall man in a grey uniform came down the stairs. He held a set of tools in a small bag.

He walked with Selin back to room twelve. He looked at the door for a long time, then he pushed it with his hand, near the bottom.

He said something to her and made the same pushing shape she made before.

Selin laughed a little, in spite of everything. "Yes," she said. "Exactly that."

# The Door That Sticks

The man with the tools worked on the door for twenty minutes.

He tried the key, then he tried a small tool between the door and the wall. He talked to himself the whole time, in his own language. One time he laughed, and Selin laughed too, but she did not understand the joke.

Finally, the door opened with a loud sound.

The man said something and made a small bow with his head. Selin understood this without words: it was fixed, but only for tonight.

"Thank you," Selin said, in his language this time. She learned that word from the book too. It was one of the only words she remembered correctly.

The man smiled, a big smile, and said something back. Then he pointed at the door and shook his head, and pointed down the hall.

Selin did not understand at first. Then she saw: he wanted to move her to a different room. This one was going to break again.

"A different room?" Selin asked, in English, and pointed down the hall too.

The man nodded fast, happy that she understood.

He picked up her bag fast, before she said anything. He walked down the hall to a door marked eighteen. He opened it with a different key, turned on the light, and stepped back for her to look inside.

The room was small but clean, with a bed near the window and a lamp on a small table. It looked warm and comfortable.

"This is better," Selin said, and smiled.

The man smiled back, put her bag by the bed, and left the room. He closed the door softly behind him.

Selin sat down on the bed. It was almost eleven now. She thought about the whole day — the long flight, the strange streets outside, the broken door. She thought about the man with the tools too. He did not speak one word she understood, but he still helped her without stopping.

She did not need words for that. She only watched his face and his hands, and she understood everything important.

Tomorrow she was going to walk around the city with her small book of words. She was going to buy bread at a shop and say thank you to the person who sold it. She was going to get lost, probably more than once, and someone was going to help her find her way back, probably without words too.

Tonight she just wanted to sleep in her new room, room eighteen. It had a warm lamp, and its door opened the first time she tried it.

She turned off the light and closed her eyes. Outside her window, the city moved and talked in its own language, full of sounds she did not know yet.

She was going to learn some of them. Not all. But some.

That felt like enough for one trip.
