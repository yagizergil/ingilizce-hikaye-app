# A Locked Door at Nine

At nine o'clock on a Tuesday morning Osman put his hand on the door of the tailor's shop, and the door did not move. He pushed again, harder, because doors sometimes stick in October when the rain comes. The glass stayed cold under his hand, and the shop behind it stayed dark.

A white card had been taped to the inside of the window. The letters on it were straight and careful: OPEN AT FOUR.

Osman knew that handwriting the way he knew his own. Hüsnü had written the prices on his receipts in those same narrow letters for forty years. Osman knocked twice, waited, and then knocked a third time with the edge of a coin, which normally brought Hüsnü out of the back room in about ten seconds. Nothing moved inside.

The young man who sold vegetables next door was putting out boxes of green peppers. He looked up without much interest.

"He opens at four now," he said. "Since the spring."

"Since the spring? It is October."

"Then since the spring," the young man said, and went back to his peppers.

Osman had spent the summer at his daughter's house in another city, and he had come home only nine days ago. In forty years he had never once found that door locked in the morning. Hüsnü opened at nine, closed at seven, and took his tea at four while standing up, because a man who sits down after tea does not stand up again.

Osman had come with a reason, and the reason was in a cloth bag under his arm. Inside it lay three and a half metres of dark grey wool, which he had bought two years earlier and kept under his bed. His granddaughter Elif was getting married on the twelfth of November, and Osman intended to stand beside her in a suit made by Hüsnü. Not a suit from a shop window. Not a suit sewn by a machine in a country he would never see.

Hüsnü had made the suit Osman wore on his own wedding day. He had also made the black one that Osman wore when Fatma died, and he had made it in two days and refused the money.

Osman put his face close to the glass and looked in. The long cutting table was there, with the wooden ruler lying across it. The iron stood on its stand in the corner. On the wall at the back, half in shadow, a brown jacket hung on a hook, and even through the dirty window Osman could see that something about the left shoulder was wrong.

He stepped back. The rain was starting again, thin and cold, the kind that gets inside a collar.

"Four o'clock," he said to nobody.

He walked home slowly, past the bank and the bread shop, and he counted the possible reasons on the way. Hüsnü had money trouble. Hüsnü had found another job. Hüsnü had grown lazy at last, at seventy-four, after forty years. None of these reasons fitted the man, and Osman knew it before he reached his own street.

# Four O'Clock

The shop was open at four, and the warm smell came out into the street: steam, wool, and the sharp clean smell of the iron.

Hüsnü was standing at the table with his back to the door. He had lost weight in the summer. His shirt, which he had certainly sewn himself, hung loose across the shoulders.

"Osman," he said, without turning. "You are back."

"I got back last week." Osman put the bag on the table. "I came this morning. Twice."

"The card is in the window."

"I read the card. I am asking about the man behind it."

Hüsnü opened the bag and took out the grey wool. He held it up to the light, felt the edge between his finger and his thumb, and something in his face got younger for a moment.

"This is good cloth," he said. "You bought this two years ago."

"You remember."

"I remember cloth. People I forget." He laid it flat and smoothed it with his palm. "The wedding is when?"

"The twelfth."

"Then we have time, if you stand still and stop talking." Hüsnü took the tape measure from around his neck. "Arms down."

Osman stood while the old man measured him, and it was the same as it had always been: the tape around the chest, the tape down the arm, the small pull at the shoulder. Only one thing was different. Hüsnü did not write the numbers down. He said them out loud and asked Osman to write them in the book.

"Your writing is better than mine," Hüsnü said.

"My writing is terrible."

"Then we will both be surprised at the fitting."

They laughed, and it was almost normal, and then Hüsnü reached for the chalk to mark the collar line. The chalk went out of his fingers and broke on the floor into three white pieces.

Neither man looked at it for a second. Then Hüsnü bent down slowly, picked up the largest piece, and put it in his pocket.

"Come on the fifth," he said. "In the afternoon. Not the morning."

"Hüsnü."

"The fifth."

Osman looked past him at the brown jacket on the hook. From inside the shop he could see it properly now. Somebody had sewn the left sleeve into the shoulder with stitches that ran crooked, in and out, like a line of ants that had lost their leader. Half of them had already been taken out again.

"That is a bad piece of work," Osman said carefully.

Hüsnü did not turn round. "The customer changed his mind about the shape."

"Whose work is it?"

"A shop does not tell you the business of every jacket in it." Hüsnü folded the grey wool with two quick movements. "The fifth, Osman. Go home. It is raining."

Osman went out. On the pavement he turned up his collar, walked six steps, and then stopped and looked back through the window, the way a man looks back at something he does not want to have seen.

Hüsnü had sat down on the wooden stool. He was holding his right hand inside his left, pressing it, the way you hold a bird that is trying to get away.

# Oranges at Half Past Eight

Osman decided that Emine must be ill.

It was the only answer that made sense to him. Hüsnü's wife was two years older than her husband, and a man who spends his mornings washing and feeding somebody cannot open a shop at nine. Hüsnü would never say such a thing out loud, because he came from a family who thought that talking about trouble was a kind of begging.

So on Thursday morning Osman bought a kilo of oranges and went to the house behind the mosque, and he knocked at half past eight.

Emine opened the door with a cloth in her hand and her sleeves pushed up to the elbow. She was not ill. She was cleaning the hall floor, and she was doing it fast.

"Osman Bey!" She looked at the oranges, and then at his face, and then she looked worried in a different way. "Has something happened?"

"No. Nothing." He stood there feeling like a fool. "Is Hüsnü at home?"

"At this hour?" she said. "He left at seven, as always."

"Seven."

"He takes the bus from the square." She stepped back to let him in. "Come in, the floor is wet, walk by the wall."

Osman sat at the kitchen table with the oranges in front of him and asked the question in the plainest words he could find.

"Emine Hanım. Where does he go at seven in the morning?"

She stopped moving. She put the cloth down on the edge of the sink, dried her hands slowly, and sat opposite him, and Osman understood that she had wanted somebody to ask her this for a long time.

"He told me you knew," she said. "He said, Osman is my oldest friend, of course I have told him."

"He has told me nothing."

"No." She smiled without any happiness in it. "Of course not."

Then she told him. In January, on an ordinary cold morning, Hüsnü had woken up and had not been able to hold his tea glass. The right side of his face had not moved properly for two days. The hospital had kept him for a week, and since then he had gone across the city three mornings a week to do exercises for his hand with a young woman who was younger than his own daughter.

"He can still cut," Emine said. "Cutting is his eyes and his brain, and those are fine. But before noon the hand shakes, and the tablets take three or four hours to work properly. By four o'clock he is steady. So he opens at four."

"And in the mornings he tells people what?"

"He tells them the shop opens at four." She turned her wedding ring round with her thumb. "In August he made a jacket for the son of the man with the electrical shop. The sleeve came out crooked. The man laughed about it in the tea house, and by the evening the whole street had heard, and Hüsnü did not eat for two days."

Osman thought about the brown jacket on the hook, half unpicked, hanging there like something the old man was punishing himself with.

"The doctor says he should stop working," Emine said. "He says the shop is finished, why do you tire yourself. And Hüsnü says nothing, and gets up, and goes."

"What do you say?"

"I say we need the money, which is true, and I say a man of seventy-four should rest, which is also true." She looked straight at him. "But if that shop closes, he will be in this house all day with me, and he will be dead in a year. I know it, and he knows it, and we do not discuss it."

Osman stood up. His knees hurt from the chair and from something else.

"Do not tell him I told you," Emine said at the door. "Please."

# Two Pairs of Hands

At four o'clock that afternoon Osman went to the shop and did the stupidest thing he had done in ten years.

"About the suit," he said. "My son has found a ready-made one in the city. Grey, good cloth. It fits well enough. There is no need for you to tire yourself."

Hüsnü was standing at the table with the wooden ruler in his hand. He put it down very carefully, as if it might break.

"You went to my house," he said.

"I took oranges."

"You took oranges at half past eight in the morning, because you wanted to see whether my wife could stand up." Hüsnü's voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. "And now my friend of forty years cannot even lie to me properly. A ready-made suit. You."

"Hüsnü—"

"I do not want your pity. Take your cloth."

He pushed the bag across the table, and Osman took it, because there was nothing else to do, and he went out into the street with his own good grey wool under his arm.

For two days he did not go near the shop. On the third day he walked in the other direction instead, two streets over, to the dry cleaner's, where a young woman called Sevgi mended zips and turned up trousers behind a glass counter.

Osman remembered something. Five or six years earlier, this girl had stood in Hüsnü's shop and asked him to teach her, and Hüsnü had said that he had no room and no time.

"He said no to me once," Sevgi said, when Osman had finished talking. She had her arms folded. "Why should I come now?"

"Because he cannot manage alone, and he will never ask you."

"That is his problem, not mine."

"It is," Osman agreed. "I am asking you for my own reasons. My granddaughter is getting married in twelve days and I want a suit."

Sevgi laughed at that, and then she stopped laughing and looked at the ceiling for a while.

"I would want proper money," she said. "Not a favour. Not tea and thank you."

"Ask him for proper money. He respects that more than anything."

She came the next afternoon at four, and Hüsnü looked at Osman with an anger that could have cut cloth.

"I do not take students," he said.

"I am not a student, I am a pair of hands," Sevgi said. "I can sew a straight line for eight hours and I never get bored. I cannot cut. Nobody in this city cuts like you, and when you stop, nobody will."

She walked past him to the back wall, took down the brown jacket, and turned it in the light.

"Whoever did this was tired," she said. "May I take the stitches out?"

Hüsnü did not answer. She sat down on the stool with the small knife and began, and after a minute the old man moved behind her and said, "Not there. Start at the bottom of the sleeve and work up, or you will pull the cloth out of shape."

Osman stayed by the door and said nothing at all.

Later, when Sevgi had gone, Hüsnü washed the two tea glasses in the little sink and spoke without turning round.

"And on Tuesday and Thursday mornings? What do I tell people?"

"Tell them you are at the hospital," Osman said. "You are seventy-four years old. Nobody in this street will be surprised, and nobody will care, and in a month they will have forgotten."

"They laughed about the sleeve."

"They laughed about one sleeve in forty years." Osman picked up his hat. "If the door stays shut all morning, they say the old tailor is finished. If it opens at nine with a young woman sewing inside, they say he has taken a partner. You choose which story they tell."

The first fitting happened on the fifth, in the afternoon, and Hüsnü marked the collar with a piece of chalk that stayed in his fingers. The suit was finished on the tenth, two days early, and it fitted so well that Osman's daughter cried in the hall, which she later blamed on the wedding.

Hüsnü came to the wedding in a jacket of his own, sat beside the door, and told anybody who would listen exactly how much work goes into a shoulder.

The following Tuesday Osman walked past the shop at nine in the morning. The light was on. Sevgi was at the machine with the wooden ruler beside her, and on the cutting table lay a length of blue cloth with white chalk lines drawn across it, straight and careful, in a hand that everybody on that street could recognise.

The old card had gone from the window. A new one was taped in its place: OPEN AT NINE. Underneath, in smaller letters, it said: Tuesday and Thursday mornings, the master is at the hospital.
