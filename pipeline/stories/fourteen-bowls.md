# The Cupboard Is Full

Yusuf opened his door at seven, and the bowl was there again.

It stood on the top step, on a small blue cloth. He bent down slowly. His back always hurt in the morning. He lifted the bowl with two hands and carried it inside.

It was warm. Not hot. Warm, like a cat.

He put it on the kitchen table and took off the plate on top. Red soup, with rice in it. Small pieces of meat. It smelled of lemon.

"Again," he said to the empty room.

Yusuf lived in flat two, on the ground floor of an old grey building. Twelve flats, four floors, and a lift that never worked. He was seventy-one. His wife died four years ago. After that the flat became very quiet, and he learned to talk to the walls.

The soup started in November. The first bowl came on a Tuesday. Then one came on a Friday. Now it was February, and the bowls came every Tuesday and every Friday.

He opened the kitchen cupboard and looked inside.

The bowls stood there in two lines, clean and white. He counted them with his finger.

Fourteen.

That was the real problem. A bowl is not a letter. You cannot keep a bowl. Somebody in this building has an empty cupboard now, and Yusuf has their fourteen bowls.

He ate the soup at the table by the window. It was good soup, better than his own. He washed the bowl, dried it, and put it with the others.

Then he went out to the hall and looked at the stairs.

The hall light was broken. It was broken for a long time, and nobody fixed it. In the morning a little grey light came from the street door, and that was all. A person can walk down these stairs in the dark and nobody sees a face.

Yusuf climbed to the first floor and knocked on flat three.

Mrs Nalan opened the door. She was small and round and she always had the television on very loud.

"Good morning," Yusuf said. "I want to ask a strange question."

"Ask."

"Do you cook soup for me?"

Nalan looked at him. "Soup?"

"Somebody leaves soup at my door. Tuesday and Friday. In a white bowl."

"Not me," she said. She looked interested now. "How long?"

"Since November."

"November!" She put her hand on the door. "And you do not know who?"

"No."

"Yusuf, that is not normal." She shook her head. "Ask the boys on the third floor. Young men always want food, but sometimes they cook too much."

He asked the boys on the third floor. Two students, both from another city, both very polite and very tired. They laughed. They ate bread and eggs, they said. Nothing with rice.

He asked the family in flat eight. The father worked at the post office. He said no. He said it kindly, but he said no.

He asked the woman in flat eleven, on the top floor. Her name was Sevgi and she worked nights at a hotel. She opened the door in her coat, with her bag in her hand. She was going out.

"Soup?" she said. "I am sorry. I do not have time to cook for myself."

"Of course," Yusuf said. "Sorry."

"I hope you find your cook." She smiled and went down the stairs, fast, and her steps got smaller and smaller in the dark.

Yusuf stood on the top floor for a moment. Then he went home.

That night he made a plan. It was a simple plan. He is going to stay awake, and he is going to open the door, and he is going to say thank you.

He sat in his chair by the door with the lights off. He put his radio on very low. He waited.

He woke up at half past eight in the morning, in the chair, with a bad neck.

The bowl was on the step. The soup was cold.

# Flour on the Floor

Yusuf took his medicine at ten o'clock every night. That was the trouble.

The doctor gave him small white pills for his heart, and the pills made him heavy. After the pills his eyes closed by themselves. He tried strong tea. He tried standing up. Nothing worked.

"So do not fight the sleep," he said to the wall. "Be clever instead."

On Thursday he went to the shop on the corner and bought a bag of flour.

That night, before his pills, he opened his door and looked at the hall. The floor was old stone, dark grey. Perfect.

He put flour on the floor in front of his door. Not much — a thin white line, like snow. Then he put more flour on the first three stairs. He closed the door and went to bed.

He slept like a stone. He always did.

In the morning he opened the door and looked down.

The bowl was there, warm, with a plate on top. And around it, in the flour, there were footprints.

Yusuf got down on his knees. The stone was cold through his trousers, but he did not care.

The prints were small. Very small. He put his own foot next to one, and his foot was much bigger.

"A child," he said.

Then he stopped. Maybe not a child. Maybe a woman with small feet. He was not sure, and he wanted to be sure.

He looked at the stairs. There were prints on the first stair and the second stair. On the third stair, nothing. The flour there was clean and flat.

So the person came down from above, walked to his door, and went back up. Somebody in this building. That was something. That was not nothing.

But there were ten flats above him.

He cleaned the flour with a wet cloth, and while he cleaned he thought about the door of the building. That heavy street door made a loud sound. Every night it woke him for one second, and then he slept again.

The soup was always warm. Warm soup does not travel far. It comes from inside the building, from a kitchen upstairs.

On Tuesday he tried something new. He tied a piece of string across his door, low, near the floor. He put a small bell on the string. His wife bought that bell for the cat, many years ago. The cat died before her, but the bell stayed in the drawer.

He went to bed and waited for the sound.

In the morning the string was on the floor, and the bell was quiet. Somebody untied it, carefully, and put it down. The bowl stood on the step. Chicken soup this time, with carrots.

Yusuf sat on the step with the bell in his hand and started to laugh. He laughed until he coughed.

"You are good," he said to the empty stairs. "You are very good."

Then he had his last idea. He is going to change the clock, not the trap.

He went to Nalan on the first floor and asked her one question. What time do people leave this building in the morning?

Nalan knew everything about the building. She counted on her fingers. The students go at eight. The post office man goes at seven. The family in flat nine goes at half past seven, with the children.

"And Sevgi?" Yusuf asked.

"She comes home at six in the morning," Nalan said. "And she goes to work at eight in the evening. Poor woman. She never sleeps."

"Who stays with her daughter at night?"

Nalan looked at him. "You do not know? Her mother stays. The old lady in the wheelchair. She cannot walk, but she can watch a child."

Yusuf went down the stairs very slowly. He was thinking about a kitchen on the top floor. He was thinking about a mother who leaves at eight in the evening. And he was thinking about a bowl of soup, still warm at seven in the morning.

The times did not fit. Nothing fitted. But now he knew where to stand.

# Four O'Clock in the Morning

Yusuf did not take his pills on Monday night.

He knew it was a bad idea. He put the small white box on the table and looked at it for a long time. Then he pushed it away and made himself a coffee instead.

At midnight he was awake. At one he was awake and his chest felt strange, but he was awake.

He sat on a chair inside his flat with the door open ten centimetres. The hall was black. He watched the small grey line of the street door and he listened.

At two o'clock a car passed outside. At three o'clock the building made a sound in the walls, the sound old buildings make, like a long breath.

At ten past four he heard a door open upstairs.

It was very soft. Then feet on the stairs — quick, light feet, and somebody who knew every step. No hand on the wall. No light.

Yusuf's heart was loud. He waited.

A shape came down and stopped at his door. It bent down, put something on the step, and picked up the empty bowl from yesterday. Small hands. Quick hands.

Yusuf opened his door.

The girl jumped back and hit the wall. The empty bowl fell and rolled and stopped, but it did not break.

"Do not run," Yusuf said quickly. "Please."

She stood there in the dark. She was about ten years old. She wore a big coat over her pyjamas, and her hair was in one thick plait. She looked at the street door, and Yusuf saw her thinking about it.

"I am not angry," he said. "I am the man with the fourteen bowls. I want to give them back."

The girl did not move. Then, in a very small voice, she said: "Fifteen. That one is number fifteen."

"Fifteen," Yusuf said. "Yes. You are right."

"Please do not tell my mother."

"Sit down," Yusuf said, "and tell me why."

She sat down on the step. He sat down too, slowly, on the other side of the bowl.

Her name was Nehir. Her mother was Sevgi, from flat eleven, the woman who worked nights at the hotel.

"My mother cooks at three in the morning," Nehir said. "She comes home at six, she sleeps, she gets up at two in the afternoon, and then she cooks. Big pots. She puts the soup on the small fire and it stays warm all night."

"And you bring it down."

"My grandmother wakes me at four. She cannot walk, so she cannot do it. She has a stick and she touches my bed with it." Nehir smiled a little. "It is our job. Mine and hers."

Yusuf looked at the soup in his hands. "Why me?"

Nehir was quiet.

"Nehir. Why me?"

"Last winter our heater broke," she said. "It was very cold in our flat. My grandmother is old and she got sick. My mother went down to ask the man in flat eight. He said his heater was broken too. Then somebody knocked on our door. There was a heater in the hall, and nobody there."

"Ah," Yusuf said.

"It was your heater. My mother saw you from the window, going back inside." Nehir looked at him. "She wanted to say thank you. She went to your door with money. You did not open."

"I do not open the door in the evening," Yusuf said. "It is a stupid habit of an old man."

"So she said, fine. We are going to say thank you in the kitchen." Nehir put her chin on her knees. "She says a thank you is not a thing you say. It is a thing you do, quietly, for a long time."

The building was completely silent. Somewhere far away a dog barked twice.

"Your mother is a serious woman," Yusuf said.

"Very serious."

"And you untied my bell."

Nehir laughed into her coat. "I saw the string. Grandmother said you are going to try something."

Yusuf stood up. His knees made a loud sound and Nehir laughed again.

"Come inside for one minute," he said. "I need help. Fourteen bowls are heavy for me."

They put the bowls in two shopping bags, seven and seven. Nehir carried one bag and Yusuf carried the other, and they climbed to the fourth floor together in the dark. She counted the stairs for him in a whisper.

At the door of flat eleven, Nehir stopped.

"She is going to be angry," she said.

"With you? No." Yusuf put the bags down. "Tell her this. Tell her the soup can come on Tuesday. Not Friday."

"Why not Friday?"

"Because on Friday I am going to cook," Yusuf said. "I make good rice. My wife taught me. And an old man with a hot pot cannot climb four floors alone."

Nehir looked up at him. "So I am going to come and carry it."

"At a normal time," Yusuf said. "Six in the evening. With the lights on."

The girl thought about it. Then she put out her hand, and Yusuf took it, and they agreed like two people at a market.

He went home and slept until eleven, and he did not dream about anything.

On Friday he cooked rice with chicken and lemon. At six o'clock somebody knocked. It was Nehir. Behind her on the stairs stood her mother in her work clothes. She held the wall and she was not sure what to say.

"Come in," Yusuf said. "The hall light is broken. I am going to fix it on Sunday."
