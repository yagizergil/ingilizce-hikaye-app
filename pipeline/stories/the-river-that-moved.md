# The Six O'Clock Bus

The bus left the village square at six in the morning, and it did not reach the school gate until twenty past eight. Emre had counted them all: eleven mornings in a row, eleven late marks in Mr. Kaya's black book. From the kitchen window at home he could see the yellow wall of that school on the other side of the river. It was six hundred metres away. The bus drove forty kilometres to get there, because the closed bridge was the only bridge in the whole valley.

The bridge had been shut since February. Men in orange coats arrived one Monday, cut two square holes in the road, put a metal fence at each end and then drove away. They told the village that the work would take six weeks. Nobody had seen them since.

"You are a good student," Mr. Kaya said on Tuesday, when the other students had gone out. "But the rules are the same for everybody. Twenty missed days and you lose the year." Emre already knew the number in the black book, because he had read it upside down from the desk. It was twelve, and it was only the middle of March.

That afternoon he did not go straight home. He walked down the muddy path to the water and stood there with his bag on his shoulder. The river was wide and slow, green under the low cloud, and the far bank looked close enough to shout across. Behind his house, under the old fig tree, his grandfather's boat still lay upside down beneath a grey plastic sheet.

Grandfather Yusuf had died two winters before, and nobody had touched the boat after that. Emre pulled the sheet off and turned the boat over with his shoulder. The blue paint had gone pale, and there was one crack near the front, about the length of his hand. The wood was dry and hard everywhere else. He pressed his thumb into it and felt nothing soft.

"Leave that thing where it is," his mother said from the door. She was drying her hands on a cloth, and she did not come any closer.

"The bus takes two hours and twenty minutes."

"Then take the bus."

On Saturday he worked all day. He washed the boat out, pushed strips of old cloth deep into the crack, and painted the whole bottom with thick black paint from a tin in the shed. Then he found the two long wooden oars, the ones his grandfather had cut himself, and fitted them into their metal rings. The paint needed two days to dry, so he sat on the bank and waited, and he watched how the water moved.

He learned things in those two days. The river ran faster in the middle than at the sides. Near the big bend below the village, the water pushed everything towards the near bank, so a branch that fell in at the top always came back to the stones down there. Emre did not think much about it at the time, but he remembered it.

Sunday morning was quiet and grey. He pushed the boat into the shallow water, stepped in, and pulled. The first three minutes were terrible, because he could not keep the front straight and the current turned him twice. Then he found the rhythm, and the far bank came towards him faster than he expected. He landed on the sand eleven minutes after he had started, and his arms were shaking, and he laughed out loud at the empty field.

On Monday he crossed at a quarter past six and walked up the lane to school with dry shoes. He was the first student through the gate. When Mr. Kaya came in at eight and saw him already sitting there with his books open, the teacher stopped and looked at him for a long moment, but he wrote nothing in the black book.

Coming home that evening, Emre found Bekir, who ran the tea shop, standing on the far bank with a heavy box of tea glasses at his feet.

"Boy," Bekir said. "Is that Yusuf's boat?"

"It is."

"Then take me over, and take the box too. I have been waiting here since three."

Emre rowed him across. On the sand, Bekir pushed two coins into his hand and closed his fingers around them before he could argue.

# Two Lira and a Tin Box

By the end of the first week, six people were waiting under the fig tree at half past five. By the end of the second week there were fourteen, and Emre had to make three crossings before he could go to school himself.

He set a price, because otherwise every crossing turned into an argument. Two lira for adults, nothing for children, nothing for anyone going to the clinic. He wrote the names in the back of an old exercise book, and he kept the money in a tea tin with a picture of a mountain on the lid. Each night he counted it at the kitchen table while his little sister Elif watched with her chin on her hands.

The money had a purpose. In Yenice there was a weekend course for the big university exam, and the fee for the rest of the year was six hundred lira, and his mother had told him in January that it was simply not possible. Emre had not argued then. Now the tin was filling, and he had stopped thinking about the bus at all.

Not everybody was pleased. Rıfat, who drove the village bus, watched from the square with his arms folded while eleven of his passengers climbed into a wooden boat. On the ninth morning he came down to the water.

"Do you have a licence for that?" he said.

"It's my grandfather's boat."

"Your grandfather is dead, and I have a family." Rıfat spat into the mud. "You are taking the bread off my table, and you are too young to understand what you are doing. You don't know this river. Nobody knows this river in April."

Emre pushed off without answering. He was sure the man was only angry about money, and that made it easy to forget the second half of what he had said.

At the end of March the village head called everybody to the square and read out a letter from the town. The company that had opened the bridge had left the job. The money for the repairs was finished, and a new company would not begin before the autumn. The bridge would stay closed until the following spring, perhaps longer.

People shouted for a while, the way people do when there is nobody to shout at. Then an old woman at the back said loudly that God had at least sent them Yusuf's boat, and half the square turned round and looked at Emre. He stood there with his hands in his pockets and did not know where to put his face.

After that he started at twenty past five. If he began early enough, he could carry everyone across and still be in class before eight. Some mornings the queue was too long, and he arrived halfway through the first lesson with wet sleeves, and Mr. Kaya wrote in the book without a word.

One Thursday Emre fell asleep in the maths lesson with his head on his arm. The teacher did not shout. He waited until the room was empty and then sat down on the desk in front of him.

"You cross a river to reach my class," Mr. Kaya said, "and then you sleep in it. Tell me how that helps you."

"I need the money, sir."

"You need the year. The money will still exist in June."

That evening his mother was sitting on the step outside, shelling beans into a bowl. She watched him tie the rope round the fig tree in the last of the light. Emre had made forty-two lira that day, which was more than his uncle earned in the fields.

"Your grandfather rowed people over too," she said. "For years, when your father was small."

"You never told me that."

"There is a lot I have never told you." She dropped a bean into the bowl. "He stopped every year in March. From March until the middle of May that boat stayed under the tree, and he would not put it in the water for anybody. Do you know why?"

"His back," Emre said. He was thinking about the tin, and about a school desk in Yenice with his name on the list.

His mother looked at the river for a long time and then carried the bowl inside.

# The Colour of the Water

It rained in the mountains for three days, and the village hardly saw a drop of it.

On the first day Emre noticed only that the water was louder at night. On the second day the colour changed. The green went out of the river and brown came in, thick brown like tea with too much milk, and the surface carried small branches down past the village at walking speed. On the third morning the water had climbed the bank and covered the flat stones where he always pulled the boat up.

He rowed anyway. Ten people were waiting and the queue was quiet, and quiet queues are the worst kind. Halfway across, the current took the front of the boat and swung it round, and he had to pull twice as hard on one side just to hold the line. He landed sixty metres below the usual place, in reeds, and his hands were shaking when he tied up.

"It's fine," he told them. "The boat is heavy. It only feels strange."

That evening his mother did not start with the beans or the tea. She put a photograph on the kitchen table, a small square one with a white border, and turned it towards him. Two young men stood in front of the fig tree, and the one on the left was his grandfather with black hair.

"Who is the other one?"

"Halil. My father's younger brother." She sat down. "He was nineteen. In April, in a year like this one, a man from Yenice offered him good money to cross with two sacks of seed. The water was brown, and Halil was strong, and he thought that strong was enough." She turned the photograph face down. "They found him four days later, near the Yenice bridge. After that my father never put a boat in the water before the middle of May. Not for the doctor, not for money, not for anybody. People called him hard. He let them."

Emre sat with his hands flat on the table. He thought about the empty months under the fig tree, and about how easily he had believed the story of an old man's bad back, because that story had cost him nothing.

He did not sleep much. At five he went down with the lamp, took the long rope from the shed and tied the boat to the fig tree with three knots. Then he walked to the water and waited for the queue.

They came out of the dark in ones and twos, and he told them the same sentence eleven times: the boat is not going today. Some of them nodded and turned back. Bekir swore softly and went to open his shop. But Hasan, who had four boxes of cheese for the Friday market and a buyer waiting since Tuesday, would not accept it.

"Twenty lira," he said.

"It isn't about money."

"Fifty, then. Boy, look at the water. It's flat."

"It's flat because it's deep and it's fast," Emre said. "Look at that branch."

Hasan looked at the branch, and Hasan did not care about the branch. While Emre was walking two women back up the path with the lamp, he heard the knots being pulled loose behind him, and he heard the boxes go down into the boat one after another.

He got back to the bank as the boat left it. Hasan was already six metres out and pulling well, and he even lifted one hand.

Then the middle of the river took him. The front swung downstream, an oar came out of its ring, and Hasan grabbed at it and lost his balance. The boat turned side-on to the current and a wall of brown water came in over the low edge. The boxes went first. Then the boat rolled, and there was only a man's dark head and one white hand.

Emre did not run to the water. He ran along the top of the bank with the rope over his shoulder, past the last house, down to the bend where the branches always came back to the stones. He knew that piece of river better than he knew the road to school. He tied one end round a stone post, wrapped the rest round his arm, and stood in the water up to his knees.

Hasan came down fast, low in the water, striking out with no strength left in his arms. Emre threw. The first throw went behind him. The second landed across his chest, and Hasan's hands closed on it and stayed closed, and the rope went hard and hot through Emre's palms until the stone post took the weight.

Bekir and two other men arrived and pulled. They got Hasan out onto the stones, where he lay on his side, coughing river water into the mud, saying nothing at all for a long time.

They found the boat that afternoon, three kilometres downstream, broken across the middle against a fallen tree.

# What the Tin Was For

Nobody in the village said an unkind word to Emre about the boat, and that was somehow worse. For two days people looked at him with a kind of careful respect, as if he had grown older overnight, and he did not feel older. He felt like someone who had nearly killed a man for two lira a head.

Then Elif reminded him of the date, and he remembered the exam.

The year-end examinations began on Saturday at nine o'clock, in Yenice, because their school sent all its students there. Rıfat's bus left the square at seven and reached Yenice at twenty past nine. It had been at six all winter, but Rıfat had moved it later when his passengers went to the river, and he had not moved it back.

Emre found him in the square on Thursday evening, cleaning the windscreen with a wet cloth.

"I need the bus at five," Emre said.

"Then buy a bus."

"There are nine of us with exams on Saturday, and three more on Monday. If we come at twenty past nine, they don't let us into the room."

Rıfat kept cleaning the glass. "An early run costs me petrol. It costs me the man who helps me load in the morning. I lost six weeks of work to a boy in a wooden boat, and now the boy is standing here telling me my timetable."

Emre put the tea tin on the seat of the bus.

There were six hundred and forty lira inside, in coins and folded notes, the whole river, every crossing since Bekir's box of tea glasses. Rıfat stopped and looked at it, and then looked at Emre's hands, which still had marks across both palms from the rope.

"What is this?"

"The early run. Until the exams finish."

"This is your course money. Half the village knows that."

"The course is in September," Emre said. "The exam is on Saturday."

Rıfat took the tin, tipped it out on the seat and counted it slowly with one finger, the way he counted change. Then he pushed most of it back into the tin and put two hundred lira into his shirt pocket.

"Petrol costs what petrol costs," he said. "I am not taking a boy's whole year for a bit of petrol. Be in the square at five, and if anyone is late I leave without them."

On Saturday morning the bus was full before it left. Bekir came, though he had no exam, and sat at the front with a bag of bread for the students. Hasan came too and sat behind Emre, and he said nothing for thirty kilometres. Then, somewhere near the Yenice bridge, he leaned forward and put a hand on Emre's shoulder.

"Thank you," he said, and sat back.

They reached the school at half past six and waited two and a half hours in the yard in the cold, and every one of them was inside the room when the papers came round. Emre finished the last question with ten minutes to spare and read it all again, and outside the window he could see the mountains where the rain came from.

The bridge opened the following April, fourteen months later, with a new grey surface and a new fence. Emre crossed it on the first morning with his bag on his shoulder, on foot, in nine minutes.

The rope was still tied round the fig tree at home. He had never taken it down. He had also kept one piece of the boat, a short blue board from the side, and it stood on the shelf in the kitchen behind his mother's photographs, where anybody could see it and hardly anybody asked about it.
