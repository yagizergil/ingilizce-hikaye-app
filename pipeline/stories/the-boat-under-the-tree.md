# Two Brothers and One Hole

Mert found the hole first, and that was the start of all the trouble.

He was under the tree at the end of the garden, on his back, half inside the old boat. The wood above his face was dry and grey. Small pieces of it fell into his hair. He pushed his finger up into a dark line near the front and his finger went through into the sun.

"Kaan," he called. "Come here."

His brother did not come. Kaan was by the wall with the radio on, cutting the grass. He was seventeen. This year he did everything slowly, and slow was his answer to everything.

"Kaan!"

"What?"

"There is a hole."

Kaan turned the radio off. He walked down the garden and looked into the boat. Then he looked at his brother's finger, still in the hole, moving like a small animal.

"That is not a hole," he said. "That is the end of the boat."

The boat was four metres long and it was blue once. Their grandfather built it in 1974 with his own hands and a friend from the factory. For thirty years it sat on the lake every summer. Then their grandfather's legs went bad. The boat came home on a truck. It stayed under the plum tree for eleven years, with old flower pots inside it.

Two weeks ago their grandfather moved into a small flat near the hospital. Their mother helped him. She came back that night with red eyes and she did not want to talk.

Yesterday she talked.

"Your grandfather is eighty next Saturday," she said. "I asked him about a party. Do you know what he said? He said he wants to sit in his boat on the water. One hour. That is all."

"On the lake?" Mert said.

"On the lake."

Kaan put his fork down. "The boat is dead, Mum."

"Then tell him that yourself," their mother said, and she took her plate to the kitchen.

Nobody told him anything. Instead, on Sunday morning, both boys were in the garden at the same time, and that was the problem.

"I am going to fix it," Mert said now. "There is glue in the shed. And Uncle Sinan has wood."

"You are fourteen. You cannot fix a boat."

"You cannot fix a boat either."

"I can," Kaan said. "I helped Dad with the roof."

"You carried the ladder."

Kaan pushed him. Not hard, but Mert fell back against the tree and hit his elbow, and after that the morning went badly.

They worked for three days, but not together. That was the strange part. Kaan came out early and worked on the left side. Mert came out after school and worked on the right side. They did not speak about it. It became a rule.

Kaan bought two boxes of white glue with his own money and put glue into every line in the wood on his side.

Mert read about boats on his phone for four hours. Then he took the glue off Kaan's side with a knife, because the internet said the wood must be dry and clean first.

On Wednesday Kaan came out and saw his three days of work in a pile on the grass.

"You took it off," he said. His voice was very quiet, which was worse than shouting.

"It was wrong."

"It was mine."

"It was going to fall off in the water."

Kaan picked up Mert's box of tools and turned it over on the grass. Then he walked back into the house.

Their mother watched from the kitchen window and said nothing that night. But on Thursday morning she left a note on the table.

It said: Saturday is in two days. He is eighty once.

Mert read it twice. Then he took it up to his brother's room and put it on the bed, and he waited by the door.

Kaan read it. He put it down.

"The hole is too big for glue," he said at last. "We need a piece of wood over it. And the wood must be thin. And we do not have thin wood, and the shop is closed until Monday."

Mert looked at the floor for a long time.

"The kitchen table in the shed," he said. "The old one. The top is thin."

"Mum's mother's table?"

"Nobody uses it. It is full of paint pots."

Kaan stood up and took his coat off the door.

"Then we must ask her," he said. "Not you. Both of us."

# The Table, the Glue and the Rain

Their mother did not say yes at once.

She stood in the shed with a light in her hand and looked at the old kitchen table. Her mother ate at that table. Then it came here, and now old paint pots and a broken lamp stood on it.

"Cut the top," she said finally. "Not the legs. I want the legs."

"Why the legs?" Mert asked.

"Because I want the legs," she said, and she went back inside.

They carried the table top out into the garden. It was heavy and they hit the door twice. Kaan held one end and Mert held the other. Nobody let go. It was the first thing they did together in eleven weeks.

Then it started to rain.

They pulled a big plastic sheet over the boat and worked under it. The air went hot and wet and it smelled of glue and old wood. Rain hit the plastic above their heads all afternoon, like small stones.

Kaan cut the wood. He was slow but he was straight, and Mert watched his hands and said nothing about it.

"Hold this," Kaan said.

"I am holding it."

"Hold it better."

They put the new piece over the hole. It did not fit. They took it out and cut it again. It did not fit. They took it out again. At six o'clock Mert threw the knife on the grass and sat down in the wet.

"It is Thursday," he said. "It is going to rain tomorrow too."

Kaan sat down next to him. Water ran off the plastic behind his back.

"Do you remember the fish?" he said.

"What fish?"

"You do not remember. You were five." Kaan looked at the boat. "Grandad took us out early, and you were asleep in the front, and a fish came out of the water next to your head. Very big. And you woke up and you cried for an hour."

Mert did not remember the fish. But he suddenly remembered the smell of the lake at six in the morning. He remembered his grandfather's brown hands on the oars. He remembered the sound of the water under the wood.

"He is not going to be able to get in," Mert said. "He cannot walk to the shop."

"Uncle Sinan can carry him. Uncle Sinan is like a wall."

"And the truck? The boat is not going to walk to the lake."

Kaan took out his phone. "Uncle Sinan has a truck too."

They called him at half past six. He listened for a long time. Then he said one word, "Saturday," and put the phone down, and they did not know if that meant yes or no.

Friday after school they worked until dark. The rain stopped in the morning and the wood dried. This time the new piece went in. Kaan put glue all around it and Mert pushed it down and held it with both hands for twenty minutes, until his arms shook.

Then they painted it. There was one old can of blue paint in the shed, from their grandfather's time. It was thick and it smelled terrible, but the colour was right. Mert painted the front. Kaan painted the back. They met in the middle at ten o'clock at night with the garden light on.

"It is not the same blue," Mert said.

"It is the same blue. It is only older."

They stood back. In the dark, with one small light, the boat looked almost new.

"There is one problem," Kaan said.

"What?"

"We do not know if it holds water."

Mert looked at him. "So we test it."

"Where? It is eleven o'clock."

"The garden."

They took the water pipe and filled the boat with cold water, right there under the plum tree, at eleven o'clock at night. Their mother came to the window in her night clothes and watched them and did not say a word.

The water came up. They waited. Mert put the light close to the new wood and watched the line where the two pieces met.

One drop came out. Then another. Then a thin line of water ran down onto the grass.

Kaan sat down on the ground and put his hands on his head.

# The Water on Saturday

Nobody went to bed.

At one o'clock in the morning their mother came out with three cups of tea and a small tin.

"Your grandfather used this," she said. "Every year. It is black stuff for boats. It goes in the lines."

Kaan looked at the tin. "You had this in the house?"

"It was in the box from his flat," she said. "I did not know what it was until tonight."

The black stuff was terrible. It stuck to everything: hands, hair, the tea cups. But it went into the line between the old wood and the new wood, and it stayed there, and it went hard.

They tested it again at half past three. The garden was cold and full of stars. They filled the boat with the pipe and then they sat down and watched the new wood with the light.

Nothing came out.

They waited twenty minutes. Still nothing. Mert put his hand under the boat and it came back dry.

Kaan lay back on the wet grass and laughed, one time, like a cough. Then he was quiet.

Uncle Sinan came at eight in the morning with his truck and three men from the factory. They lifted the boat over the garden wall because it did not go through the gate. It took forty minutes and a lot of shouting. Their mother made bread and cheese for everybody.

At eleven o'clock they were at the lake.

Their grandfather came in Uncle Sinan's car. He was thinner than in the summer, and his stick was new, and somebody put a hat on his head that was too big. He looked at the water. Then he looked at the boat on the grass by the water, and he stopped walking.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Who did that?" he said.

"Both of us," Kaan said.

The old man walked slowly to the front of the boat. He put his hand on the new piece of wood. He moved his fingers over it, up and down, like a man reading.

"The wood is not right," he said.

"It is a table," Mert said.

"Whose table?"

"Grandma's."

Their grandfather laughed. He laughed until he had to hold Uncle Sinan's arm.

"She is going to be angry with me for a hundred years," he said.

They put the boat in the water. Uncle Sinan and two men carried the old man in, and he sat down in the middle with his stick across his knees. Then Kaan got in the back and Mert got in the front, and Uncle Sinan pushed them out.

The water was flat and grey and very quiet. Kaan took the oars. He was bad at it for the first minute and the boat turned in a circle. Their grandfather said nothing for a while. Then he said, "Small movements. Do not fight the water." After that it went better.

They went out to the middle of the lake. From there the town was small and the trees were a long dark line.

Mert watched the new wood at the front. It stayed dry. He watched it for a long time, because he did not want to look at his grandfather's face.

"Boys," the old man said.

"Yes?"

"Turn the boat. I want to look at the mountain."

They turned the boat. Nobody talked for a while. A bird stood on a stone near the water and then went up into the sky.

"Your uncle and I built this in the summer of 1974," their grandfather said. "We did not talk for three weeks in the middle of it. He wanted the seats in the wrong place."

Kaan and Mert looked at each other.

"How did you finish it?" Mert asked.

"His wife told us to stop being stupid," the old man said. "So we stopped."

They stayed on the water for one hour and ten minutes. Then they came back and the men lifted him out. He sat in a chair on the grass and drank his tea. He told the story of the boat to everybody, twice.

On the way home Mert fell asleep in the truck with his head on the window. Kaan was awake. He watched the boat behind them, moving a little on every corner, blue and old and dry inside.

Their mother asked one question that night, at the table.

"Are you going to keep it under the tree?"

"No," Kaan said. "The tree is bad for it. Uncle Sinan has a place at the lake. We can put it there."

"We," their mother said.

"We," Mert said, without opening his eyes.
