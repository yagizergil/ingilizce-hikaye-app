# Platform Nine

At ten o'clock every night, Nora Vale buys a coffee at Leo's café. Then she walks across the street to the station.

The night train leaves from platform nine at 22:40. It goes north, through small towns and dark forests, up into the mountains. In the morning it arrives in a town called Hollen. Then, in the evening, it comes back.

Nora is the conductor. She checks the tickets, helps people with their bags and answers questions. She also carries a small blue notebook in her pocket. In the notebook she writes little things about her passengers. A red umbrella. A sad song. A woman who always sits facing the back of the train.

"Ready?" asks Bruno. He is the driver. He is sixty years old and he loves old songs.

"Ready," says Nora.

The doors close. The train moves slowly out of the city lights.

At eleven o'clock, Nora starts to check the tickets. Most passengers are tired. They show her their phones, and she scans the codes. A student is asleep on her books. A family shares a bag of bread and cheese.

In the last carriage, an old man sits alone by the window. He wears a grey coat and a hat. On the table in front of him there is a small paper ticket. Nobody uses paper tickets now.

"Good evening," says Nora. "Can I see your ticket, please?"

The old man gives it to her with careful hands.

Nora looks at the ticket. The paper is yellow and soft. The name of the station on it is Brennmoor.

Nora knows every station on this line. There is no station called Brennmoor.

# A Closed Station

"Sir," Nora says gently, "this ticket is very old."

"Yes," says the man. "I bought it a long time ago. My name is Emil."

"Emil, I am sorry, but the train does not stop at Brennmoor."

"I know the train stops there," he says. "Somebody is waiting for me. I promised."

Nora sits down opposite him. She does not want to make him feel stupid. She takes out her notebook and opens a map of the line on her phone.

"Tell me about Brennmoor," she says.

Emil looks out of the dark window. "It is a small station after the long bridge, before the tunnel. There is a little house next to it with a green door. Her name is Marta. Forty years ago I left on this train. I told her: one day I will come back. Today is the day."

Nora thinks about the line. After the long bridge, before the tunnel, the train passes some old buildings in the forest. She sees them every night in the dark, but she never thinks about them.

She goes to the front of the train and asks Bruno.

"Brennmoor?" Bruno says. He turns the music down. "Yes, I remember it. It closed about thirty years ago. Nobody lives there now. Why?"

Nora tells him about Emil.

Bruno is quiet for a moment. "We cannot stop there, Nora. It is not on the timetable."

"I know," she says.

# The Green Door

Nora goes back to the last carriage. Emil is still looking out of the window. His hands are on his old ticket.

She sits down again. "Emil, what do you want to say to Marta?"

"That I am sorry," he says. "That I was young and afraid. That I never forgot her."

Nora thinks. Then she has an idea. She takes a page out of her blue notebook and gives it to him with her pen.

"Write it here," she says. "I promise you something too."

Emil writes for a long time. His letters are small and careful. When he finishes, he folds the paper.

At three o'clock in the morning, the train goes over the long bridge. Then it slows down. Bruno is driving very slowly, more slowly than usual.

"Look," says Nora.

Outside the window, in the light of the train, they see an old platform with grass on it. A small sign says BRENNMOOR. Next to it, there is a little house. The windows are dark, but the door is still green.

Emil puts his hand on the glass. He does not speak.

Then the station is gone, and the train goes into the tunnel.

In the morning, at Hollen, Nora asks at the small station office about Brennmoor. A woman there knows the name.

"Marta Kell? She moved to Hollen when the station closed. She lives on Mill Street. She is old now, but she still walks to the bakery every morning."

Nora looks at the folded paper in her hand. Then she looks at Emil, who is waiting on the platform with his small bag.

"Emil," she says, "I think you are one station late. That is all."

That night, back in Port Lumen, Nora writes in her blue notebook: _Paper ticket. Green door. Forty years._ Then she turns the page and stops.

On the next page, in old blue ink, there is something she did not write. It is a name. It is her father's name.
