# A Phone in a Shoebox

Kai Doren's shop in the Old Market is so small that two customers cannot stand in it at the same time. There is a counter, a shelf of old radios that he will never sell, and a desk under a bright lamp where he spends most of his day with tiny screwdrivers and broken screens.

On Tuesday afternoon, the bell above the door rang, and a boy of about fifteen came in. He was holding a shoebox with both hands, as if there was something alive inside it.

"Are you the repair guy?" he asked.

"I am the repair guy," said Kai. "What do you have?"

The boy put the box on the counter and opened it. Inside, wrapped in a kitchen towel, there was an old phone with a cracked back and a screen that was black and dead.

"It was my grandmother's," the boy said. "She died in March. My name is Danny, by the way."

"I am sorry, Danny."

Danny nodded quickly, the way people nod when they do not want to talk about something. "The phone stopped working last year. She never fixed it, but she never threw it away either. My mother says there is a voice note on it. My grandmother recorded herself singing a song for my mother's birthday, a long time ago. My mother wants to hear it again. She talks about it every day."

Kai picked up the phone and turned it slowly in his hands. It was a model that was popular about ten years ago. The battery was probably finished, and the water marks near the charging port told him that it had fallen somewhere wet at least once.

"I can try," he said. "I cannot promise anything. Sometimes the memory survives, sometimes it does not."

"How much will it cost?"

Kai looked at the boy's shoes, which were too big for him, and at the coins he was already taking out of his pocket.

"We can talk about money if I find the song," he said.

# Inside the Machine

That evening, after Kai closed the shop, he stayed at his desk. His sister Lina was working a night shift at the hospital, so there was no reason to go home early, and he liked working on difficult repairs when the market outside was quiet.

He opened the phone carefully. The battery was swollen, and two small parts on the board were green with corrosion. He cleaned the board with alcohol and a soft brush, replaced the battery with one from his box of spare parts, and connected the phone to his computer.

For a long time, nothing happened. Then the screen gave a weak white light, and a small logo appeared.

Kai smiled. "Hello," he said to the phone.

The phone was locked, but Danny had written the code on a piece of paper: his grandmother's birthday. Kai typed it in. The home screen opened, full of colourful icons and a photo of a garden with red flowers.

He did not look at the messages or the photos. That was his rule. He opened only the voice recorder application.

There were fourteen recordings. Most of them were short: a shopping list, a doctor's phone number, the name of a medicine. Near the top, there was one called _For Ines – Happy Birthday_. Ines, Kai guessed, was Danny's mother.

He pressed play.

An old woman's voice came out of the small speaker, a little out of tune but warm and full of life. She laughed at the beginning, said "Oh, is it recording?" and then started to sing. It was a simple song, the kind of song people sing at kitchen tables, and at the end she said, "I love you, my girl. Happy birthday."

Kai sat back in his chair. He felt the satisfaction he always felt when something broken started to work again. He saved a copy of the file on his computer.

Then he noticed the last recording in the list. It was called _For Ines – only after_.

# The Second Message

Kai did not press play immediately.

He sat with his hand above the mouse for a long minute. The title was clear. _Only after._ After what? He thought he knew. It was a message that the grandmother wanted her daughter to hear after she died.

It was not his message. It was not even Danny's message. But Danny had asked him to find one song, and he had found it. Nobody had asked him about anything else.

He thought about saving the file without listening. He could give the phone back, say "the song is there," and let the family find the second recording by themselves. But the phone was old and weak. The memory was damaged. If the phone died again, the message would probably die with it, and nobody would ever know that it existed.

"You are getting too involved again," he said to himself, because Lina said it to him often.

He saved a copy of the second recording without playing it. Then he closed the computer and went home.

The next morning, Mr Okafor, the old man who sold radios in the shop next door, came in with two cups of tea, as he did most mornings.

"You look like a man who did not sleep," said Mr Okafor.

Kai told him the story. Mr Okafor listened, drinking his tea slowly.

"So what is the question?" he asked at the end.

"Should I listen to it? Maybe it is something bad. Maybe the grandmother says something that will hurt them. Maybe I should warn the boy first."

Mr Okafor shook his head. "Kai, you fix machines. You do not fix families. The message is not yours to open, and it is not yours to judge."

"But if I say nothing, they may never find it."

"Then do not say nothing," said Mr Okafor. "Say the truth, and let them choose."

# Danny's Choice

Danny came back on Friday after school. He looked nervous.

"Did it work?"

Kai turned the phone on. The screen lit up with the photo of the garden, and Danny made a small sound, half a laugh and half something else.

"The song is there," Kai said. "I also saved a copy on this memory stick, because the phone is old and may stop again."

He played the birthday song. Danny listened with his eyes closed. When his grandmother said "I love you, my girl," he wiped his face with the back of his hand and pretended that he had something in his eye.

"Thank you," he said. "My mother is going to cry for a week."

"There is one more thing," Kai said. He turned the phone towards the boy and showed him the list. "There is a second recording for your mother. Look at the name."

Danny read it. _For Ines – only after._

"What is it?" he asked quietly.

"I do not know. I did not listen. I think it is not for me, and I think it is maybe not for you either. It is for your mother. But I did not want you to leave without knowing it was there."

Danny looked at the phone for a long time. Kai waited. Outside, someone in the market was shouting the price of oranges.

"She was very sick at the end," Danny said finally. "She could not talk much. My mother always says she did not have time to say goodbye properly." He took a deep breath. "I think maybe she did."

He put the phone and the memory stick back in the shoebox, very carefully, and wrapped them in the kitchen towel.

"How much do I owe you?"

"Bring me the shoebox back when you do not need it," Kai said. "I always need boxes."

Danny smiled for the first time. At the door, he stopped.

"Why did you not just listen to it? Nobody would know."

Kai thought about it. "I would know," he said.

# A Different Kind of Repair

A week later, a woman with short grey hair and Danny's eyes came into the shop with a cake in a paper bag. She did not say much. She put the cake on the counter, held Kai's hand for a moment with both of hers, and said, "She did say goodbye. Thank you for letting me hear it first."

When she left, Mr Okafor came in from next door, looked at the cake, and raised his eyebrows.

"You see?" he said. "You did not fix the family. You gave them back their own key."

Kai cut the cake into two pieces and gave the bigger one to Mr Okafor.

That afternoon, the bell rang again. A woman in a long camel coat came in, out of breath. She put a phone on the counter. The screen was completely smashed, as if someone had hit it with a hammer.

"I am Mara Lind, from the Herald," she said. "I need to know what is on this phone. And I need you not to tell anyone that I brought it here."

Kai looked at the phone. Then he looked at her.

"Is it yours?" he asked.

Mara Lind did not answer.
