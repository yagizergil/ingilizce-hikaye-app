# The Box with the Hole

Deniz put the box down on the station floor. Her arms hurt. The box moved again.

"Be quiet," she said. "Please."

A black nose pushed out of the hole in the side. It was wet and cold. She touched it with one finger.

Her mother looked at the big clock over the doors. "Twelve minutes," she said. "Do not open that box on the train. Not once."

"He does not like the box."

"I know. But the train man can put you both off at any station. Dogs travel in a box. That is the rule."

Deniz sat down next to the box and put her hand on the top. Inside, the dog stopped moving.

Kara was old. He lived with her grandfather for eleven years, in a house with a garden and a hill behind it. Her grandfather died in March. Now Kara lived with Deniz and her mother in two small rooms on the fourth floor. He did not sleep. Every night, at about two o'clock, he stood by the door and cried.

The neighbours came up twice. The second time, the man from the flat below did not smile.

"Your uncle has land," her mother said again. "Fields. Sheep. Kara is going to run all day there."

"You said that yesterday."

"It is true today too."

The station was full of people with bags. A woman sold tea from a small cart. Two boys ran past and one of them hit the box with his foot.

"Hey!" Deniz said.

The boy did not stop. Inside the box, Kara made a low sound.

Her mother gave her a paper bag. "Bread and cheese. And water for him. Your uncle is going to wait at Bahçe station at seven in the morning. Green truck. You know his face."

"I know his face."

"Deniz." Her mother put her hands on her shoulders. "You are twelve. This is one night on one train. You can do it."

Deniz did not answer. She looked at her shoes.

The train came in slowly. It was long and grey and it smelled of hot metal. People moved. Everybody stood up at the same time.

Her mother lifted the box. Deniz carried the bags. They walked along the train and found her seat by the window.

"Put him under," her mother said. "Not in the passage. People walk there."

They pushed the box under the seat. Deniz put her grandfather's old brown hat on top of it. The hat still smelled of him — smoke, and rain, and the soap he used.

"You must not lose that hat," her mother said.

"I never lose it."

A man in a blue coat walked past and looked at the box. Deniz put her bag in front of it.

Her mother kissed her head. Then she went out onto the platform and stood by the window. She did not wave. She just stood, with her arms in front of her, and watched.

The train started. The station moved away. The city moved away too — the lights, the tall buildings, then long dark fields.

Deniz waited ten minutes. Then she looked down the carriage. Most people were reading or sleeping. She opened the small door of the box.

Kara put his head out. His face was grey around the eyes now. He looked at her, and he put his nose in her hand.

"Two stations," she said quietly. "Then I am going to give you water. Then you sleep."

An old woman across the passage watched them. She had a basket on her knees.

"Is he yours?" she asked.

"He was my grandfather's."

The woman agreed slowly. "Old dogs do not like new places," she said. "They like old smells."

"He is going to my uncle's farm."

"Ah." The woman opened her basket and took out a piece of bread. "Then he is a lucky dog." She held the bread out. "Can he eat this?"

Kara ate it in one second.

The train ran into the dark. Deniz put her head against the cold window. Under the seat, the old dog breathed slowly and did not cry.

For the first time in four months, Kara slept.

# Twenty Minutes at a Small Station

The train stopped at half past one in the morning.

Deniz woke up. The lights in the carriage were low and yellow. Outside there was almost nothing — a short platform, two lamps, a small white building, and black hills behind it.

Nobody moved. Then the man in the blue coat walked through the carriage.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "We must wait here. Another train is coming the other way. Twenty minutes."

Some people got up and went outside to smoke. The old woman with the basket was asleep.

Under the seat, Kara moved. He pushed his nose against the box door and made a low sound. Then he did it again, louder.

Deniz put her hand on the box. "Not now," she said.

The sound came again. A man three seats away lifted his head.

Deniz looked at the open door of the train. Cold air came in. Twenty minutes. The platform was empty and dark. Kara was in that box for five hours.

She made her decision fast, and later she thought about it every day.

She pulled the box out. She took her grandfather's hat and put it in her coat. Then she carried the box down the steps and onto the platform.

The air outside was very cold and very clean. It smelled of wet grass. Deniz walked away from the smokers, to the dark end of the platform, and put the box down on the stones.

"Two minutes," she said. "Then we go back."

She opened the door.

Kara came out slowly. His back legs were slow and hard. He stood on the stones and lifted his head. He pulled the air into his nose. The grass, the hills, the water somewhere in the dark. His whole body changed. He was not an old dog in a flat anymore.

Deniz smiled. "You see? It is good here. The farm is—"

A dog barked.

It came from behind the white building — a big, deep, angry sound. Then a second bark, closer.

Kara turned. For one moment he stood very still.

"Kara. No—"

He ran.

He was old but he was fast. He went off the end of the platform, down onto the stones by the tracks, and into the dark field.

Deniz ran after him. She jumped down. The ground was soft and full of holes and her foot went into water. She fell on her hands, got up, and ran again.

"Kara! Kara, come!"

The night ate her voice. She saw nothing — only grey grass, and further away, a line of trees.

Behind her, the train made a long, hard sound.

She stopped and turned. The lamps of the station were far away now. She was maybe two hundred metres out in the field. On the platform, the man in the blue coat was closing the doors, one by one.

Deniz stood in the wet grass with her heart hitting her body.

Her bag was on that train. Her bread and cheese were on that train. Her uncle was going to wait at Bahçe station at seven in the morning. He was going to stand by a green truck. Nobody was going to come out of the train door.

She looked at the train. She looked at the black field.

"Kara!" she shouted.

The train started to move. It went past her slowly, then faster, a long yellow line of windows. She saw the old woman's basket. She saw her own empty seat.

Then the last carriage went past, and the red light on the back of it got smaller and smaller.

And then it was dark, and quiet, and Deniz was alone in a field with wet feet.

# The Hat on the Grass

The station man found her ten minutes later. He came out of the white building with a lamp.

"Girl!" he shouted. "Come here! There is another train at four o'clock, but not on this line!"

Deniz walked back to the platform. Her shoes made water sounds.

The man was old and short and he was angry, but the anger was not really for her.

"You got off the train," he said. "Alone. At night."

"My dog ran away."

"A dog." He put his hand on his face. "Where are your people?"

"My uncle is at Bahçe. At seven."

"Bahçe." He said something quietly. "Come inside. There is a fire."

"No."

"What?"

"He is out there." Deniz pointed at the field. "He is old. He does not know this place. I must find him."

The man looked at her for a long moment. Then he went inside and came out with a second lamp.

"Take this," he said. "Do not go past the trees. There is a river past the trees."

They walked out into the field together, in two lines, and they called. The man's voice was big and rough. Deniz's voice went thin after a while.

The dog did not come.

At three o'clock the man stopped. "I must open the station," he said. "The four o'clock train comes on the other line."

"I am going to stay."

"You are going to freeze."

"I am going to stay."

He gave her his own coat. It was big and it smelled of smoke. Then he went back to the lamps.

Deniz sat down on a stone at the edge of the field. Her hands did not work well anymore. Her feet were wet and hard.

She thought about the flat on the fourth floor, and the two o'clock crying, and the man from the flat below.

Then she stopped calling.

She took her grandfather's hat out of her coat.

It was old brown wool with a broken line inside. It smelled of smoke and rain and soap. It smelled of a house with a garden and a hill behind it. It smelled of a man with big hands. Eleven years ago that man put food on the floor for a young black dog.

Deniz put the hat down on the grass in front of her.

Then she sat back and she did not move and she did not speak.

The sky went from black to a hard, cold grey. She saw the trees now. She saw the shape of the hills.

At about half past five, something moved by the trees.

It was low and it came slowly, along the ground, stopping and starting. Deniz did not call it. She kept her hands on her knees and her eyes on the hat.

Kara came out of the grass. His legs were full of mud and there was a cut on his ear. He walked past her without looking at her.

He put his nose into the brown hat and he stood there and he breathed.

Then he sat down beside it, and he put his weight against her leg, and he closed his eyes.

Deniz picked up the hat. She picked up the dog too. He was heavy and he smelled terrible and she did not care.

She carried him back across the field to the yellow lamps.

The station man was standing at the door with a telephone in his hand.

"I called Bahçe," he said. "Your uncle is coming. He is going to drive here. Two hours." He looked at the dog. "So. Is this the animal?"

"This is the animal."

The man opened the door with his foot. "Bring it inside. There is a fire, and there is soup, and you are both going to sit next to it."

At half past eight, a green truck stopped outside the station. Deniz's uncle was tall and he did not talk very much. He looked at his sister's daughter, asleep in a chair with a station man's coat over her. He looked at the old dog on the floor by the fire.

Then he picked up the box, took it outside, and put it in the back of the truck.

"He is not going to need this," he said.

Kara rode to the farm on the front seat, with his head out of the window and his ears flat in the wind.

That night, the farm was very quiet.

Deniz woke up once, at about two o'clock, and she listened.

There was nothing. Only the wind, and somewhere far away, sheep.

She went to the window. Down in the yard, under a big white moon, an old black dog was asleep. He lay in the middle of the grass with his nose in a brown wool hat.
