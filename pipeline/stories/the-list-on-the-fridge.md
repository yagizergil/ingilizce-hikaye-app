# The Pocket of the Grey Coat

The rain stopped a little after nine, and Emre took his wife's grey coat from the hook by the door, because his own coat was still wet from the morning.

He had not touched her coat for six weeks. It still smelled of her soap and of the cold air of the market.

His hand went into the right pocket, where Nihal always kept her keys, and found a folded piece of paper instead. He sat down on the low chair by the door and opened it carefully, as if the paper could break in his fingers.

It was a shopping list. Her handwriting leaned to the right, small and quick, the way she wrote when she was in a hurry.

She had written bread, then half a kilo of white cheese, then six eggs, tea, soap and a jar of olives. Under the olives there was one more line, in the same blue pen. Thread, dark blue — Sema, Thursday.

Emre read the last line three times. He knew the name of every woman in his wife's life: her sister Perihan, the neighbours on all four floors, the two friends from her old school, the woman who cut her hair. There was no Sema.

The telephone rang at eleven, and it was his son. Kerem called every Sunday from the city where he worked, and every Sunday he asked the same question.

"Come and stay with us for the winter," Kerem said. "The room is ready. Nobody should be alone in that flat."

"I am not alone. I have the neighbours, and I have my chair, and I have my own bed."

"What did you eat yesterday?"

"Rice and chicken," Emre said, and it was not true. He had eaten bread and had drunk four glasses of tea, and the fridge held one tomato and half a lemon.

After the call he stood in the kitchen for a long time. Nihal had done the shopping for forty-one years, and she had always written the list on Sunday night and gone to the market on Monday morning. The list in his hand was the last one she wrote. She had never used it, because on Sunday night her heart had stopped while she slept.

He put on the wet coat, took his old cloth bag, and went down the stairs with the paper in his hand.

The market was full of noise and water. Men shouted the price of apples, and the ground shone under the plastic roofs. Emre bought the bread first, then the cheese, then the eggs, and he counted them into the bag one by one while the woman waited.

At the last stand he bought tea and soap and a small jar of olives from Rıza, who had known Nihal for twenty years.

"Where is the thread?" Emre asked.

Rıza laughed. "Thread? Not here, Emre Abi. We sell food. You want a sewing shop for that."

"She wrote it on the list. Dark blue thread."

"Then she knew where to buy it." Rıza put the olives in the bag and pressed the top down. "Ask her sister. Women know these things."

Emre walked home slowly, with the bag pulling on his shoulder. He put the bread on the shelf and the cheese in the fridge, and then he stood the list against the sugar tin, where he could see it.

Six things were finished. One thing was not.

# Thursday

Perihan answered the phone on the fourth ring, and her voice was tired.

"Emre? Is something wrong?"

"Nothing is wrong. I want to ask you a small thing." He held the paper close to the window light. "Do you know a woman called Sema?"

There was a pause. "Sema? No. Who is she?"

"Nihal wrote her name on a shopping list. Thread, dark blue — Sema, Thursday."

"Maybe it is the name of a shop," Perihan said. "There are little shops like that near the mosque."

"On Thursdays she came to you," Emre said. "Every Thursday she took the bus at nine and came back at two. She always brought me something from your kitchen."

The pause was longer this time, and Emre began to feel cold in his hands.

"Emre," his wife's sister said slowly, "Nihal has not come to my house on a Thursday since last winter. We talked on the telephone. She said your back was bad and she did not want to leave you alone for so many hours."

He looked at the empty chair by the balcony door. The cloth on the arm still had the shape of her elbow in it.

"She brought me food from your kitchen," he said again. "Rice with beans. You know I cannot cook beans."

"I sent nothing," Perihan said. "Emre, sit down. Are you sitting?"

He said goodbye politely and put the phone down, and then he sat for half an hour without moving. A woman does not tell a lie every Thursday for a year with no reason. She takes the bus, and she comes home with cooked food and a tired face, and she says nothing.

The next morning he went back to the market before the crowds came. Rıza was cutting the tops from a box of green onions.

"Rıza. On Thursdays, did my wife come here?"

"Every Thursday, early." Rıza did not look up. "She bought two lemons and sometimes a bag of tomatoes. She was always in a hurry."

"And then? Which way did she go?"

Rıza pointed with his knife, down the wet street. "Left, always left. Towards the old station. She had that green cloth bag on her arm, the one with the handle she repaired herself."

"There is nothing at the old station," Emre said. "The buses stopped going from there ten years ago."

"There are still shops behind it," Rıza said. "Small ones. Half of them are closed now, but not all."

Emre walked to the corner and turned left. The road went down between grey buildings, past a barber, past a place that repaired washing machines, past three shops with metal shutters closed and locked. His back began to hurt at the second corner, and he stopped and put his hand on a wall.

He thought about his shoe shop, which had closed two years ago. For thirty-one years he had cut leather and pushed a needle through it, and his fingers still knew the movement. Then the rents rose and the customers stopped coming, and he had signed a paper at the bank for money they borrowed to keep the shop open one more year. That last year had eaten everything.

When the shop closed, Nihal had said, "Do not think about the bank. I sold the two gold bracelets, and I have my savings from the sewing. It is finished." And he had believed her, because for forty-one years she had never once said a thing that was not true.

He turned around and went home. It was Tuesday, and something in him wanted to wait.

On Thursday morning he put on the grey coat again, took the list, and took the bus at nine.

# The Shop Behind the Station

The shop was the fourth one down, and it had no proper sign. In the window there was a card with letters cut from a magazine, and it said SEMA — SEWING AND REPAIRS. Behind the card he could see a machine, a shelf of cloth, and a coat hanging on a nail.

A woman of about thirty-five opened the door before he knocked. She had short hair and a pin between her lips, and when she saw his face she took the pin out and held it in her hand.

"You are Nihal Abla's husband," she said.

"I am Emre."

"I know. She showed me your photograph. You were standing in your shop with a shoe in your hand." Sema stepped back from the door. "Come in. The kettle is on."

The room was small and warm, and it smelled of hot metal and cloth. There were trousers folded on one chair and a school shirt on another. On the table, under the lamp, there was a child's coat in dark blue, and the inside of it was open, with pins along the edge.

Emre sat down slowly. He put the shopping list on the table and turned it so that she could read the last line.

Sema looked at it, and her eyes filled, and she pressed the back of her hand against them and laughed at herself.

"She wrote it on a Sunday," she said. "She always wrote her list on Sunday night."

"What was she to you?"

"She worked here." Sema pointed at the second machine in the corner. "Thursday mornings, and Monday mornings when there was a lot. Eleven months. She mended trousers, she turned collars, she sewed buttons faster than anybody I have ever seen."

"For money," Emre said.

"For money, yes. I paid her every second week."

The kettle began to make its noise, and neither of them moved.

"She told me she went to her sister," Emre said. "She brought food home. She said her sister cooked it."

"I cooked it," Sema said. "She would not eat here, so I put it in a box and she carried it home in the green bag. She said you liked beans."

Emre put both hands flat on the table. The child's coat lay there between them, and one blue sleeve nearly touched his finger.

"Why?" he said. "Why did she hide this from me? Am I a man who does not let his wife work?"

"She said you would go back to work if you knew." Sema spoke carefully, the way a person speaks who has thought about the words before. "She said you would find some old friend with a shop and stand at a table twelve hours a day, and that your back and your hands could not do it any more. She said it would kill you, and then what use would the money be."

"So instead she carried it. Alone."

"She said it was not alone." Sema turned off the gas. "She said she was carrying half. She said you carried it for thirty-one years, and now it was her turn."

Emre stood up. His face was hot and his throat hurt, and the small room seemed to have no air in it.

"I have to go."

"Emre Bey—"

"I will come back," he said, and he was not sure that it was true.

He left the paper on the table by mistake, and he walked up the hill without stopping, past the closed shutters, past the barber, and he did not feel his back at all until he reached the corner.

# Dark Blue

He did not sleep that night. At three in the morning he got up, turned on the kitchen light, and took down his wife's sewing box from the top of the cupboard.

Under the buttons and the small scissors there was a thin notebook with a brown cover. He had seen it a hundred times and had never opened it, because it was hers.

Inside there were dates and numbers in her small quick handwriting. March, four hundred. April, four hundred. May, two hundred and fifty, and next to it the word _tooth_, because that was the month his tooth broke. Under each number there was another number, and the second number grew smaller month after month, the way a wall grows smaller when a man takes it down stone by stone.

On the last page there was a receipt from the bank, dated two weeks before she died. Under it she had written one word, and she had drawn a line under the word twice. _Finished._

Emre put his hand flat on the page and left it there for a long time. Then he closed the notebook, washed his face, and made tea, and he watched the sky get light over the balcony.

On Thursday he took the bus at nine. He went into the first sewing shop he found near the mosque, but it was closed, and a paper on the door said the owner would come back at eleven. He did not want to wait until eleven. He walked three more streets, found another shop, and bought two rolls of dark blue thread, because he was not sure how much a coat would need.

Sema opened the door with the pin in her mouth again. This time she did not look surprised.

"I made shoes for thirty-one years," Emre said, and he put the thread on the table next to the blue coat. "Leather is harder than cloth. My hands are slow now, but they are not stupid. Show me what she was doing."

Sema took the pins out of the edge one by one and explained. The coat belonged to a boy of eight, and his mother had brought it in October so that the inside could be repaired before the cold came. The mother had already asked about it twice.

Emre sat under the lamp, put on his glasses, and began to sew. The first ten minutes were bad, and he had to take out his work and start again. Then his fingers remembered the rhythm, and after that the room was quiet except for Sema's machine and the rain on the window.

At half past twelve the mother came, and the boy came with her. He put the coat on and pulled the sleeves down over his hands and turned around in the middle of the small room.

"Who mended it?" the boy asked.

Emre looked at the blue thread on the table, and at the empty chair in the corner where the second machine stood.

"Two people," he said.

He walked home with the green cloth bag on his arm, because Sema had found it on her shelf and had given it back to him. On the kitchen table he put down the bag, the box of food, and the shopping list she had brought out of her pocket and returned to him that morning.

He took the blue pen from the drawer. He drew a careful line through the last item, the way Nihal always did when a thing was done. Then he turned the paper over and wrote on the empty side, in his own large letters, which did not lean to the right at all.

Bread. Half a kilo of white cheese. Six eggs. Tea. Soap. Olives.

And under the olives, because it was six days until Thursday and he would need it: thread, dark blue — Sema, Thursday.
