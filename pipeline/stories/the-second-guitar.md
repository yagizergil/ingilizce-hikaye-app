# The Wrong Door

Deniz found the music room on her second day. She was looking for the toilets. She opened the wrong door and she heard a guitar.

A boy sat on a chair by the window. He played four notes and then he stopped. He played the same four notes again.

"That one is wrong," Deniz said.

The boy looked up. His hair was short and his shoes were very big. "What?"

"The third note. It is too high."

"Who are you?"

"Deniz. I am new here."

"I know you are new," the boy said. "Everybody knows. You came on Monday." He put his hand flat on the strings. The sound stopped. "I am Kerem. This is my room."

"The school gives you a room?"

"I am in the music group. We play at the show on Friday." He looked at the clock on the wall. "So this week, yes, it is my room."

Deniz walked in anyway. There was an old piano under the window with a broken leg. There were ten chairs and eight of them were empty. Somebody painted a big yellow sun on the back wall. The sun was old and the paint was falling off.

"Can I try?" Deniz asked. She looked at the guitar.

"No."

"One minute. Just one."

Kerem stood up. He put the guitar in a black bag and he closed the bag. "It is not my guitar. It is the school guitar. There is only one."

"I am not going to break it."

"Everybody says that."

He picked up the bag and walked past her. At the door he turned around.

"You play?" he asked.

"At my old school. Three years."

"Everybody says that too," Kerem said, and he went out.

Deniz stood alone in the music room. She looked at the yellow sun for a long time.

At home her mother was cooking. The kitchen was hot and the window was full of steam. Her little brother sat on the floor with his cars.

"How is the new school?" her mother asked.

"There is a music group," Deniz said. "They play on Friday, in front of the whole school."

Her mother did not turn around. "And?"

"And I want to play with them."

"Then talk to the teacher."

Deniz talked to the teacher on Wednesday. Her name was Mrs. Aylin and she taught history, but she was also the head of the music group. She listened. She wrote nothing down.

"The show is in nine days," Mrs. Aylin said. "The group started in September. They know all the songs."

"I learn fast."

"I believe you." The teacher pushed her glasses up. "But we have one guitar, Deniz. One. The school has no money for another one. Kerem plays it. He practises every lunch time."

"Then I can play something else."

"Can you sing?"

Deniz thought about it. "No," she said. "Not really."

Mrs. Aylin smiled a small, tired smile. "Come in January," she said. "We are going to make a new group after the winter holiday."

Deniz walked home the long way, past the market. The men were putting the boxes into their vans. One man was selling the last tomatoes very cheap. She counted the money in her pocket. Forty-two lira.

She did not want January. She wanted Friday.

At school the next morning she went to the music room early. The door was locked. She put her ear on the wood and she heard nothing.

Uncle Sami came down the corridor with a bucket. He was old and he cleaned the school every morning, and he carried all the keys on a big ring.

"That room opens at ten," he said.

"I know," Deniz said. "I am waiting."

He looked at her. Then he looked at the ring of keys in his hand.

"You are the new girl," he said. "From Izmir."

"Yes."

"There is another room," Uncle Sami said slowly. "Downstairs. We put the old things in it. Broken chairs, broken tables." He started to walk away. Then he stopped. "Broken other things too."

# The Room Under the Stairs

The room was cold and it smelled of wet paper.

Uncle Sami turned on the light. One lamp worked and one did not. Deniz saw chairs on top of chairs, a green board with no legs, and forty old maps in a pile.

"Ten minutes," Uncle Sami said. "Then I lock it."

Deniz moved a chair. Behind it there was a box, and behind the box there was a guitar.

It was brown and dusty. Two strings were missing. There was a long crack near the hole in the middle, but the crack was thin, like a hair.

She lifted it. It was lighter than the school guitar. She touched the four old strings and the sound came out flat and sad, but it came out.

"That thing is a hundred years old," Uncle Sami said from the door.

"Can I take it?"

"It is school property."

"It is school rubbish," Deniz said.

The old man laughed. He laughed for a long time and then he coughed.

"Take it," he said. "But do not tell Mrs. Aylin. Not before it works."

Deniz carried the guitar home under her coat. Her brother wanted to touch it and she said no, and then he cried, and then she let him touch it for two minutes.

That evening she sat on her bed and she looked at the crack. Her father came and stood in the door with a cup of tea.

"Where is that from?" he asked.

"The school."

He took the guitar and turned it over in his hands. He was a builder and his fingers were hard and grey.

"The wood is good," he said. "Somebody made this by hand. Look here." He showed her the line inside the hole. "It is not a machine guitar."

"It has no strings."

"Two strings. It has four." Her father gave it back. "Strings cost money."

"How much?"

"I do not know. Ask in the shop on Sunday."

The music shop was near the bus station. The man behind the desk was watching a football match on a small television.

"Six strings, a whole set," he said. "Ninety lira."

Deniz put her forty-two lira on the desk. "And two strings? Just two?"

"I do not sell them one by one."

"Please."

The man looked at her, then at the television, then at her again. "Which two?"

"The thin ones. The first and the second."

He went into the back and he came out with a small paper packet. "Thirty," he said. "And you do not tell anybody."

She paid and she ran to the bus.

She put the new strings on that night. Her hands did not know this guitar and it took a long time. One string broke and she nearly cried, but it was not broken — it was only loose. She turned the key again, slowly, and the note came up and up and then it was right.

The old guitar was not loud. But it was clear, and it was warm, and the crack made a small buzz on the low notes. She liked the buzz.

She played for two hours. Her brother came and sat on the floor and put his head on the bed and went to sleep there.

On Monday she took it to school in a bag. She did not go to the music room. She went behind the sports hall, where nobody goes in winter, and she sat on a wall and she played.

On Tuesday two girls stopped and listened.

On Wednesday there were six people, and one of them was Kerem.

He stood at the back with his hands in his pockets. He did not smile. When she finished he came closer and looked at the guitar for a long moment.

"Where is that from?" he asked.

"The room under the stairs."

"That room is full of rubbish."

"I know," Deniz said.

Kerem sat down on the wall. He did not ask this time. He took the guitar and he played the four notes from the first day, and then he stopped.

"The third one is too high," he said.

"Yes."

"I know it is too high." He looked at his own hands. "My finger does not go there. It is short. I try every day."

Deniz took the guitar back. She played the same four notes and she used a different finger for the third one.

Kerem watched. He watched very carefully.

"Do it again," he said.

# Friday, Ten Past Two

The show started at two o'clock in the sports hall.

The whole school sat on the floor. The small children sat at the front and they were noisy. Somebody put a blue cloth on the wall behind the chairs and it fell down twice.

Deniz stood at the side with the old guitar under her coat. Mrs. Aylin walked past her three times and did not see her.

The music group played two songs. Kerem played the school guitar and a tall boy played the drum box. A girl called Melis sang. It was good. The children clapped.

Then, in the middle of the third song, the sound went wrong.

Kerem's hand stopped. He looked down at the guitar. One string hung loose across the wood like a piece of grass.

The hall went quiet. Somebody at the back laughed.

Melis looked at Mrs. Aylin. Mrs. Aylin looked at the door. Two hundred children waited and the quiet became big and heavy.

Deniz did not think about it. She walked out from the side of the hall with the guitar in her hands.

"Take it," she said.

Kerem looked at the old brown guitar. Then he looked at her.

"You play," he said.

"I do not know the song."

"You know it. You listened to us all week." He moved his chair to the side and made a space. "Sit down. Play the low notes. I am going to sing with Melis."

Deniz sat down. Her hands were cold and wet. Somebody at the front shouted her name, or maybe another name, she did not hear it.

She played the first note. The crack buzzed and the sound went out into the hall.

Melis started to sing again. Kerem sang under her, low and quiet, and he was not very good, but he was loud enough. The tall boy hit the drum box. Deniz found the song after four or five bars. Then she was inside it, and she stopped thinking about her hands.

They played to the end. The children clapped and the small ones at the front stood up and jumped.

After the show Mrs. Aylin came and stood in front of Deniz with her arms crossed.

"Where is that guitar from?" she said.

"The room under the stairs."

"Nothing in that room works."

"This one works," Deniz said.

Uncle Sami was at the back of the hall with his bucket. He was looking very hard at the floor. His shoulders were moving up and down.

Mrs. Aylin took a long breath. She looked at the guitar, at the two new strings, at the thin crack in the old brown wood.

"You put the strings on yourself?"

"Yes."

"With your own money?"

Deniz said nothing.

"January," Mrs. Aylin said. "The new group starts in January. You are in it." She pointed at the guitar. "And that thing is school property. So it stays with you, and you must look after it."

She walked away. Then she turned back.

"The buzz," Mrs. Aylin said. "On the low notes."

"I can fix it," Deniz said quickly. "I think."

"Do not fix it," said the teacher.

Kerem was waiting by the door with the black bag over his shoulder. They walked out together into the cold yard. The sun was low and orange behind the sports hall.

"My finger," Kerem said. "Show me again. The third note."

"Now?"

"Now."

They sat down on the cold step. Deniz put the old guitar across her knees and she showed him, slowly, three times.

The last children came out of the hall. They went home in the orange light. The two of them stayed on the step and played the four notes again and again, until they were right.
