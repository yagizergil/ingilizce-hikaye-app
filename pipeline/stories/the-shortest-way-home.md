# Twenty Minutes

The last box was heavier than it looked, and Kerem had to lift it with both arms. He pushed it into the back of the van, where it slid against two empty crates and stopped. Rain had started again, and the yard behind the office smelled of wet stone and old oil.

Nadir came out of the office with a paper in one hand and a pen behind his ear. "One more," he said.

"You told me the milk boxes were the last ones," Kerem said, and he closed the van doors harder than he meant to.

"This came in at four o'clock. Alaca village, up the hill road. The customer has been waiting three days, and she has called twice today."

Kerem looked at his watch. It was twenty past four, and his daughter's birthday dinner would start at seven. He had promised Elif that he would be home before the candles were lit. He had made the same promise last year, and last year he had walked through the door at nine, when the plates were already washed and dry. Elif had said nothing about it that night, which was worse than any shouting.

"How long to Alaca?" he asked.

"Forty minutes if the main road were open." Nadir shook his head. "But they closed it on Monday for the bridge work, so you have to go around the lake. Ninety minutes each way, maybe more in this rain."

Kerem did the numbers in his head and did not like the answer. Three hours of driving, plus the delivery itself, would put him in his own street at eight o'clock or later.

"Sign it," Nadir said, "or leave it until Monday and I will telephone her myself."

Kerem took the pen and signed, because he had never once left a box in the yard. Then he remembered something, and the memory arrived like a small light in a dark room. His father had driven a delivery truck for twenty-six years, and his father had always used the old stone road over Yellow Hill. That road climbed behind the town, ran along the top, and came down again on the far side near Alaca.

"There is the old hill road," he said.

Nadir made a face. "Nobody uses it now."

"My father used it every week. Twenty-five minutes to Alaca, he said, and he was never wrong about roads."

"Your father drove that truck a long time ago." Nadir pulled the pen from Kerem's fingers. "Do what you want. But if you break something on that road, the company will not pay for it."

Kerem climbed into the van and started the engine while the rain grew heavier on the roof. Before he pulled out of the yard, he called home, and his wife answered after two rings.

"Where are you?" Hale asked.

"Leaving now. I have one box for Alaca, and then I am finished."

"Alaca? Kerem, the main road is closed."

"I know a way." He watched the wipers push water across the glass. "Tell her I will be there before seven."

There was a small noise on the line, and then Elif's voice came, quick and close to the phone. "Say it properly, Dad."

"I promise I will be home before you light the candles."

"You said that before."

"I know what I said before." He put the van into gear. "This time I am saying it and I mean it. Save me the chair by the window."

# The Old Stone Road

The hill road began behind the football field, and it narrowed almost at once. Grass grew in a green line down the middle, and small stones jumped under the tyres like fish. Kerem drove slowly, with both hands on the wheel, and after five minutes the town disappeared behind him in the grey rain.

It was better than the lake road. He was sure of that. His father used to talk about this hill the way other men talked about old friends, and he could still hear the words. Watch the second bend, the rain sits there. Take the top slowly and you will see the whole valley.

The second bend was full of water, exactly as his father had promised, and Kerem laughed out loud when the van came through it.

At the top the trees opened, and he saw a farmhouse below the road on the left side, with a light in one window and a wet roof shining. He passed it without stopping. Alaca village was further on, over the top and down the other side, and he had twenty minutes of daylight left in his hands.

Then the road ended.

He saw it too late to be careful about it. Half of the hillside had come down across the road, a long brown wave of earth and stones with the broken branches of trees sticking out of it. In front of the earth lay a fallen sign, face up in the mud, and the letters had almost washed away. Kerem stopped the van and sat for a moment with the engine running.

"Two years," he said to nobody. "Maybe three."

He could not go forward, so he had to turn around. The road was too narrow for a clean turn, but there was a soft, flat place on the right where the ground opened a little. He reversed carefully, turned the wheel, and felt the back of the van drop.

It happened quietly, which surprised him afterwards. The rear wheels sank into wet ground up to the middle, and when he pressed the accelerator the engine shouted and the van moved nowhere at all. He tried again, more gently. The wheels turned and threw brown water up the sides of the doors.

Kerem got out into the rain and looked, and his stomach went cold. The van was leaning back and to the left, with one wheel almost buried, and it was not coming out by itself.

He took his phone from his pocket. There was no signal, not one bar. He walked up the road, then down the road, then climbed a wet bank and held the phone above his head like a man asking the sky for help. Nothing changed.

The time was twenty-five past five.

He worked for half an hour anyway, because standing still was worse. He found flat stones and pushed them under the wheels. He broke branches from the fallen trees and laid them in the mud. He took the rubber mat out of the driver's side and put it where the tyre could bite it. Each time he tried, the wheels threw the stones out again, and the van sank a little deeper.

At six o'clock he stopped and stood in the road with rain running off his chin. The valley below was already dark, and he could see one small light in the trees, five or six minutes back down the hill.

He locked the van, put the delivery paper inside his jacket, and started walking toward the light.

# The House with the Green Door

The farmhouse had a green door, an oil lamp burning inside, and an old brown dog that got up slowly and did not bark. A woman opened the door before Kerem had finished knocking, as if she had been watching the road for a long time.

"You are wet through," she said.

"My van is stuck up there, at the landslide. I need a telephone."

"Then you need a different house." She stepped back to let him in. "The line came down in the storm on Tuesday, and there is no signal on this hill. The men from the company say they will come next week."

The room was warm, and a pot of soup sat at the back of the stove. Kerem stood on the mat, dripping, and something in his chest went tight when he understood what she had told him.

"Is there a car? A neighbour?"

"My son has the truck in town, and he comes on Sunday." The woman poured hot water into a glass and pushed it into his hands. "Sit down before you fall down. I am Sevda."

Kerem's fingers stopped on the glass. He took the wet paper from his jacket and read the name again, and then he laughed, because there was nothing else to do.

"Sevda Bilgin," he said. "Alaca village, hill road. I have your box in the van."

She sat down opposite him and looked at him properly for the first time. "You came over the hill? Nobody comes over the hill. That road has been closed for two years."

"My father drove it every week."

"Your father drove it before the ground came down." Her voice was kind, but it did not soften the facts. "The post comes up from the town now, the same way you came, and then it goes back down again."

Kerem put his head in his hands for a second. Everything he had believed at twenty past four was wrong, and his daughter was setting a chair by the window for a man who was sitting on a hill in the dark.

"What is in the box?" he asked, mostly to keep talking.

"The pump for my well. The old one burned out, so I have been carrying water from the stream in buckets since Monday." Sevda smiled without much humour. "So we are both waiting for something."

"Is there a tractor?"

"In the shed. It has not started since the spring, because the battery is dead and my son keeps forgetting."

Kerem stood up so quickly that the dog lifted its head. "Show me."

The shed smelled of dust and diesel, and the tractor stood under a grey sheet with a rope and a heavy chain hanging on the wall behind it. Kerem pulled the sheet off, opened the metal cover, and looked at the battery for a long moment while his mind moved.

"My van has a good battery," he said. "It is only twenty minutes up the road."

"Your van is stuck."

"The van does not need a battery to be pulled. The tractor does." He was already picking up the chain. "I take the battery out of my van, carry it down, put it in the tractor. We drive up, pull the van out with the chain, and then I put my battery back where it belongs."

Sevda looked at the tractor, then at him. "And if it does not work?"

"Then I have walked up a hill twice in the rain," Kerem said, "and I have still not been home."

# Candles at Ten Past Eight

They took the lamp and Sevda's late husband's tools, and they walked up together, because she said she was not going to sit by the stove while a stranger carried her tractor battery through the mud.

The van stood exactly where he had left it, leaning and beaten and half brown. Kerem opened the hood, worked the wet nuts loose with cold fingers, and lifted the battery out with a grunt. It was heavier than the box had been at four o'clock, and it seemed to grow heavier with every step down the hill.

The dead battery came out of the tractor easily. Kerem put his own in its place, tightened the connections, and climbed into the metal seat.

"If this does not turn," he said, "close your ears."

He turned the key. The engine coughed once, stopped, and something in the shed went very quiet. He waited, counted to ten, and tried again. This time the tractor woke up with a noise like a bad-tempered animal, and blue smoke rolled out under the roof, and Sevda put her hands flat against her face and laughed.

The tractor climbed the hill in low gear with the lamp swinging on a hook. Kerem backed it up to the van, hooked the chain to the frame, and told Sevda to sit in the driver's seat of the van and steer while he pulled. The chain came tight. The tractor pushed down on its big wheels, the mud held on for a few seconds, and then the van came out with a long wet sound and stood on stones again, dirty and free.

Kerem swapped the batteries back in the rain and started the van at the first turn of the key.

They went down to the house together, and he carried her box in and cut the tape with his knife, because he wanted to see the pump with his own eyes. Sevda signed the wet paper against the wall.

"Sit and eat," she said.

"I cannot. I am already late."

She did not argue, which he was grateful for. She went to the shelf by the stove and came back with a small wooden horse, brown and smooth, the kind of thing a person makes slowly over many winters.

"My husband carved these. Give it to your daughter and tell her the truth about tonight."

The old road took him down in twenty-two minutes. At the top of the last bend his phone woke up in his pocket and rang and rang with all the messages that had been waiting since half past five. He stopped at the side of the road and called home.

"We ate," Hale said. "The neighbours went home at eight."

"The candles?"

"She will not let anybody touch them."

He reached his own street at ten past eight. Elif was sitting on the front steps in her coat, with her chin on her knees, and she stood up when the headlights found her. Kerem got out covered in mud to the elbows, and she looked at him without saying anything at all.

"I broke it by twelve minutes," he said. "I am not going to pretend I did not."

"Where were you?"

"On a road that is not there any more." He put the wooden horse into her hand. "An old woman gave me this. She has water in her house tonight because I was late, and I would like to tell you about it while you light your candles."

Elif turned the little horse over twice, and then she took his dirty hand and pulled him toward the door.
