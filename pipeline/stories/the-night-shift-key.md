# The Key in the Drawer

Rain hit the glass doors of the Hotel Deniz at ten past three, and the front hall was empty. Selin had checked the guest list twice already, so she opened the lost and found drawer to keep her hands busy. Inside there were two phone chargers, a child's blue shoe, a pair of reading glasses and a book in German. Under the book lay a key. The small wooden ball on its ring had a number painted on it in white: 407.

The hotel still used real keys, because the owner said that guests liked the weight of them in their pockets. Every key came back to the hook behind the desk when a guest left, and that was the first rule Halit had taught her. Selin turned in her chair and looked at the board. The hook for 407 was empty.

She was twenty-two, and she had been working the night desk for seven months. Her mother's little shop had closed in the winter, so Selin paid the rent now and her brother's school fees as well. She studied in the afternoons, slept in the early evening and came to work at eleven. The job was not interesting, but it was hers, and she thought about that every time Halit raised his voice.

She typed the room number into the computer, and the screen told her a simple story. Mr. Arda Kaplan had arrived on Friday, stayed three nights and checked out on Monday morning at twenty to seven. That was three days ago. The bill was paid, the card was signed, and nothing about it looked strange.

Then Selin opened the black folder where the cleaning staff wrote their notes at the end of every shift. Monday, room 407: could not enter, door locked from inside. Tuesday, room 407: the same. Wednesday, room 407: the same, told the manager. Three days, three lines, and the same short words in three different hands.

She sat still for a while and listened to the rain. The night receptionist was supposed to stay at the desk until six, and she knew that rule too. But the hotel had forty rooms, the phone had not rung since midnight, and the key was warm now in her hand. She took the master key from the drawer and went up the stairs.

The fourth floor smelled of old carpet and cleaning liquid. Selin walked to the end of the hall, stopped in front of 407 and knocked twice. "Hotel service," she said, and her voice sounded thin in the empty hall. Nobody answered. She knocked again, waited, then put the master key into the lock and turned it.

The door opened two centimetres and stopped. Behind it she could see the little metal bar that guests push across the door at night, and it was in place. Someone had closed that bar from the inside. Through the narrow opening she saw a strip of dark room, the corner of a bed and a curtain that moved slightly. The window was open.

"Is anyone there?" she said.

The curtain moved again, and cold air came through the gap and touched her face. She thought about pushing harder, but the bar would break the door frame, and the noise would wake the whole floor. She closed the door, locked it and stood in the hall while her heart went much too fast.

Back at the desk she wrote three words in the night book: room 407, door. Then she looked at the words for a long time and drew a line through them. Halit read the night book every morning at eight, and Halit did not like questions from the night desk. Selin put the key in her pocket instead of on the hook. It was not a decision she could explain, even to herself.

# Out of Service

Halit arrived at five to eight with his coat over his arm and his coffee already half finished. He was a heavy man of about fifty who spoke to the staff as if they were slow children. Selin waited until he had put his bag down, because she had learned that bad timing made everything worse.

"There is a problem with 407," she said. "The cleaning staff cannot get in, and the bar is across the door on the inside."

"The lock is broken," Halit said, without looking up. "It happens with old doors. The room is out of service."

"But a bar can only be closed from inside the room."

He looked up then, and his face was tired rather than angry. "The room is out of service," he repeated. "I have written it in the system. If a guest asks, we are full on the fourth floor. Go home, Selin. You look terrible."

She stood there for a moment with her bag on her shoulder. Halit opened his newspaper, which meant the conversation had ended. On her way out she passed Vedat, the repair man, who was carrying a box of tools towards the lift.

"Are you going up to 407?" she asked him.

"Why would I go to 407?"

"Because the lock is broken."

Vedat put the box down and rubbed his neck. "Nobody has told me about any lock," he said. "The only thing broken on that floor is the pipe in 406, and that has been broken since January. Water came through the ceiling, and they closed the room. It is full of dry buckets and old chairs now."

"406 is next to 407," Selin said slowly.

"They are joined," Vedat said. "There is a door in the wall between them, for families with children. We never lock those doors, because the keys were lost years ago." He picked up his box again. "Ask Halit. He does not like me touching the fourth floor."

Selin went home and lay in bed with the curtains closed, but sleep did not come. She kept seeing the curtain moving in the dark room and the little metal bar sitting in its place. If a guest had gone, the bar should be open. If the bar was closed, somebody had closed it. Those two facts could not both be true, and yet they were.

That night she came to work early. Between one and two she went up to the fourth floor and stood at the end of the hall in the dark, feeling slightly ridiculous. At twenty past two a thin yellow line appeared under the door of 407. It stayed there for perhaps three minutes and then went out.

Selin did not move. At four o'clock she heard water running through the pipes above her head, the sound a shower makes when somebody is careful with the tap. Then the water stopped, and the building was quiet again.

She thought about the police. She thought about the words she would have to say, and about Halit in the morning, and about her brother's school fees. Then she thought about a person washing quietly in a room that was supposed to be empty, three metres from where she was standing.

At half past four she went downstairs, took the key for 406 from the board and put it in her pocket next to the other one.

# The Room Behind the Wall

Room 406 opened with a soft, tired sound, and the smell of damp came out to meet her. Vedat had told the truth about the buckets. There were six of them on the bare floor, and the ceiling above the bed had turned brown and rough, like old bread.

The connecting door was in the wall on the right, behind a chair. Selin moved the chair, put her hand flat on the wood and listened. Somebody on the other side was breathing, and the breathing was too quick for sleep.

"I work downstairs," Selin said quietly. "I am the girl at the night desk. I am not the police, and there is nobody with me."

Nothing happened for a long moment. Then the door opened, and a young woman stood there in a hotel dressing gown with her arms crossed tightly over her chest. She was younger than Selin, perhaps nineteen, and her hair was still wet.

"Please," she said. "Please just let me get my bag."

The room behind her was clean and almost empty. The bed was made in the careful hotel way, with the corners tight. A plastic bag of clothes sat on the chair, and a bus ticket lay on the table next to a half bottle of water.

"What is your name?" Selin asked.

"Nihal." The girl's hands were shaking, so she pushed them into the pockets of the gown. "I cleaned here for eight months. Rooms 400 to 520, every day except Sunday."

They sat down, one on the bed and one on the chair, because standing had become strange. Nihal talked in a low, flat voice, as if she had already told the story to herself many times.

In March a guest in room 512 had said that his gold watch was missing. Nihal had cleaned 512 that morning. Halit called her into the office at four in the afternoon, closed the door and told her that the hotel could not keep her. She asked for proof, and he said that proof was not her business. She asked for her last two weeks of pay, and he said that she should be glad he was not calling the police.

"I lived in the staff building," Nihal said. "That is part of the job, so I lost the bed on the same day. My family is six hours away, and my father would believe him, not me." She looked at the wall. "I wash dishes at a restaurant near the station. They pay me at the end of each night, but it is not enough for a room in this city."

"And 407?"

"That man ran for his bus on Monday morning. He dropped his key by the lift, and I picked it up." Nihal shook her head slowly. "I knew every empty room in this hotel. I knew 406 was closed and that the door in the wall does not lock. On Tuesday I got frightened and put the key in the drawer downstairs, so that it would look like a normal lost key. I thought that would make it finish. I did not think about the bar."

Selin looked at the little metal bar across the door and understood the whole thing at once. Nihal had closed it every night, the way anybody would in a strange building. The bar had been telling the truth for three days, while everyone else told a different story.

"I thought someone had died in here," Selin said.

"I am sorry."

"No." Selin pressed her hands against her knees. "I am glad. But this is worse in another way, because now I know it."

Nihal understood the sentence immediately. "If you tell him, I will be outside by lunchtime, and you will be fine. If you say nothing, we will both be fine until somebody else finds me." She stood up and reached for the plastic bag. "So the easy thing is the first one."

"Sit down," Selin said. "The watch. Was it ever found?"

Nihal stopped with the bag in her hand. "I do not know," she said. "Nobody ever told me anything again."

# The Lost and Found Book

The lost and found book lived in the bottom drawer, and nobody had looked at it seriously in years. It was a wide grey book with a line for the date, the item, the room and the name of the person who handed it in. Selin took it back to the desk at half past five and turned the pages slowly under the lamp.

March came after two blank weeks. On the eighth there was a line about a telephone left in the restaurant. On the fourteenth she found it, written in blue ink in Halit's own hand: gold watch, found inside laundry bag, room 512, returned to guest the same evening. He had signed his name at the end of the line, the way he signed everything, with a long tail under the H.

Nihal had been sent away on the ninth of March. The watch had come back on the fourteenth. Halit had known for six months, and he had written it down himself.

Selin sat with the book open until the sky turned grey behind the glass doors. Mrs. Aydın, the owner, came in every Friday at nine to look at the numbers, and this was Friday. If Selin waited for her, she would also have to say that she had opened two rooms at night with a master key, and that she had known about Nihal since four in the morning and had done nothing about it. There was no version of the story that left her clean.

At six she hung both keys on their hooks. At nine she was still there, standing beside the desk in her coat, and Halit had already asked her twice why she had not gone home.

Mrs. Aydın was a small, quick woman who never took her coat off before she had read the night book. Selin put the grey book on the counter in front of her, open at the fourteenth of March, and told the whole thing from the beginning without stopping. The key in the drawer. The bar across the door. The connecting wall in 406. The girl upstairs who had been sleeping in an empty room and washing at four in the morning so that nobody would hear the water.

Halit's face went from red to a kind of grey. "She is a thief," he said, "and this girl has been walking into locked rooms all night."

"Yes," Mrs. Aydın said. "One of those two things is written here in your handwriting, Halit." She turned the book towards him and put her finger on the line. "Read it out loud, please."

He did not read it. He looked at the wall behind Selin instead, and the silence became very long.

They went up to the fourth floor together. Nihal was sitting on the made bed with her plastic bag on her knees, dressed and ready, exactly as she had been for the last three hours. She stood up when the door opened and started to apologise, and Mrs. Aydın raised one hand to stop her.

"You will come back on Monday," she said. "The staff building has a free bed on the second floor. Your two weeks of pay will be in an envelope this afternoon, and I will speak to you properly when I have spoken to other people first."

She turned to Selin in the hall. "You entered guest rooms at night alone, and you left the desk empty for nearly an hour. That goes in your file as a written warning, and if it happens again you will not be working here." She paused, and something moved at the corner of her mouth. "You will find me here every Friday at nine. Next time, come at nine."

Selin walked home through the wet morning streets with her hands deep in her pockets. She slept badly, woke at four in the afternoon and went to work at eleven as usual.

The room was cleaned on Saturday and rented on Sunday to a family from Ankara with two children, who asked for the connecting door to 406 and were told it was closed for repair. When they came down for breakfast, the father put the key on the counter, and Selin lifted it and hung it back on its hook, where a key is supposed to be.
