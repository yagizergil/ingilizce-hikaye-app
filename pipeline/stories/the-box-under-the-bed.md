# Coin by Coin

Aslı kept her money in an old glass box under her bed.

Every week, she put a little more inside. Money from small jobs: carrying bags at the market, watching the neighbor's baby, selling old toys she did not use anymore. Coin by coin, note by note, the box grew heavier.

She was saving for a bicycle. A blue one, in the shop window on Main Street. Every day after school, she walked past that window and looked at it. The bicycle had a silver bell and a basket at the front.

"Two more months," she told her best friend, Defne. "Then I can buy it."

"What color is the seat?" Defne asked.

"Black," Aslı said. "With a white line on the side."

They stood at the window together, noses almost touching the glass.

"You worked so hard for this," Defne said. "Carrying bags in the hot sun. Watching that baby who never sleeps."

"It is worth it," Aslı said. "No more walking to school. No more waiting for my brother to finish with his old bike."

That evening, Aslı counted her box again at the kitchen table. Her little brother, Kaan, sat next to her, watching the coins.

"How much now?" he asked.

"Three hundred and forty lira," Aslı said, proud. "I need one hundred more."

Kaan reached for a coin, and Aslı moved the box away fast.

"Do not touch," she said. "This is for the bicycle."

Their mother walked in from the other room. She looked tired, more tired than usual. She sat down slowly and put her hand on her back.

"Are you okay, Mama?" Aslı asked.

"Just tired," her mother said. "A long day at work."

But the next morning, her mother was not able to get out of bed. Her back hurt too much. Aslı's father called the doctor, and the doctor came to the house that afternoon.

"She needs an X-ray," the doctor said. "And maybe more tests after that. Her back is not right."

"How much does that cost?" Aslı's father asked quietly, in the kitchen. He thought the children were not able to hear.

Aslı stood behind the door and listened. She heard a number. A big number.

She walked back to her room and sat on her bed. Under it, the glass box waited, full of coins and notes she counted every night for two months.

She thought about the blue bicycle in the window, the silver bell, the black seat with the white line.

Then she thought about her mother, lying in bed, unable to stand up straight.

She pulled the box out from under the bed and held it in her legs for a long time.

# The Hard Choice

At dinner, nobody talked much.

Aslı's father made soup, but he forgot to add salt. Kaan pushed his spoon around the bowl without eating. Their mother stayed in her room, resting on the doctor's orders.

After dinner, Aslı knocked on her parents' door.

"Come in," her father said.

Her mother lay on the bed, a bed behind her back. She smiled when she saw Aslı, but the smile looked small and careful, like it hurt to make it too big.

"How are you feeling?" Aslı asked.

"Better," her mother said. "Do not worry about me."

Aslı sat on the edge of the bed. She held the glass box in both hands.

"I have money," she said. "For the tests. For the doctor."

Her father looked up fast. "Aslı, no. That is your bicycle money. You worked for months."

"I know," Aslı said. "But Mama is more important than a bicycle."

Her mother tried to sit up, then stopped, her face tight with pain.

"Aslı," she said, "I do not want your savings. That money is yours. You earned every coin."

"You always say family helps family," Aslı said. "This is me helping."

The room was quiet. Kaan appeared in the door, listening.

"I have money too," he said then. He ran to his room and came back with a small bag. "Twenty lira. From my birthday."

Their father looked at both of his children, and his eyes were wet.

"I am not going to take your money," he said. "We are going to find another way. Maybe I ask for more hours at work. Maybe we sell something we do not need."

"Papa," Aslı said, "the tests are this week. We do not have time to find money slowly. I have three hundred and forty lira right now. That helps today."

Her mother reached out and took Aslı's hand.

"You wanted that bicycle for so long," she said, her voice soft. "I remember you talking about it every single day."

"I can want it again next year," Aslı said. "I cannot get another mother."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then her mother pulled Aslı close and held her tight, careful not to move her back too fast.

"Thank you," she said very quietly. "I am going to pay you back, every lira, when I am better."

"You do not have to," Aslı said.

"I want to," her mother said. "That box was your dream. A gift does not erase a dream. It just waits for a better day."

The next morning, Aslı's father took the box to the bank and changed the coins into paper money. He added some money from his own savings, not enough for everything, but enough to start the tests that week.

Aslı watched him leave with the box, now half empty of coins but in some way feeling less empty inside than before.

# The Blue Bicycle

The tests took two weeks.

The doctor found a problem in her mother's back, but not a dangerous one. With rest and the right medicine, she was going to get better, slowly but certainly.

Aslı visited the shop window less often now. It felt strange to look at the bicycle when her box sat empty under her bed. But one afternoon, Defne pulled her toward Main Street anyway.

"Just look," Defne said. "It does not cost anything to look."

The bicycle was still there, blue and bright, with the same silver bell.

"Someone is going to buy it soon," Aslı said. "It stayed in that window a long time already."

"Maybe," Defne said. "Or maybe it is waiting for you."

Aslı laughed, a little sad. "I have no money now. I gave it all to Mama."

"You did the right thing," Defne said. "My mother told my father about it. She said not many kids do that."

At home that evening, Aslı found her mother sitting up in the kitchen, much stronger now, cutting vegetables for dinner.

"You are cooking again," Aslı said, happy.

"A little," her mother said. "Slowly. The doctor said I can stand for short times now."

Kaan ran into the kitchen with a paper in his hand.

"Look what I found," he said. "Papa put an envelope in the drawer. It has your name on it."

Aslı opened the drawer carefully. Inside was a small envelope, and on it, in her father's handwriting: "For Aslı's bicycle."

Inside were fifty lira.

"Papa put a little money in there every week," her mother said. "He started the day you gave us your box. He wanted to surprise you."

Aslı felt her eyes fill with tears, happy ones this time.

"But we still owe money for the tests," she said. "This must go to that first."

"The tests are paid," her father said, walking in from the hallway. "My extra hours at work covered the rest. This money is only for you. For your bicycle."

"Fifty is not three hundred and forty," Aslı said, but she was smiling now.

"No," her father agreed. "But it is a start. And this time, we are all going to help fill the box. Together."

That week, the whole family found small jobs. Kaan sold his old toys at the market, next to Aslı. Their mother, once she felt stronger, sewed clothes for a neighbor. Their father worked extra hours on weekends.

Two months later, Aslı stood outside the shop with a full envelope in her hand. The blue bicycle was still there, waiting, just like Defne said.

Inside the shop, the owner counted her money carefully, then smiled.

"This is exactly right," he said. "Down to the last lira."

He moved the bicycle outside. The silver bell caught the sunlight. Aslı touched the handlebar, hardly believing it was finally hers.

Her mother stood nearby, steady on her feet now, watching with a proud smile.

"Ring the bell," Kaan said. "Come on, ring it!"

Aslı pressed the small silver bell, and it rang bright and clear across the street. She climbed onto the bicycle, wobbled once, then rode forward, her family walking behind her, all the way home.
