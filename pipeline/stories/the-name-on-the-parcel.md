# The Name on the Box

The young man from the delivery company said the name twice, and both times Selin was sure that she had heard it wrongly. She was standing at the top of the stairs with wet hands, because she had been washing dishes since seven in the morning.

"Hakan Demir," he said again. He was holding a box against his chest with both arms. "Second floor, above the bread shop. This is the right building, isn't it?"

"The building is right," Selin said, "but nobody with that name lives here."

"The address is right as well." He turned the box so that she could read the label. The letters were printed by hand in blue ink, small and very careful, and the address was theirs, down to the number of the flat.

Selin dried her hands on her skirt and took it. The box was smaller than a shoebox and much heavier than it looked, and when she tilted it, something inside moved once and then stopped. The corners were soft with old tape, and there were pale marks on the top where other labels had been pulled off. The driver was already going down the stairs two steps at a time, like a man with forty more stops before lunch.

Her mother was cutting bread at the kitchen table when Selin came in with the box under her arm.

"Who was at the door?"

"A delivery, but the name on it is wrong." Selin put the box down between the bread and the salt. "Hakan Demir. Do we know anybody called Hakan Demir?"

The knife stopped. Her mother did not look up and did not put the knife down, and for a few seconds the only sound in the flat was the fan of the bread shop under the floor.

"Take it back down to the street," she said.

"The man has gone already."

"Then leave it in the hall. Somebody will come for it."

"Mother, who is he?"

Nurten pushed the bread to one side, as if the table had suddenly become too small for everything on it. "My brother," she said, and then she carried the knife to the sink.

Selin sat down slowly on the other chair. She had heard that name perhaps three times in her whole life, always quietly, always in the voice that people keep for illnesses. She was eleven years old when he left, and what she remembered was a thin man who smelled of oil, who kept little wheels and springs in a jam jar, and who had fixed the bell on her bicycle twice in one summer. Then she remembered the rest of it, which had come from the neighbours and never from her mother: money gone from her father's shop, a bag packed at night, a brother who never wrote a single line to anyone.

"Is he coming back?" Selin asked. "Is that why the box came here?"

"He is not coming back, and nobody in this house is waiting for him." Her mother began to wash her hands, and she washed them for much longer than her hands needed.

Selin carried the box into the hall and put it down beside the shoe shelf, but she did not walk away from it. The label had two lines that she had not read properly at the door. In the bottom corner, in the same blue ink and the same careful handwriting, somebody had written the name of the sender: H. Demir, Clocks and Radios, Station Road, Kavak.

She read it three times before she understood. The man had sent the box to himself, from a town six hours away by bus, to a home that he had left twelve years ago.

Her mother came to the hall door with a towel in her hands. "Don't open it," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because it isn't ours." Then she turned off the hall light, although Selin was still standing in it.

# The Five O'Clock Van

The box was gone from the hall before Selin woke up.

"The company sent somebody for it," her mother said, without turning round from the stove. "Refused packages go back on Friday."

Selin ate her bread and said nothing, but the sentence stayed with her all the way to work and all through the day. She spent nine hours at the print shop cutting paper for wedding cards, and while the machine went up and down, she kept asking herself the same question. Her mother had never worked for a delivery company. So how did a woman who never left the street know which day the refused packages travelled?

On Friday she left the print shop twenty minutes early and half ran to the delivery office by the bus station. It was a small place with a high counter, a shelf of grey plastic trays behind it, and a green van waiting outside with its back doors open. The clock on the wall said twenty to five.

"There was a package for our address on Wednesday," Selin said. "A box for Hakan Demir. My mother sent it back, but I want it."

The woman behind the counter was about sixty, and she looked at Selin over her glasses for a long moment before she touched the computer. "Are you Hakan Demir?"

"He is my mother's brother."

"Then bring me your mother's brother, or bring me a letter from him." She typed the address anyway, because people who work at counters are curious like everybody else. Then she read the screen, and her face changed in a way that Selin did not like at all. "Wait. This address has refused this package eleven times."

"Eleven times?"

"Once a year, always in the second week of September, always the same sender and the same address." The woman turned the screen a little, as if the numbers would explain themselves. "Eleven years. Somebody in your family says no at the door, and we carry it back to Kavak. He pays for it himself every time, and it is not a cheap journey."

Selin held the edge of the counter. Her uncle had left twelve years ago, so in the first year he had sent nothing at all. She thought about the pale marks on the lid where the old labels had been pulled off, one every September, and about her mother saying that nobody in the house was waiting for him.

"Can I stop it going back?"

"Not today, and not without him." The woman looked at the clock, and then she did something kind. She wrote the sender's address on the back of an old form and pushed it across the wood. "This was printed on the box anyway. If you want him, you will have to go and stand in front of him."

At five o'clock the driver carried the trays out, and the soft brown box was in the second tray, near the top. Selin watched him lift it without looking at it. The van turned at the traffic lights, and she stood in the warm air of the road with a piece of paper in her hand, feeling that she had lost something that had never been hers.

She did not go straight home. She walked twice round the market instead, past the men folding their tables, and by the time she reached the stairs above the bread shop she knew exactly what she was going to say.

# What the Coat Held

Selin put the paper on the kitchen table, between the salt and her mother's tea, and she smoothed it flat with two fingers.

"Station Road, Kavak," she said. "Eleven times, Mother. Eleven Septembers, and every one of them you said no at the door before I came home from school."

Her mother looked at the paper for a long time. Then she stood up, went to the room she had shared with Selin's father, and came back with a brown envelope, soft at the edges like the box had been. She sat down heavily and put the envelope beside the paper.

"Count it if you want," she said.

Selin did not touch it. "What is it?"

"It is the money that your uncle took from the shop." Her mother's hands were flat on the table. "Except that he did not take it. Your father took it, and he hid it, and then he was too frightened of me to put it back."

The fan of the bread shop turned under the floor, the way it did every evening.

"When your father said that the money was gone, I believed the first thing that came into my head. Hakan was living with us, he had no work that summer, and people had started saying things in the street." Her mother's voice was very level, and that was worse than crying. "So I said it too. I stood on the stairs in front of three neighbours and I called my own brother a thief. He did not shout at me. He packed his bag that night, and he asked me only one thing, which was to look after our mother's clock. Then he went, and everybody in this street decided that a man who goes away like that must be guilty."

"And the money?"

"Nine years ago, when your father died, I gave his clothes away." She turned the envelope over, so that the open side faced down. "The money was folded inside a sock, in the inside pocket of his grey coat, with the paper band from the shop still around it. I sat on the floor of that room until it got dark. I have not spent one note of it since, and I have never told anybody, not even you."

Selin thought about the eleven boxes and about her mother standing at the top of the stairs, saying no, closing the door, and then going back to the kitchen to cut bread. "You could have written to him."

"What would I write? That I was wrong? Eleven Septembers, and every year the box came back to him with my answer on it, and every year he paid again." Her mother pressed her hand over her eyes for a second. "There is no letter in the world that makes eleven years shorter."

"Then I will go instead of a letter." Selin picked up the paper. "I have Saturday free. I am going to Kavak."

Her mother did not say yes and did not say no. She washed the two cups, put them on the shelf, and stopped with her back to the room.

"The bus from the station leaves at seven," she said. "The one at nine goes through the hills and takes two hours longer. Take the seven."

Selin did not ask how she knew.

She woke at half past five, dressed in the dark, and was in her seat before the driver had finished his cigarette. Somewhere after the second town, when the sun was coming up over the fields, she opened her bag to look for her water. The brown envelope was lying under her jacket, and on the back of it, in her mother's careful handwriting, there were two lines.

It was in your father's coat. I have not touched one note in nine years.

# Station Road

Kavak was smaller than Selin had expected, and Station Road began at the bus station and ended at a bridge, so she found the shop in four minutes. The window was full of clocks. Most of them showed different times, but there was one row along the bottom where six clocks all showed the same minute, and somebody had put that row in the front on purpose.

Inside, a man was sitting at a table under a strong lamp with a radio open in front of him. His hair was grey, his glasses were pushed up on his forehead, and his hands were exactly as Selin remembered them: thin, oily, completely still while the rest of him moved.

"I will be with you in one moment," he said.

"Uncle Hakan."

He put down the small tool he was holding. He looked at her for a while, and Selin watched him work through the years in his head, the way people do when they cannot ask directly.

"You stand like your mother," he said at last. "Are you Selin?"

"Yes."

"Then sit down, because I am going to make tea, and I am not going to hear anything until I have made it." He filled the kettle at a tap in the corner. His hands were not steady, and he had to try twice with the match.

On the shelf behind him, between a broken television and a box of wire, sat the brown package. There was a white label across the top with the word REFUSED printed on it in large letters. It had arrived that morning, he said, and he had not opened it, because there was no reason to open a thing that he had packed himself.

"I put it away for the winter, usually," he said. "In September I write the address, and then I put it away again. It has become a habit, like taking medicine."

Selin took the envelope out of her bag and put it on the table between them.

"I don't want money," he said quickly.

"It isn't my money and it isn't hers. It is the money from the shop." Selin turned the envelope over, so that he could see the two lines. "My father hid it in his coat. She found it nine years ago, after he died, and she has kept it in a drawer ever since because she could not think what else to do with it."

Her uncle read the two lines. Then he read them again, and he sat down slowly on the stool by the table, and he kept the envelope in both hands without opening it, as if it were something he had repaired and did not want to break.

"Nine years," he said.

"Twelve, if you count from the stairs. She told me all of it on Thursday night, and she told me the bus times as well." Selin pushed her tea away, because her throat had gone tight. "Open the box. Please. You know what is in it, but I don't."

He cut the tape with a blade so old that the handle was black. Inside, wrapped in a shirt that had been folded and unfolded many times, there was a small square wooden clock with a face the colour of milk and a little key of yellow metal tied to it with string.

"Your grandmother's," he said. "It stopped in the week that I left, and I had promised to mend it. I finished it in my second month in this town, and then I had nothing left to promise anybody." He turned the key a few times, and the ticking came up out of the wood and filled the whole shop, louder than seemed possible for something so small. "Every September I wound it and sent it home. It was the only way I could knock on the door without asking to come in."

Selin looked at the row of six clocks in the window that all agreed with each other.

"There is a bus at four," she said.

Her uncle stood up, wiped his hands on a cloth, and put a card in the window with the closed side facing the street. He did not take much: the envelope, a bag, a coat for the evening. He would not let Selin carry the clock, so he held it against his chest with both arms the whole way to the station, exactly the way the delivery driver had held the box.

Selin telephoned from the station and said only four words, because she had to say them fast or not at all: there are two of us. Then they sat in the front seats, and the clock ticked between them for six hours, through the fields and the two towns and the long slow climb into the dark.

It was nearly ten when the bus reached their street. The bread shop was shut and the whole building was black, except at the top of the stairs, where somebody had turned the hall light on and left it burning.
