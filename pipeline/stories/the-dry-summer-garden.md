# No Rain for a Month

Selin checked the garden every morning before the sun got too hot.

This summer was different from other summers. No rain fell for four weeks. The ground was hard and grey, like old bread. The green leaves in her grandmother's garden were turning yellow at the edges.

"The beans do not look good," Selin said, touching a dry leaf.

Her grandmother stood next to her with a metal bucket. "No rain means no easy food this year," she said. "We must carry water by hand now."

Every day, they walked to the village well with two buckets each. The well was deep, and the water inside was lower than Selin had ever seen it. She looked down into the dark water and felt a little afraid.

"Is the well going to dry up?" Selin asked.

"Not yet," her grandmother said. "But we must be careful. Everyone in the village needs this well."

At the well, other people from the village waited in a line. Selin saw her neighbor, a young man named Kaan, filling three big buckets for his own garden.

"Three buckets, every day?" Selin's grandmother asked him. "That is a lot, Kaan."

"My tomatoes need it," Kaan said. "I am going to sell them at the market next month. I need a good harvest."

"We all need water," an old woman in the line said. "Not just for tomatoes."

Kaan did not answer. He picked up his buckets and walked away quickly.

Selin's grandmother watched him go, her face worried. "This is how problems start," she said quietly. "When water is short, people forget to share."

That evening, the village leader came to talk to the families near the well. He was an older man with a calm voice, and people listened when he spoke.

"The well is getting low," he said. "If we are not careful, it can go dry before the rain comes back. From tomorrow, every family can take two buckets a day. Not more."

Some people were not happy about this. Kaan stood at the back of the group with his arms crossed.

"Two buckets is not enough for a real garden," he said loudly.

"Two buckets is enough for people to survive," the village leader answered. "A garden is important, but it is not more important than water for cooking and drinking."

Selin's grandmother agreed. "He is right," she said to Selin as they walked home. "But it is going to be a hard month for the garden."

That night, Selin lay awake and thought about the beans, the tomatoes, and the small apple tree behind the house. She thought about her grandmother's face when she looked at the dry ground.

She had an idea, but it was still small and unclear, like a shape in the dark.

The next morning, she woke up early and walked around the house before anyone else was awake. She looked at the roof, the old metal pipe near the kitchen window, and the empty barrels behind the shed.

An idea began to grow clearer in her mind.

# Water from the Sky

At breakfast, Selin told her grandmother about her idea.

"When it rains again, all the water from the roof just runs onto the ground," Selin said. "What if we catch it instead?"

Her grandmother looked up from her tea. "Catch rain water? How?"

"With the old barrels behind the shed," Selin said. "We can put them under the metal pipe, where the water comes down from the roof. When it rains, the barrels fill up instead of the ground."

"But it is not raining now," her grandmother said. "The sky is clear every day."

"I know," Selin said. "But it must rain again sometime. And when it does, we are going to be ready, instead of losing all that water."

Her grandmother thought about this for a moment. "It is a good idea," she said. "But it does not help us today. Today the garden is still dry, and we still only have two buckets."

Selin agreed with her, but her mind kept working. That afternoon, she visited the old woman from the well, the one who talked about sharing water.

"Do you know anything about saving water?" Selin asked her. "For a garden, in a dry summer?"

The old woman smiled. "My mother grew vegetables in a much drier place than this, many years ago," she said. "She put water only at the base of each plant, in the evening, never in the middle of the day. The sun does not take it away as fast at night."

"Not in the middle of the day?"

"In the hot sun, most of the water disappears into the air before the plant can drink it," the old woman explained. "Evening water goes down into the ground and stays there until morning."

Selin thanked her and ran home to tell her grandmother.

That evening, they tried it. Instead of watering the garden in the morning, as they always did, they waited until the sun went down. They poured the water carefully around the base of each plant, not on the leaves.

"This uses less water for the same result?" her grandmother asked, watching closely.

"That is what she said," Selin answered.

Three days later, the beans looked a little better. Not perfect, but better. The yellow leaves stopped spreading, and a few small new leaves appeared, bright green against the tired plants.

Selin also found an old cloth and covered the ground around the plants with it, the way she read in an old book at school. The cloth kept the sun off the ground and helped it stay wet longer.

Word about Selin's ideas spread through the village. A few other families started watering in the evening, too, and some began collecting old containers to catch rain when it finally came.

Kaan came to see Selin's grandmother one afternoon. He looked tired and a little embarrassed.

"My tomatoes are still dying," he said. "Two buckets is not enough for me, even in the evening."

"Maybe it is not about more water," Selin said. "Maybe it is about using the water better."

She showed him the cloth around the plants, and the way they watered only the base, only at night. Kaan watched carefully, saying nothing for a long time.

"I am going to try this," he said finally. "I have nothing to lose now."

That night, dark clouds moved slowly across the sky for the first time in weeks. Nobody was sure if they meant rain, but everyone looked up anyway, hoping.

# The First Rain

The rain came three days later, in the middle of the night.

Selin woke up to a strange sound on the roof, soft at first, then louder and faster. She sat up in bed and listened. She was not sure this was real.

Water. Real water, falling from the sky.

She ran to wake her grandmother. "It is raining! It is really raining!"

Her grandmother came outside in her nightclothes, laughing, her face turned up to the sky. Around the village, other doors opened, and other people stepped outside into the rain, some laughing, some just standing quietly with their eyes closed.

"The barrels!" Selin shouted. "We must put out the barrels!"

They ran to the shed and pulled the old barrels under the metal pipe, just as Selin planned weeks before. Rain water poured down from the roof, filling the barrels quickly in the dark.

By morning, the rain stopped, but the barrels were full. The ground in the garden was dark and wet for the first time in over a month.

The beans and tomatoes did not fix themselves overnight, but they looked stronger already, their leaves standing up instead of hanging down.

Kaan came by later that morning, checking his own garden on the way. "My tomatoes look better too," he said, sounding surprised. "Maybe the cloth trick really works."

"Maybe it is the rain," Selin's grandmother said, smiling.

"Maybe it is both," Kaan said, and for the first time in weeks, he almost smiled too.

The village leader came to check the well that afternoon. The water level was rising slowly, filled by the rain soaking into the ground.

"We are not out of danger yet," he told the families gathered near the well. "One rain does not end a dry summer. But it gives us more time, and time is what we needed most."

He looked at the barrels standing outside several houses now, more each day as families copied Selin's idea.

"Whoever thought of these barrels," he said, "that person helped this whole village."

Selin's grandmother put a hand on Selin's shoulder, but she did not say anything. She did not need to.

Over the following weeks, it rained two more times, never a lot, but enough. The garden slowly turned green again. The beans grew long and full. Even Kaan's tomatoes, once nearly dead, filled out and turned red in time for the market.

On market day, Kaan brought a basket of his tomatoes to Selin's house before he left.

"For you and your grandmother," he said. "For the cloth trick. And for the barrels."

Selin's grandmother took the basket and smiled. "You did the hard work," she said. "We only shared an idea."

"An idea is worth something too," Kaan said, "especially in a dry year."

That evening, Selin stood in the garden and looked at the barrels, full and ready for the next dry week, whenever it came. The beans moved a little in the evening wind, green and alive again.

She was not worried about the next dry summer anymore. Now she knew what to do.
