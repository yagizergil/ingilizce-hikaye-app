# The Big White Birds

Every year, big white birds came to Ali's village in spring.

They built their homes on the roofs and near the road, high above the cars. Ali's grandfather called them the sign of spring.

"When they come, winter is really over," he said. "Not before."

This year, two birds came back to the same roof as always: Ali's roof. His grandfather said they were the same two birds from last year. Ali did not know how his grandfather knew one bird from another, but he believed him.

"How do you know?" Ali asked.

"The male has a small dark mark on his leg," his grandfather said. "Look next time he stands still."

All summer, Ali watched the two birds. They caught fish and small animals near the river. They fed their young. By late summer, three young birds stood on the roof with them, tall and grey, learning to move their long legs.

"Soon they fly away," his grandfather said one evening. "All of them, together. South, to a warm country."

"Why can't they stay here?" Ali asked.

"Because winter comes, and there is no food here in winter," his grandfather said. "They must go, or they die. It is not a choice for them. It is the only way."

Ali did not like this answer, but he understood it.

In early autumn, the air turned cold at night. Ali saw that the birds on the roof were restless. They opened their wings and closed them again and again. It looked like practice.

"They are getting ready," his grandfather said. "In a few days, they leave."

One morning, Ali woke up to a strange, loud sound. Birds from all over the village were flying up into the sky, circling, calling to each other. It was the biggest group of birds Ali ever saw in his life.

He ran outside without his shoes.

The sky was full of them, hundreds of white shapes turning together, like one huge, slow animal. Ali watched from the garden, his neck bent back, his mouth open.

Then he looked at his own roof.

Two birds were still there. The mother bird and one young one. The father bird and the other two young ones were already high in the sky, moving away with the big group.

"Grandfather!" Ali called. "Two of them did not go!"

His grandfather came outside slowly and looked up at the roof, then at the sky.

"That is strange," he said. "They do not usually wait."

The young bird on the roof stood in a strange way. One wing was down, and the other was not. It tried to open both wings together, but only one wing moved.

"Something is wrong with its wing," Ali said.

His grandfather's face became serious. "If that is true," he said, "it cannot fly south. And winter is coming fast."

The mother bird stayed beside her young one on the roof, even as the sky grew empty and quiet again. She did not follow the others.

Ali looked at his grandfather. "We must help it," he said. "We cannot just watch."

His grandfather agreed, but he did not look happy. "Wild birds are difficult to help, Ali," he said. "But we can try."

# A Wing That Will Not Open

Getting the young bird down from the roof was not easy.

Ali's grandfather borrowed a tall ladder from a neighbor. He climbed up slowly, one careful step at a time, while Ali held the ladder steady from below.

The mother bird watched from the roof, making loud, sharp sounds. She did not attack, but she stayed very close.

"Easy," his grandfather said quietly, to the bird, not to Ali. "Easy now."

He wrapped the young bird in an old blanket and carried it down. Up close, Ali saw the wing more clearly. It looked swollen near the top, and the bird made a small, pained sound when it moved.

"Is it broken?" Ali asked.

"I am not a doctor for birds," his grandfather said. "But I think it hit something. A wire, maybe, or a branch, when it was practicing to fly."

They put the bird in a big wooden box in a small room by the garden, with water and small pieces of fish. The mother bird stayed on the roof above it, watching, calling sometimes.

"Is she going to leave without her baby?" Ali asked.

"I do not know," his grandfather said. "Mother birds are strong, but winter does not wait for anyone, not even for love."

Every day, Ali brought fish from the market and fed the young bird by hand. At first, it did not trust him and moved away. After a week, it began to eat from his fingers.

"You are patient," his grandfather said, watching from the door. "That is good. Wild animals learn to trust patience, not speed."

Ali checked the wing every day, too. He was careful, but the bird still made small sounds of pain when he touched the swollen part.

"It is not getting better," Ali said one evening. "The days are getting colder. What if it never flies again?"

His grandfather sat down next to the box. "Then it lives here," he said. "In this room, through the winter. We keep it warm and fed. Not every bird has to fly to be alive."

"But it wants to fly," Ali said. "I can see it. It tries every day."

His grandfather looked at the young bird for a long time. "Yes," he said finally. "It does want that. So we keep trying, too."

They asked the old man at the market, who kept farm animals, for advice. He looked at the wing and pressed it softly in different places.

"Not broken," he said. "Just badly hurt. Rest, warmth, and time. That is the only medicine for this."

"How much time?" Ali asked.

The old man was not sure. "Weeks, maybe. But winter is close. I cannot promise you weeks."

Ali walked home with his grandfather in silence. Above them, the sky was grey and heavy, and the wind smelled like the first snow.

# South Before the Snow

The mother bird did not leave.

She stayed on the roof through cold mornings and grey afternoons, even as every other bird in the village disappeared into the southern sky. Ali worried about her, too, now — two birds instead of one, both waiting for a wing to heal.

"She needed to go with the others," Ali said. "Now she is in danger too."

"Some mothers cannot leave," his grandfather said simply. "It is not always the smart choice. It is just the only one they can make."

Ali worked harder with the wing. He warmed water and pressed a soft, warm cloth against the swollen part, the way the old man showed him. Slowly, over many days, the swelling went down.

One cold morning, three weeks after they found the bird, Ali opened the door and the young bird stood up differently. It opened both wings together, evenly, for the first time.

"Grandfather!" Ali shouted. "Come and look!"

His grandfather hurried out in his coat, half-dressed, and watched the bird open and close its wings again and again in the small room.

"It is not perfect yet," his grandfather said carefully. "But it is much better."

They carried the bird outside, into the open garden, and let it go. It walked a little, opened its wings fully, and tried a short flight across the garden. It landed hard, but it landed on its own legs.

The mother bird called loudly from the roof.

For three more days, they practiced together in the garden, the young bird flying a little farther each time. On the fourth day, dark clouds moved in from the north, low and heavy.

"That is snow," his grandfather said, looking at the sky. "Real snow, tonight or tomorrow."

That afternoon, the young bird flew up onto the roof beside its mother for the first time in weeks. It opened its wings fully in the wind and moved them slowly.

"Is it ready?" Ali asked.

"I do not know," his grandfather said. "But I think it must try now, or wait for spring in a cold room."

As the sun went down, both birds stood on the roof, facing south, into the wind. Ali watched from the garden, his hands cold, his heart beating fast.

The mother bird opened her wings first. The young one followed, slower, less sure. Together, they rose off the roof, circled once above the house, and flew south, low and steady, toward the grey horizon.

Ali watched until they were too far to see.

"They are going to make it," his grandfather said, putting a hand on Ali's shoulder. "Not because it is easy. Because they did not stop trying."

That night, the first snow fell on the empty roof.

In spring, when the warm wind returned, Ali stood in the garden every morning and watched the sky.

One clear morning, two white birds landed on the roof. One of them had a small dark mark on his leg. Next to him stood a young bird, and its wing opened perfectly now, both sides the same.

Ali smiled and ran to get the fish.
