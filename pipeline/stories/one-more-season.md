# The Letter in the Rain

It had rained all morning, and the field behind the school was more water than grass. Nuri stood at the edge of it with his hands deep in his coat pockets and counted his players twice. Nine, and then ten, because Salim was busy pulling a flat ball out of a puddle by the fence.

"He's coming, coach," Salim said without looking up. "His mother had a busy morning at the shop, and he stayed to help her."

"Training starts at four," Nuri said.

"It's Emre, so he'll come."

Nuri blew his whistle and sent the nine of them running around the outside of the field. They ran like men who already knew the result of Sunday's match, and that told him plenty. For thirty-one years he had stood in this same corner, in rain and in summer heat, and he could read a team's shoulders from twenty metres away. These shoulders said Yeşiltepe.

Yeşiltepe was the big town on the other side of the hill, and everything there was newer. Their ground had proper seats, their shirts arrived in boxes every August, and they had won the district cup four times in six years. Kavaklı had reached the final once, thirty years ago, and had lost that match by three goals. The wooden shelf in the clubhouse, which somebody had built for cups, had held nothing but dust since the day it went up.

On Sunday, Kavaklı would play Yeşiltepe in the final, and on Monday, Nuri would turn sixty-four. He had told the club committee that this was his last season, and they had thanked him twice and then talked about the roof.

Emre came through the gap in the fence at twenty past four, breathing hard, with his jacket half open. He was seventeen and thin as a fence post, and he ran with his head up, which was the thing that made him different from every other boy Nuri had coached. Under his jacket he carried a plastic bag, and inside the bag there was a white envelope.

"I'm sorry I'm late," he said, pushing the bag at his coach. "The postman came at four. Look."

Nuri dried his wet hands on his trousers before he touched the envelope. The letter inside was printed on thick paper, and at the top there was the blue and white mark of Deniz Spor, the big club in the city. He read it slowly, because his eyes were not what they had been ten years ago.

Deniz Spor was pleased to invite Emre Kaya to join the youth team of the club for the coming season. A club doctor would examine him on Monday morning at nine o'clock, and he should bring the letter and a family member with him.

At the bottom, under the printed part, somebody had written three lines by hand.

"One more thing. Please do not let the boy play any match before Monday. If he is hurt this weekend, we cannot hold the place open. There are other boys. A. Aydın."

Nuri read the handwriting three times, and when he lifted his head the running had stopped. All nine of them were standing in the rain, looking at the paper in his hands.

"Read it out," Salim said.

So Nuri read it out, all of it, in the flat voice he used for team news. When he reached the name Deniz Spor, Ferit the goalkeeper shouted so loudly that two birds came off the school roof. The boys hit Emre on the back and pulled at his wet hair. Salim was thirty-four, worked in the bread shop, and had never been asked to join anything in his life; he shook the boy's hand for a long time.

Then Ali, who was fifteen and said everything, said the thing that nobody else wanted to say.

"So you can't play on Sunday."

After that the field was very quiet, and the rain was the only sound in it. Emre looked at his boots, and water ran off the end of his nose.

"That's what it says," he answered.

Nuri folded the letter along its old lines and put it in his inside pocket, where the rain could not reach it. "Two lines," he said. "Passing, both feet, and I want to hear the ball."

They trained for forty minutes, and it was the worst forty minutes of the whole year. Afterwards Nuri rode his bicycle home along the wet road, and the letter sat against his chest like a stone in a coat.

# The Empty Shelf

On Friday evening the clubhouse was full, which happened perhaps three times a year. Hakan, who owned the shop that sold tools and paint and paid for the team shirts, had brought tea in a big pot. The talk was all about Sunday, and the talk went around and around the same corner.

"He plays," Hakan said. "Half an hour, no more. He plays, we win, and he goes to the city on Monday like a king."

"And if a Yeşiltepe boy stands on his ankle in the first minute?" Salim asked.

"Then one day he'll have a boy standing on it in the city as well."

"In the city they have doctors who fix ankles," Salim said. "Here we have your cousin and a bag of ice."

The old men laughed, but nobody looked at Nuri, and that was how he knew they had decided already. They wanted the boy on the field. They had watched him grow up between the goalposts, and they had waited thirty years for a cup on that shelf. The shelf was behind Hakan's head while they argued, and it was as empty as ever.

Nuri drank his tea and said very little. When he walked out into the dark he passed the shop where Emre's mother sold bread and vegetables and telephone cards. She was closing the shutters, and she stopped when she saw him.

"Nuri Bey," she said. "Is it true that he can't play?"

"That's what the club wrote."

She pushed a piece of hair back under her scarf and looked up the empty street. "His father has been in the city for three years, and he sends money in the winter," she said. "If Emre goes, at least there'll be somebody there who knows his name." Then she pulled the shutter the rest of the way down. "But he wants that cup. He talks about it in his sleep."

On Saturday morning Nuri told the team in the changing room, standing with his back against the door. He had learned long ago that bad news should always be given standing up.

"Emre isn't in the eleven tomorrow, and he isn't on the bench either," he said. "He'll sit with me in his own clothes, and he'll shout, and on Monday morning he'll get on the seven o'clock bus, and that's the end of it."

Ali began to argue at once, and Ferit joined him, and for half a minute the room was loud. Salim let it run, and then he said one word, "Enough," and the room went quiet the way it always did for Salim.

Nuri watched Emre while all this happened, because he had learned to watch faces instead of listening to voices. He had given bad news to a hundred boys in thirty-one years, and he knew every shape a face makes when a door closes. Emre's face did none of those things; it went loose, like a hand opening.

The boy said that it was all right, and then he pulled his shirt over his head very quickly. For a moment nobody could see him.

They trained without him, and he stood by the fence with his hands in his pockets and shouted the right things at the right times. Twice he told Ali where to run, and Ali ran there, and he looked like a coach already. He looked, Nuri thought, like a boy who had been given a heavy bag to carry and had suddenly been allowed to put it down.

That evening Nuri sat at his kitchen table with the team sheet in front of him, and he wrote ten names and stopped. Outside, the rain had gone and the sky over the hill was clear, which meant a dry field on Sunday. A dry field was a fast field, and on a fast field a boy like Emre was worth two goals.

He read the handwritten lines again. There are other boys.

Something in that sentence had been sitting wrong all week, like a stone inside a shoe. Deniz Spor had watched Emre four times, and they had sent a man in a grey car to a field with no seats. Clubs like that did not write by hand about ankles.

Nuri looked at the clock, and it was too late to telephone anybody. He put the sheet in his pocket, went to bed, and did not sleep much.

# What Mr Aydın Said

He used the telephone in the shop at nine on Saturday night, because the shop had the only phone on the street. Emre's mother let him use it for free, and she went into the back room while he waited. The number was printed at the top of the letter.

Mr Aydın answered on the fourth ring, with a television loud behind him. He found young players for Deniz Spor, and he had the voice of a man who was always about to leave a room.

"Kavaklı," he said. "The boy with the head up. Yes."

"I want to ask about Monday," Nuri said. "If the boy plays half an hour tomorrow and comes on Tuesday instead, is that a problem for you?"

"Why would he not come on Monday?"

"Because you wrote on the letter that he mustn't play."

There was a pause, and then the television got quieter. "Coach, we don't write things like that," Mr Aydın said. "Boys play, boys get hurt, and that's football. If we waited for boys who never got hurt, we would have an empty club."

Nuri put his hand flat on the shop table. "It's on your letter, in your own writing."

"It's there because he asked me to put it there," Mr Aydın said. "He telephoned me on Tuesday, and he asked me to write it in my own hand, so that people would believe it. I thought his mother was worried, so I did it as a kindness. Is there a problem?"

"No," Nuri said. "No problem at all. Thank you for your time."

He walked to the field instead of going home. The lights were off, but there was a moon, and he heard the sound before he saw anything. It was the flat knock of a ball against the wall behind the goal, again and again, at the same speed a man's heart works when he is afraid.

Emre stopped when the gate made its noise.

"You should be asleep," Nuri said.

"So should you."

Nuri sat down on the low wall, which was cold, and patted the place beside him until the boy came and sat too. For a while nobody said anything, and the ball moved slowly away into the dark.

"I spoke to Mr Aydın this evening," Nuri said.

Emre put his hands over his face. He did not cry, but he stayed like that for a long time, and when he took his hands away he looked younger than seventeen.

"They still talk about it," he said. "The kick. In the tea house, in the barber's shop, everywhere. Last May, two goals each against Alaköy, ninety minutes gone, and I put the ball over the wall and into the road. Uncle Recep said it in front of everybody: the boy has good feet and no heart." He pulled his sleeves down over his hands. "If I don't play tomorrow, then I'm the boy who went to Deniz Spor. If I play tomorrow and it happens again, then I'm the boy who missed twice, and they'll say it about me here for forty years."

"And in the city?"

"In the city nobody knows me."

Nuri looked out at the goal he had painted every spring since he was thirty-two. "When I was nineteen, a club came for me," he said. "It wasn't Deniz Spor, it was a smaller one, but it was a real club with a real ground. My father had a bad back that year, and there was the shop, so I thought about it for a week and I stayed. Everybody said I was a good son, and they said it for about a month, and then they stopped saying anything at all." He put his hand on his bad knee. "That was forty-five years ago, and I have thought about it every single week since then. Not because I would be famous now. Because I never found out what I was."

"So you think I should go."

"Of course you should go," Nuri said. "Get on the bus, do the doctor's tests, be polite to everybody, and work harder than the city boys." He stood up slowly, holding the wall with one hand. "But you asked a man in the city to write a lie, so that I would keep you off the field. And I nearly did it for you."

Emre said nothing at all.

"There are ten names on my sheet, and there's one empty line," Nuri said. "I'm not putting anything on it tonight. If you want to play, come tomorrow at one o'clock and say it out loud, in the changing room, in front of Salim and Ferit and Ali. Not to me on a wall in the dark."

He walked home, and at the kitchen table he read the ten names once more. Then he turned the letter over and looked at the handwriting for a long time before he put it away.

# Eleven Metres

The Yeşiltepe supporters filled one whole side of the ground, and their team came out in shirts that still had the folds from the box. Kavaklı had the wall, the fence, and about two hundred people who had shut their shops for the afternoon.

At one o'clock the changing room smelled of ice and old shirts. Nuri read out ten names, slowly, and then he stopped and looked at his paper as if the last line had fallen off it.

Emre stood up in his kit, and his boots were tied twice.

"Write my name," he said.

Salim laughed once, loudly, like a man dropping a heavy box on a stone floor. Ferit hit the wall with both hands, and somebody's water bottle went over. Nuri wrote the name on the empty line, pressing hard, and gave the sheet to Ali to take to the referee.

Yeşiltepe scored after twenty-six minutes, and it was nobody's fault except everybody's. A long ball bounced twice, Ferit came out and then changed his mind, and their tall boy put it into the empty net. The Yeşiltepe side of the ground sang a song about the hill.

Kavaklı did not fall apart, which surprised Nuri more than he wanted to say. Salim stopped playing football and started working like a man in a field, winning every second ball. Emre kept his head up and kept running at the two big defenders, even when they knocked him down. They knocked him down four times before half-time, and he got up four times, but the fourth time he was slower.

"Come off now," Nuri said at half-time. "You've done enough, and Monday is tomorrow."

"Twenty minutes more," Emre said.

In the sixty-fourth minute Emre took the ball on the left, went inside, and passed it low across the box. Salim arrived late and hit it under the goalkeeper with his bad foot. The wall behind Nuri shook, and Salim ran to the fence and stood in front of the bread shop customers with his arms out. The referee had to bring him back.

After that both teams got tired and afraid, and the football turned ugly. Nuri stood on the line and said the same three things over and over again. The sun went behind the hill, and his knee hurt, and the minutes would not move.

In the eighty-eighth minute Ali played a ball over the top, and Emre was running before it came down. The tall defender caught him inside the box, took his shirt first and then his legs, and the whistle went at once. The referee pointed at the spot, eleven metres from the goal line. The Yeşiltepe players shouted at him for half a minute and got nowhere.

Salim picked the ball up and looked at Nuri, and Nuri looked back and said nothing at all. There was nothing left that he could usefully say. So Salim turned and put the ball into Emre's hands.

The ground went quiet in the strange way that grounds do, and Nuri could hear a car on the road behind the school. He watched the boy put the ball down, step back four paces, and look up once at the fence. Uncle Recep was standing there with his hat in his hands.

Emre ran and hit it low and hard, inside the goalkeeper's right hand.

What happened next took eleven minutes, and Nuri missed most of it. People he had known for sixty years were holding him by the shoulders and shouting into his ear. When the final whistle came, the Yeşiltepe players were still two metres from the corner flag, and the score was two goals to one. That evening the district cup came into the clubhouse in the arms of a boy who put it on the wooden shelf himself. The singing went on until eleven.

At seven the next morning Nuri was at the bus stop. Emre came with his mother, one bag, and a limp that he was trying to hide.

"Show the doctor the ankle," Nuri said. "Don't hide it, because they know that boys play."

"Coach," Emre said, and then he stopped.

Nuri gave him the envelope with the letter inside it. The three handwritten lines at the bottom had been crossed out with a pen, twice, very firmly.

"Take that with you," he said. "And when somebody asks you what you did the day before, tell them the truth."

The bus pulled out past the school, and Nuri stood in the cold until he could not see it. Then he walked to the field, because the goalposts needed painting before the spring. He was the only one who knew where the paint was kept.
