# The Blue Folder

Deniz stood outside the big glass building and looked at his watch.

Nine o'clock. He had one hour.

Inside his bag was a blue folder. It had his papers, his photo, and a letter from his old teacher. The letter said he was a good worker. He needed this job. His father was sick, and the family needed more money.

He walked to the door, but a guard stopped him.

"Where are you going?" the guard asked.

"I have a meeting," Deniz said. "For a job. At ten o'clock."

"Which company?"

Deniz looked at his paper. "Aydin Trade. Room nine."

The guard looked at a big list. "Aydin Trade is not on this floor," he said. "They moved last month. New building, next street."

Deniz felt his heart jump.

"Next street? Where?"

"Number twelve. Blue door." The guard pointed. "You have time. Go now."

Deniz ran. His bag hit his back with every step. The street was busy, and cars moved slowly in a long line. He looked for a blue door, but every door looked grey or brown or green.

He stopped a woman with a red bag.

"Excuse me. Number twelve?"

"Two more streets," she said. "Turn left at the market."

Deniz ran again. He turned left at the market, past boxes of tomatoes and bread. A man called out prices, loud and fast. Deniz did not stop.

He found number twelve at nine forty. The door was blue, small, and old. Not like the glass building. He stood there and breathed hard.

Inside, a woman sat at a desk. She looked tired.

"I am here for the ten o'clock meeting," Deniz said. "For the job."

The woman looked at her computer. "Name?"

"Deniz Kaya."

She read the screen again, slowly. "I do not see this name," she said. "Are you sure it is today?"

Deniz's stomach dropped. He opened his blue folder and showed her the letter.

"It says here," he said. "Ten o'clock, Tuesday."

The woman looked at the letter. Then she looked at her screen again.

"Oh," she said. "This is wrong. Somebody put you in the wrong list. You are with Mr. Aydin, not Mrs. Aydin. Different room."

"Is that bad?"

"No," she said. "But it is upstairs. Room twenty, third floor. You must hurry. Mr. Aydin does not like to wait."

Deniz thanked her and ran to the stairs. He did not want to wait for the small, slow lift.

On the stairs, his blue folder slipped from his hand. Papers fell everywhere — his photo, his letter, and a list of questions he wrote for the job talk. He got down on his knees and picked them up, one by one. His hands moved fast, but the stairs were long.

A man walked down the stairs and stopped.

"Do you need help?" he asked.

"No, thank you," Deniz said, still picking up papers. Then he looked up. The man wore a dark blue suit and held a coffee cup.

"Are you going to room twenty?" the man asked.

"Yes! Do you know it?"

"I am Mr. Aydin," the man said. "And you are late."

Deniz stood up fast, papers still in his hands.

"I am sorry," he said. "I went to the wrong building first. Then the wrong list. I ran across three streets."

Mr. Aydin looked at him for a long moment. He did not smile, but his eyes were not angry either.

"Room twenty," he said. "Two minutes. I am going to wait there."

He walked up the stairs, and Deniz followed, holding his folder tight against his chest.

# The Ten Questions

Room twenty was small, with one table and two chairs.

Mr. Aydin sat down and put his coffee on the table. Deniz sat across from him. His hands were still shaking a little from the stairs.

"Tell me about your work before," Mr. Aydin said.

"I worked in a shop for two years," Deniz said. "I sold shoes. I also helped with the money at the end of the day."

"Why do you want this job?"

Deniz thought for a second. He wanted to say the true thing, not just a nice thing.

"My father is sick," he said. "He cannot work now. I need a job with good pay. But I also want a job where I can learn something. Not just stand and wait for customers."

Mr. Aydin wrote something on his paper.

"This job is in the office," he said. "You check numbers. You check boxes that come from other cities. It is not exciting work."

"I like numbers," Deniz said. "In the shoe shop, I never made a mistake with money. Not once, in two years."

"That is a big promise."

"It is true."

Mr. Aydin looked at him. Then he pushed a paper across the table.

"Here is a test," he said. "Ten numbers. Add them. Five minutes."

Deniz picked up a pen. His hand still shook, so he waited two seconds before he started. Then he began.

The numbers were long, with many parts. He added them slowly, then checked his work again. Outside, he heard the small clock on the wall. Every second felt loud.

He finished with one minute left. He checked his answer one more time, then pushed the paper back.

Mr. Aydin checked it against his own paper.

"Correct," he said.

"Good," Deniz said, and let out a breath.

"Now, one more question," Mr. Aydin said. "This morning, you went to the wrong building. Then the wrong room. You lost time twice. Give me one reason to trust you with important boxes and numbers."

Deniz did not answer right away. He thought about lying, about saying it was not his mistake. But that felt wrong.

"The letter had old information," he said. "I did not check it again before I left home. That is my mistake. I can check things twice next time. I already checked my work on your test twice."

Mr. Aydin's face did not change, but he wrote something else on his paper.

"One more thing," he said. "On the stairs, your papers fell. I saw your questions for me. You wrote ten questions about this job."

"Yes," Deniz said, a little surprised he saw that. "I wanted to know about the job too. Not just answer questions."

"Ask one," Mr. Aydin said.

Deniz looked at his list. He picked the question that mattered most.

"If I do good work," he said, "can I learn more jobs here? Not stay in one place forever?"

Mr. Aydin almost smiled this time.

"Yes," he said. "People who check numbers well often move up. We had a man like that. Now he runs this whole floor."

He stood up and put out his hand.

"Come back Monday," he said. "Eight o'clock. Do not be late."

Deniz shook his hand. "I will be early," he said.

# The Right Building

On Monday, Deniz walked to the street with the blue door.

He arrived at seven forty, twenty minutes early. He checked the address three times before he left home, and again on the bus.

The small office looked different in the morning light. Boxes stood by the wall, ready to go out. A woman he did not know was already at a desk, drinking tea.

"Are you the new one?" she asked. "Deniz?"

"Yes."

"I am Sibel. I work with the boxes and the lists." She pointed to a chair next to hers. "Sit here. Mr. Aydin is coming at eight."

Deniz sat down. On the desk was a stack of papers with numbers on them. They were orders from shops in other cities, boxes of shoes going out that day.

"Can I look at these?" he asked.

"Go ahead," Sibel said. "You are going to check numbers like these every day."

He looked at the first paper. Twenty boxes to one city, fifteen to another. The numbers on the paper did not match a smaller list next to it. He checked again.

"This is wrong," he said. "The paper says twenty boxes, but the truck list says eighteen."

Sibel looked over his shoulder.

"You are right," she said, surprised. "Good eyes. That truck leaves in one hour. We must fix this now."

They found the missing two boxes near the back door, not yet on the truck. Sibel called the driver and told him to wait five more minutes.

At eight o'clock, Mr. Aydin walked in. He saw the boxes moving, and Sibel talking fast on the phone.

"What happened?" he asked.

"The new boy found a mistake," Sibel said. "Two boxes were missing from the truck list. We fixed it before the truck left."

Mr. Aydin looked at Deniz.

"Your first hour, and already a mistake found," he said. "Not bad."

"I only checked the numbers," Deniz said. "Like you asked."

"That is the job," Mr. Aydin said. "Checking numbers is not small work. A missing box means an angry shop and lost money. You saved us both today."

He walked to his own desk at the back of the room, then stopped and turned around.

"Deniz," he said. "Do you remember your question? About moving up?"

"Yes."

"Ask me again in six months," Mr. Aydin said. "Not today. Today, just check your numbers."

Deniz smiled and picked up the next paper on the desk. The numbers were long, but he was not shaking anymore. He read them one by one, careful and slow, the way he checked his own work on the test.

At lunch, he called his father.

"I got the job," he said. "I start today. Real work, real pay, every week."

His father was quiet for a second.

"Did you check the address twice this time?" his father asked. Deniz heard the smile in his voice.

"Three times," Deniz said. "I am not going to be late again."

He went back to his desk. Sibel gave him a cup of tea and a new stack of papers.

"Welcome to Gate Nine," she said. "That is what we call this building. The old one had nine floors and one gate. This one has one floor and no gate at all. But the name stayed."

Deniz laughed and picked up his pen. Outside, a truck started its engine and drove away, full of boxes, every number correct.
