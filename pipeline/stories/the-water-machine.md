# No Water in the Gardens

The knocking started before six in the morning.

Nesrin opened one eye. Somebody was hitting her workshop door with a stone, or a shoe, or an angry hand. She put on her trousers and went down the stairs in the dark.

Three men stood outside. Behind them the sky was orange over the hill.

"The water is not coming," the first man said.

"Good morning to you too, Osman."

"Nesrin. There is no water in the gardens."

She woke up properly then. She got her tools and walked with them to the top of the village, past the school and the empty market place. Nobody talked. The air was already warm and it was not seven o'clock.

The water machine sat in a small stone house by the road. It pulled water up from under the ground and pushed it out to fifty gardens. In July those gardens were everything. Tomatoes, beans, peppers, and the melons that people sold in town on Sundays.

Nesrin put her hand on the metal. It was cold. A working machine is never cold.

"When did it stop?" she asked.

"Last night," Osman said. "Maybe eleven. My wife heard a bang."

"A bang is not good."

She opened the side of the machine and looked inside with her light. Then she was quiet for a long time. The men stood behind her and breathed.

"Well?" Osman said.

"Give me some space."

She took the machine apart on the floor of the stone house. Piece by piece, in a line, in the right order. She did this for twenty years and her hands knew the way. Oil, black and thick, went up her arms.

At half past eight she found it.

It was a small round piece of metal, about the size of an egg, deep inside the machine. It was broken in two. One half sat in her hand and the other half was in three small pieces.

She showed it to Osman.

"That is a small thing," he said.

"It is the thing that turns everything else."

"So put a new one in."

Nesrin looked at the broken metal. There were letters on the side, very old, and a number: 1974.

"This machine is older than me," she said. "Nobody makes these pieces now."

The men looked at each other. A boy came running up the road with a bottle of tea for his father, and stopped when he saw everybody's face.

"How long do the gardens have?" Nesrin asked.

Osman looked at the sky. It was blue and hard and completely empty.

"Two days," he said. "In this heat, two days. Then the young plants are going to die. After that the melons."

"Three days for the melons," another man said.

"Two."

Nesrin put the broken pieces in her pocket. She stood up and her knees hurt.

"I am going to town," she said. "The parts shop opens at ten."

"And after the parts shop?"

"Then I am going to think of something else."

She took the eleven o'clock bus back from town at three in the afternoon. Her bag was as empty as it was in the morning.

The man in the parts shop was kind. He looked in three books and made two phone calls. Then he told her the truth: that piece stopped being made in 1991. He can order one from a big city in the west. It comes in ten days, maybe fourteen.

Ten days is nine days too many for a tomato plant in July.

Nesrin got off the bus by the school. Osman was waiting on the wall with his hands between his knees.

"No," she said, before he asked.

He nodded slowly at the ground.

"There is one more thing," Nesrin said. "There is another machine like ours. The same year, the same maker."

Osman looked up. "Where?"

"In Bekir's yard."

Osman laughed. It was not a happy laugh. He got off the wall and started to walk home.

"Then the gardens are finished," he said.

# The Man with the Old Machine

Bekir's house was the last one on the river road.

His yard was full of metal. Old car doors, a bed, wheels, a boat with a hole in it, and things that were not anything anymore. In the middle of it all, under a piece of plastic, sat a water machine from 1974.

Nesrin stood at the gate for a minute. Then she went in.

Bekir was sitting on a chair by his door in the shade. He was eighty years old and he did not look up.

"Go away," he said.

"I am not selling anything."

"Everybody is selling something."

Nesrin took the two halves of broken metal out of her pocket and held them up. "The village machine is dead," she said. "This piece killed it. You have the same machine in your yard."

Bekir looked at the metal for a long moment. Then he looked at his plastic sheet.

"That machine is mine," he said.

"Yes."

"Your father took my land."

Nesrin put her hands down. "My father is dead, Bekir. Eleven years."

"He took the field by the water. He took it with a paper and a man from town." Bekir's voice did not go up or down. "I was thirty-one. I remember every day of that year."

"I know the story."

"You know a story. I know a year."

Nesrin sat down on an old wheel in the sun. She said nothing for a while. A bird moved in the tree above them.

"The gardens have two days," she said at last.

"The gardens are not my gardens."

"No. They are Osman's, and Meryem's, and the teacher's, and the family with the six children by the bridge." She looked at him. "And they are your gardens too. Your beans are in the ground like everybody's beans."

Bekir moved his feet.

"I carry water from the river," he said.

"You are eighty."

"I carry water from the river."

Nesrin stood up. She walked over to the plastic sheet and pulled a corner of it back. The old machine underneath was red with rust and full of dust, but the shape was right. It was the same shape she took apart that morning.

"Do not touch it," Bekir said.

She let the plastic fall.

"I am going to say one true thing and then I am going to go home," she said. "I do not want your machine. I want one piece from inside it, the size of an egg. Your machine is never going to work again. Mine can work tonight."

"Go home then."

She went home.

She did not sleep well. At two in the morning she went down to the workshop and tried to make the piece herself. She had metal, and a fire, and tools, and twenty years of hands. She worked until the sun came up.

At seven she looked at what she made. It was close. It was not right. She put it on the table and sat with her head in her hands.

Osman came at eight with bread and cheese and did not ask any questions.

"Some of the small plants went yellow in the night," he said finally.

"I know."

"People are talking about carrying water in buckets from the river. Every family, all day."

Nesrin looked up. "Fifty gardens. In this heat. With buckets."

"I know," Osman said. "But people must do something. Nobody can sit and watch it happen."

Nesrin looked at the bad piece of metal on her table. Then she stood up and got her tools and put them in her bag.

"Where are you going?" Osman asked.

"To carry water," she said.

# One Small Piece of Metal

They started at nine in the morning.

Thirty people, and then forty, went down to the river with buckets and pots and old oil cans. The river was low and brown. They made a long line up the hill, and they passed the water hand to hand, and it went into the gardens cup by cup.

It was hard, stupid, slow work. A bucket of water in a garden is nothing. But the line kept moving.

Nesrin worked in the middle of the line, between the teacher and a girl of about twelve. Her back began to hurt at eleven and by one o'clock she stopped feeling her hands.

At two the girl next to her said, "Look."

An old man was coming down the road with a wheel from a bicycle in one hand. He walked slowly and he stopped twice.

Everybody in the line stopped moving. Water went on the ground.

Bekir came up to Nesrin and stood in front of her.

"I cannot carry a bucket," he said. "I tried this morning in my own garden. I got four metres."

Nesrin waited.

"So I am going to give you the piece," Bekir said. "Not the machine. The piece."

"Thank you."

"I do not want your thanks and I am not your friend." He turned and started to walk back up the road. "Bring your tools. And bring somebody strong, because my machine is heavy and my arms are finished."

They took the old machine apart in Bekir's yard in the afternoon sun. It was full of rust and dirt and a family of small animals in the bottom. Nesrin worked with her light between her teeth. Two men held the metal for her.

The piece came out at half past four.

It was dirty and brown, but it was whole, and the number on the side was the same number: 1974. Nesrin cleaned it with oil and a cloth for ten minutes. Then she held it up in the light and turned it, and she looked for lines and holes in the metal, and she found none.

"Well?" said Bekir, from his chair.

"It is good," Nesrin said.

"Of course it is good. It is my machine."

They put it in at six. Nesrin worked alone inside the little stone house because too many people wanted to help. She put the pieces back in the same order. Everything went in. One small thing did not want to go in, and then it did.

She closed the side. Half the village stood on the road outside.

"Osman," she called. "Turn it on."

The machine made a noise like an animal waking up. Then it made a much worse noise, and Nesrin's heart went cold, and then the noise became a low, flat, boring sound.

It was the most beautiful sound in the world.

The water came out of the pipe at the bottom of the hill three minutes later. It was brown for a moment and then it went clear. People shouted. Somebody's small son ran into it with all his clothes on.

Nesrin sat down on the step of the stone house and put her black hands on her knees. Osman gave her a glass of tea from somewhere.

"Ten days," she said. "The shop in town said ten days."

"You did it in two."

"Bekir did it in two."

Osman looked up the road. The old man was standing far away, by the tree at the corner, alone. He watched the water for a while. Then he turned and walked home.

Nesrin put her tea down and got up and went after him.

She caught him at his gate. She was carrying the empty red body of his old machine, and it was heavy, and she was not going to say that.

"You forgot this," she said.

"It is dead metal."

"It is dead metal in your yard, and that is where it lives." She put it down inside the gate, in its old place. Then she picked up the plastic sheet and put it over the top, and she pushed stones on the corners, the way he did.

Bekir watched her.

"The field by the water," he said. "It is nothing now. Stones and thorns."

"I know."

"But it was my father's field."

"I know," Nesrin said.

She picked up her bag of tools. At the gate she stopped.

"There is a tap by the school," she said. "The water is coming again. If your arms are tired, send a child with a bucket. Everybody is going to fill it for you."

Bekir did not answer. But in the evening the sun went down behind the hill. A boy of about ten walked slowly up the river road. He had a full bucket in each hand.
